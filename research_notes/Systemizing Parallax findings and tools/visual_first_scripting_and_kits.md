# Visual-first scripting and reusable production kits (research notes, compiled 2026-10-11)

Reading notes for the report writer: almost everything below is paraphrase, not quotation; check the linked source before quoting. Each finding is tagged **[evidence]** (controlled studies or meta-analysis), **[descriptive research]** (academic taxonomy built from coding examples, not tested), **[craft]** (practitioner convention or a practitioner's own account), **[opinion]** (one person's view) or **[vendor]** (a company describing its own product). "Assumes footage/crew" marks guidance that does not transfer directly to a solo, no-live-action, 11-minute narrated essay.

## 1. Writing for pictures: what practitioners teach about scripting so the pictures carry meaning

### Takeaway
Across broadcast news, documentary, explainer and animation practice the shared rule is that narration should add what the picture cannot show (cause, context, meaning, stakes) while still landing at the same moment as the matching picture; the dispute is only over how literal the match should be. The strongest explainer practitioners (Vox's Caswell and Fong, Kurzgesagt) decide what will be on screen before or while the sentence is written, and rewrite sentences so they point at the picture, whereas the common YouTube-essay failure named by critics is a finished text with pictures laid on afterwards.

### Cited Findings

**Broadcast news: "write to the video" and the "see dog, say dog" dispute**
- **[craft]** A TV-news trade tip sheet (Feb 9, 2015, no named author) calls the old "see dog, say dog" admonition wrongheaded: viewers can already see the picture, so the reporter should "Use words to add detail, context and meaning to what they're looking at." Its worked example: over traffic-jam pictures, do not say traffic is bad; say how much longer the drive downtown will take. — [b-roll.net, "More tips for writing TV news"](https://b-roll.net/today/2015/02/more-tips-for-writing-tv-news/)
- **[craft]** The same sheet gives four companion rules: do not write more words than you have pictures for; do not fight your pictures (if officials are shown smiling and shaking hands, viewers will believe they get along whatever the narration says, so the picture must prove the line); preserve surprises by letting picture and sound reveal an event rather than announcing it; and leave short pauses for the picture to run. — [b-roll.net](https://b-roll.net/today/2015/02/more-tips-for-writing-tv-news/)
- **[craft, assumes footage]** A journalism-training guide frames TV writing as "writing to tape": the script is built around footage already shot, and should be no longer than the pictures warrant. — [IJNet, "How to write broadcast news stories"](https://ijnet.org/en/resource/how-write-broadcast-news-stories)
- **[craft, assumes footage; unofficial source]** A widely circulated "Writing to pictures" training deck (uses a BBC News package as its example but is not confirmed as BBC Academy material) teaches: choose and cut the pictures first, then write the words; budget about 3 words per second (about 180 words for a 60-second film before interviews); and treat narration that restates the visible action as weak writing. — [SlideShare, "Writing to pictures: the basics"](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/writing-to-pictures-the-basics/2491250)
- **[craft]** A commentary-writing guide makes the same point by analogy to a presenter reading slides aloud: the audience needs the reason a number moved, not the number they can already read. — [British Guild of Travel Writers, "Commentary writing"](https://bgtw.org/commentary-writing)
- **[craft; secondary, wording unverified]** A trade-press post reprinting Al Tompkins (Poynter) praises a reporter for letting footage carry a scene and describes not competing with the images as the key to writing to video. I could not open the original Poynter column. — [Broadcasting & Cable](https://www.broadcastingcable.com/blog/poynter-uses-wtvf-story-teachable-moment-66705)

**Explainer practice: the visual is decided before the sentence is final**
- **[craft, practitioner's own account]** Estelle Caswell (Vox, Earworm; School of Motion podcast, page undated) says most of her effort goes in before the script is locked, that while writing she already knows exactly what she will show, and that she edits sentence by sentence so the wording makes viewers attend to what they are looking at. Her pitches must list the visual hooks and visual evidence up front; she does archival research during reporting and writes toward a specific source (a particular article, not a generic image); for abstract ideas she borrows a familiar visual framework (circle of fifths presented like a designer's colour wheel) and builds a small visual language she can teach the viewer. — [School of Motion, Estelle Caswell interview](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft]** Caswell hires animators on how well they communicate a script rather than how much they decorate it, and says transitions carry no storytelling meaning and are used, like holds, mainly for pacing. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft]** Joss Fong (Vox; interview published Jan 7, 2020, about an Apr 10, 2019 video) says voice-over must be simpler than print prose because viewers process sound and image together; that she ordered the piece so viewers grasp why the problem is hard before seeing the result; and that every Vox video goes through script editors whose confusion flags reveal gaps. — [The Open Notebook, "Videogram"](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[craft]** Fong (interview published Feb 15, 2022) credits tight correspondence between what is seen in an animation and what is heard with improving comprehension and retention, says this is harder when the topic is less literal and physical, warns against packing in so much information that nothing sticks, and aims for one moment per episode that stays in the mind. — [Video Consortium, "Episode 1: Joss Fong"](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-1-joss-fong)
- **[craft, studio's own account]** Kurzgesagt's process page (undated) calls the script the backbone, revised in around twelve rounds, and places visual planning in a sketching phase where the team settles the best visual metaphor and idea for each scene; the narrator's recording then sets timing for animators. — [Kurzgesagt, "Our video process"](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- **[craft, studio's own account]** In Kurzgesagt's 2020 making-of video (transcript copy), sketching starts only after the script is done and covers three things: visual metaphors, transitions between ideas, and which scenes deserve the most attention; writing is called the bottleneck. — [LingQ transcript of "How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours"](https://www.lingq.com/en/learn-english-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/); dated Feb 2020 via [kottke.org](https://also.kottke.org/20/02/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-just-1200-hours)
- **[craft]** TED-Ed collaborators describe a script-first, narration-first order: one educator cut the script roughly in half with an editor, recorded narration, was then paired with an animator who built to the voice track, and saw an animated storyboard before the film. — [Linguist Educator Exchange](https://linguisteducatorexchange.com/?p=90449). A two-person studio made the storyboard/animatic for a 5.5-minute TED-Ed lesson in about four days and the film in under three weeks, with the educator checking images for accuracy so that the pictures explained more than the words did. — [Buzzco](https://buzzco.nyc/?p=366)

**Video-essay practice and its critics**
- **[craft]** Tony Zhou and Taylor Ramos (Every Frame a Painting; reported Dec 4, 2017) reduced each premise to a simple statement or question and tested it on people before making the video, built the thesis by arguing with each other, and spent about 8 hours of editing per finished minute; the stated aim was explaining visual ideas to non-visual people. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2017/12/rip-every-frame-painting-farewell-advice-tony-zhou-and-taylor-ramos)
- **[opinion, academic]** Kiss and van den Berg (Film Studies in Motion, 2016) argue that too often the video is a mere illustration of a conventional text instead of making an argument of its own. — [Film Studies in Motion (Scalar)](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/film-studies-in-motion/what-makes-a-video-essay-great-by-kevin-b-lee)
- **[opinion]** Kevin B. Lee (Fandor video, Dec 2014) names a mode he calls hypernarration (fast delivery, fast montage, assertive opinion) and says it produces a cognitive-treadmill effect on viewers. — [Film Studies in Motion (Scalar)](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/film-studies-in-motion/what-makes-a-video-essay-great-by-kevin-b-lee)

**Evidence bearing on the craft rules**
- **[evidence]** Mayer and Fiorella (Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2014): presenting corresponding animation and narration at the same time beats presenting them successively (temporal contiguity; 9 of 9 tests, median effect size 1.22); graphics plus narration beats graphics plus narration plus the same words as on-screen text (redundancy; 16 of 16 tests, median 0.86); leaving out extraneous material helps (coherence; 23 of 23, median 0.86). — [Cambridge University Press chapter abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)

**A vocabulary for how words and pictures can relate**
- **[craft taxonomy]** Scott McCloud's seven word-picture combinations, as summarised in a 2014 Yale teaching unit: word-specific (picture only illustrates a complete text), picture-specific (words only add a soundtrack), duo-specific (both say the same thing), additive (one amplifies the other), parallel (they follow separate courses), montage (words are part of the picture), interdependent (together they convey what neither does alone; McCloud calls it perhaps the most common). — [Yale-New Haven Teachers Institute](https://teachersinstitute.yale.edu/curriculum/units/2014/1/14.01.01/6)

### Inferences
- The "see dog, say dog" quarrel dissolves once two things are separated: timing (the word and its picture should coincide; supported by temporal-contiguity evidence and by Fong's account) and content (the word should not merely name the picture; a craft rule, with the redundancy principle giving partial support for the narrower case of on-screen text duplicating narration). A usable rule for an essay: sync the noun, add the meaning.
- McCloud's categories give a compact audit vocabulary for a two-column script: "duo-specific" and "word-specific" rows are the ones the critics (Kiss and van den Berg) object to; "interdependent" and "additive" rows are what Caswell and the news writers describe.
- The news rule "pictures first, then words" exists because footage is a fixed given. With generated illustrations and coded graphics nothing is given, so the transferable part is Caswell's version: know the picture while writing the sentence, and rewrite the sentence to point at it.
- Word budgets from broadcast sources bracket the user's draft: 11 minutes is about 1,430 words at the 130 words-per-minute template default (see Q2) and about 1,980 at 3 words per second, so a 1,500-word script sits at the slow, picture-friendly end.
- Fong's remark that correspondence is harder for less literal, less physical topics applies directly to philosophy and AI subjects; her and Caswell's answer is a borrowed familiar framework plus a taught visual language rather than literal depiction.

### Gaps
- I could not open the narration chapters of Sheila Curran Bernard (Documentary Storytelling), Alan Rosenthal (Writing, Directing, and Producing Documentary Films) or Michael Rabiger (Directing the Documentary). Searches returned only publisher listings (Rabiger's 6th edition has a "Creating Narration" chapter and advertises expanded narration-writing coverage). No claim about their specific narration rules is made here.
- No authoritative origin for the "say dog, see dog" phrase was found; the only sourced use is the 2015 trade sheet above, in the order "see dog, say dog".
- No official BBC Academy or NPR Training page on writing to pictures was retrieved.
- Johnny Harris: an Adobe MAX 2025 session page describes his approach as visual-first ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/max/2025/sessions/scrappy-smart-and-human-the-future-of-storytelling-os558.html)) and a March 2025 "How I Write" podcast episode has chapters titled on writing visual scripts and being a video-first writer, but I could not access a transcript, so nothing specific about his script format is attributed to him.

## 2. Script formats that carry visual intent

### Takeaway
The two-column audio-visual script is the standard carrier for narrated non-fiction and it is deliberately coarse in the picture column until late; detail is added by separate documents downstream (storyboard or shot list, then animatic). Small explainer teams often skip the formal chain: Vox's Caswell said she had never heard of the mood-board to style-frame to board pipeline and animates while reporting.

- **[craft]** Thompson's reason for the two-column form: picture and sound sit side by side, the speaker is always identified, and every choice stays visible in the context it was taken from. He notes the form also serves narrative development and paper edits, not only finished scripts. — [Chicago Media Works](https://www.chicagomediaworks.com/2instructworks/3editing_doc/3docedit_scriptmpl/3editing_docspaperedit.html)
- **[craft]** When an interviewer laid out the conventional motion-design chain (mood boards, style frames, production boards, rough edit, animation), Caswell said she had never heard of that process; she animates most of her own work and animated one episode while still reporting it. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft]** TED-Ed publishes a making-of episode whose listing describes planning through storyboard and animatic before animation. — [TheTVDB listing, "Making a TED-Ed Lesson"](https://thetvdb.com/series/ted-ed/episodes/9427177)

### Inferences
- In every format the picture column's vocabulary is a small closed set of labels (source type, shot size, a duration), not prose description. That is the same design as mode tags plus a technique name per row; the published formats give precedent for keeping the picture cell to a tag, a subject and a duration at draft one, and adding camera and motion only after script lock.
- The documents form a ladder of increasing commitment: pitch (visual hooks) then AV script (tag per block) then storyboard or shot list (one frame per shot) then animatic (time). A solo creator with code-rendered graphics can collapse the last two, since a rendered preview against scratch narration is an animatic.
- Colour scripts and beat boards are the two formats aimed at the whole piece rather than the shot; both are cheap (thumbnail-sized, a few per minute) and neither needs a crew.
- Paper edits and B-roll planning assume recorded footage and do not transfer, except for Thompson's habit of striking through rather than deleting, which preserves why a choice was made.

### Gaps
- No published real example of a Vox, Kurzgesagt, TED-Ed, Wendover or Johnny Harris working script was retrieved, so the actual wording they put in a picture column is unknown.
- Zhou and Ramos published the script of their unfinished final episode with their Dec 2017 postmortem, but the Medium page returned an access error and the mirror was truncated.
- Bernard's book reportedly reproduces sample script pages (per bookseller listings seen in search), not opened.
- No source described how the picture column changes draft by draft in an explainer studio; the draft-stage claims above come from documentary and template sources.

## 3. Structure-to-visual mappings: published frameworks, and which are evidence versus convention

### Takeaway
Three academic taxonomies map narrative or rhetorical function to visual treatment (Segel and Heer 2010; Hullman and Diakopoulos 2011; Bach and co-authors 2018), and all three are descriptive codings of existing work, not tested prescriptions. The only body of controlled evidence is Mayer's multimedia-learning programme, which supports a handful of general constraints (cut extraneous material, cue the structure, sync word and picture, keep labels next to the thing, do not duplicate narration as on-screen text) but says nothing about which treatment fits which sentence function; film-grammar rules are craft convention with a few perceptual studies behind continuity editing.

### Cited Findings

**Segel and Heer, "Narrative Visualization: Telling Stories with Data" (IEEE InfoVis 2010) [descriptive research]**
- Method: a design-space analysis of 58 examples from online journalism, graphic design, comics, business, art and visualization research, organised by affinity diagramming; the authors say the sample is not exhaustive, that the taxonomy involves unavoidable subjectivity, and that they are not experts in narrative. — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- Seven genres: magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, film/video/animation; genres differ mainly in number of frames and how strictly they are ordered, and can be combined. The authors state there is no a priori right genre for a given story. — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- Visual narrative tactics, three groups. Visual structuring (tells the viewer where they are in the whole): establishing shot or splash screen, consistent visual platform, progress bar or timebar, checklist progress tracker. Highlighting (directs attention): close-ups, feature distinction, character direction, motion, audio, zooming. Transition guidance (moves between scenes without disorienting): familiar objects, viewing angle, camera motion, continuity editing, object continuity, animated transitions. — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- Narrative structure tactics: ordering (linear, user-directed, random access); interactivity; messaging (captions and headlines, annotations, accompanying article, multi-messaging, comment repetition, introductory text, summary or synthesis). — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- Observation relevant to linear video: author-driven pieces (linear order, heavy messaging, no interactivity) suit storytelling and efficient communication; across the corpus, messaging devices such as repeating key points and closing summaries were under-used, and were more common in slide shows and videos, which the authors link to those genres feeling more like stories. — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)

**Hullman and Diakopoulos, "Visualization Rhetoric" (IEEE InfoVis 2011) [descriptive research]**
- Four editorial layers where a designer can add or omit to steer interpretation: data, visual representation, textual annotation, interactivity. — [project page](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/project/vis-rhetoric)
- Five classes of technique with members: information access (omission, thresholding, metonymy by selecting, averaging or aggregating); provenance (citing sources, stating method, representing uncertainty, identifying the author); mapping (obscuring, visual metaphor and metonymy such as left for past and right for future or up for more and down for less, contrast, classification by size, position or colour, redundancy); linguistic (typographic emphasis, irony and rhetorical questions, similarity devices including analogy, metaphor, parallelism and simile, and individualisation by addressing the viewer); procedural (anchoring through default views, fixed comparisons, spatial ordering, and partial animation that pauses on a view for a climactic effect). — [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisRhetoric-InfoVis.pdf)
- The ten most frequent techniques in their sample: grouping by colour, aggregating values, suggestive spatial mappings, goal suggestions, bold type, source citations, metaphoric statements, colour mappings, direct address, variable splices. Techniques usually appeared in combination, and the authors suggest spreading emphasis over several less obvious strategies to avoid the appearance of bias that heavy use of one strategy creates. — [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisRhetoric-InfoVis.pdf)

**Bach, Stefaner, Boy, Drucker, Bartram, Wood, Ciuccarelli, Engelhardt, Koeppen, Tversky, "Narrative Design Patterns for Data-Driven Storytelling" (book chapter, 2018) [descriptive research]**
- Eighteen patterns, each tied to a purpose, drawn from a 2016 Dagstuhl workshop and also issued as a card deck. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/narrative-design-patterns-for-data-driven-storytelling/); [NAPA cards](http://napa-cards.net)
- The eighteen, with the purpose categories assigned in a later study's summary table (Arg = argumentation, Flw = flow, Frm = framing, Emo = emotion, Eng = engagement):

| Pattern | Categories | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Compare | Arg | Juxtapose several views and highlight the difference |
| Concretise | Arg, Emo | Turn an abstract concept or number into solid, known references |
| Repetition | Arg, Flw | Reuse the same kind of view to show an effect across dimensions |
| Rhetorical question | Arg, Frm, Emo, Eng | Present the argument as a question |
| Exploration | Flw, Eng | Let the audience interact freely |
| Gradual reveal | Flw, Emo, Eng | Unfold hierarchically, by granularity or subset |
| Speed-up / slow-down | Flw, Emo, Eng | Use animation speed to show change in intensity and urgency |
| Convention breaking | Frm | Break an established graphic convention to convey surprise |
| Defamiliarisation | Frm | Show a familiar object in an unexpected way to prompt rethinking |
| Physical metaphor | Frm | Use direction and space to carry meaning |
| Familiarisation | Frm, Emo | Make it personal using what the audience knows |
| Make-a-guess | Frm, Eng | Ask the audience to commit to a conclusion first |
| Addressing the audience | Frm, Emo, Eng | Make the audience part of the narrative |
| Breaking the fourth wall | Frm, Emo | A subject in the story addresses the audience unexpectedly |
| Humans-behind-the-dots | Emo | Connect data to the people it describes |
| Silent data | Emo | Emphasise by de-emphasising or hiding some data |
| Users-find-themselves | Emo, Eng | Let the audience reach the conclusion themselves |
| Call for action | Eng | Ask the audience to act |

  — [Blount, Koesten, Zhao and Simperl, CHIRA 2020, Table 1](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/101216/101216.pdf)
- **[small empirical study of use, not of effect]** In 67 data stories (43 by postgraduate students, 24 award winners from 2014 to 2019), the median story used three of the eighteen patterns; students mostly chose compare and exploration; reported and actual use often diverged; award winners shared the students' preferences but also used the harder patterns. — [Blount et al. 2020](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/101216/101216.pdf)
- **[evidence, single study]** A 2022 IEEE VIS paper derived six patterns for breaking the fourth wall from 58 data stories and reported in a user study that the device improved self-story connection, engagement and recall. — [IEEE VIS 2022](https://content.ieeevis.org/year/2022/paper_v-full-1198.html)
- **[craft guidance]** The EU data-visualisation guide recommends building a chart up in steps rather than showing it whole, to create tension and curiosity, and treats question-then-answer as a relative of small multiples (repetition used to split a multi-series chart into single-series pieces). — [data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/narrative-story-patterns)

**Mayer's multimedia learning principles [evidence]**

| Principle | Statement | Tests supporting | Median effect size | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | Leave extraneous material out | 23 of 23 | 0.86 | [Mayer and Fiorella 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E) |
| Signaling | Add cues that show how the essential material is organised | 24 of 28 | 0.41 | same |
| Redundancy | Graphics plus narration beats graphics plus narration plus duplicate on-screen text | 16 of 16 | 0.86 | same |
| Spatial contiguity | Put words next to the part of the picture they describe | 22 of 22 | 1.10 | same |
| Temporal contiguity | Present matching narration and animation together, not in sequence | 9 of 9 | 1.22 | same |

- A 2020 meta-analysis of signaling alone found a smaller average effect (d = 0.38; 44 effects from 29 studies, 2,726 participants), larger in higher-quality studies. — [Washington State University repository](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)
- Cromley and Chen (Educational Research Review, 2025) pooled Mayer's own corpus from 1990 to 2022 (92 articles, 181 studies, 591 effects): overall g = 0.37; every moderator mattered, including a small decline in effect per publication year; the largest reliable effects were for removing seductive details, modality (spoken rather than written words with pictures), personalisation (conversational wording), the multimedia principle itself, sentence-level coherence and self-explanation; cueing was medium; text plus diagrams was large and consistent across outcome types; animation, games and simulations were inconsistent. — [NSF public access record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- A commentary on that paper (Sept 16, 2025) reports that benefits were largest for university and medical students and for STEM and medical content, smaller for liberal-arts content, and larger for transfer and inference than for factual recall. — [Carl Hendrick, The Learning Dispatch](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually)
- Segmenting (breaking a continuous lesson into learner-paced parts) is reported in secondary summaries at roughly 0.67 to 0.70 from a small number of tests; figures vary by edition and I did not confirm them against a primary table. Note the principle as tested concerns learner-controlled pacing, which a linear video offers only through the pause button. — [EdTech Books, "The Future of Multimedia Learning"](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)
- The multimedia principle weakens for viewers with high prior knowledge (expertise reversal), and depends on the instructional quality of the graphic. — [university lecture slides on multimedia learning, file dated Nov 2018](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)

**Film grammar and editing [craft, with some perceptual evidence]**
- **[craft]** Walter Murch's rule of six ranks what a cut should satisfy: emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, two-dimensional screen plane 5%, three-dimensional space 4%; sacrifice from the bottom up. The percentages are relative weights from an editor's judgement, not measurements. — [University of Texas course wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)
- **[evidence]** Smith and Henderson (2008) showed that cuts made according to continuity-editing rules go unnoticed more often than cuts that break them; about a quarter of same-scene viewpoint cuts were missed, rising to about a third when the cut coincided with a sudden onset of motion. — [Journal of Eye Movement Research](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/2264). Smith's attentional theory of cinematic continuity (2012) explains match-on-action, shot/reverse-shot, the 180-degree rule and point-of-view editing by where attention is at the cut. — [UAL Research Online](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21187/)
- **[evidence, mixed]** The Kuleshov effect (meaning produced by juxtaposition) has a contested record: earlier replications were conflicting or unreliable; a 2016 eye-tracking study (36 participants) found some form of the effect; a 2024 study using director-shot film sequences also found that the adjoining scene changes how a neutral face is read. — [Barratt et al., Perception 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27056181/); [2024 study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11299807/)
- **[craft]** Bruce Block's The Visual Story organises visual structure around one principle, contrast and affinity, applied to seven components (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm), and ties visual structure to story structure. — [O'Reilly, chapter 2](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9781317745693/xhtml/Ch02.xhtml); [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story/Block/p/book/9781138014152)

**Explanation design [craft/opinion]**
- Bret Victor, "Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction" (Oct 2011): understanding comes from moving between a single concrete case, the same case abstracted over time, and abstraction over a parameter; the insight lies in the transitions, and every abstraction should offer a way back down to a concrete instance. — [worrydream.com](http://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)
- McCloud's word-picture combinations (see Q1). — [Yale-New Haven Teachers Institute](https://teachersinstitute.yale.edu/curriculum/units/2014/1/14.01.01/6)

**Evidence versus convention, summary**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Cut decorative or tangential material; sync narration with its picture; place labels beside what they label; do not duplicate narration as full on-screen text | Evidence (Mayer; strongest and most consistent) |
| Cue structure (highlight, arrows, outlines, headings) | Evidence, small to medium and variable |
| Conversational narration; static diagram plus words works reliably | Evidence (2025 meta-analysis) |
| Animation is better than a still diagram | Not established; inconsistent results |
| Function-to-treatment catalogues (genres, tactics, 18 patterns, rhetoric classes) | Descriptive; they record what skilled makers do |
| Meaning of a cut, close-up or montage; rule of six; contrast and affinity | Craft convention; continuity editing and juxtaposition have some perceptual support |
| Ladder of abstraction; word-picture combinations | Individual authors' frameworks, widely adopted, untested |

### Inferences
- The three taxonomies are the nearest published precedents for a catalogue that maps what a sentence is doing to a picture technique. Their own authors frame them as vocabularies for choosing and discussing, not as rules; that is the defensible status for any 70-technique catalogue too.
- Several patterns line up with sentence functions directly: compare (contrast or analogy sentences), concretise (counts and magnitudes), gradual reveal (enumerations and build-ups), repetition (the same frame reused across cases), silent data (taking one thing away or dimming everything but one), physical metaphor and suggestive spatial mapping (before/after, more/less), speed-up/slow-down (urgency), make-a-guess and rhetorical question (set-ups before a reveal), humans-behind-the-dots (abstract claim brought to a person).
- The finding that even award-winning stories use a median of three patterns suggests that a piece is carried by a few recurring moves, with the rest of a catalogue used occasionally.
- Mayer's evidence gives a small set of veto rules that apply to every technique, which is a cleaner use than trying to rank techniques by it. Two cautions for this channel: the benefits were measured mostly on STEM and procedural material with learning outcomes and were smaller for liberal-arts content, and the corpus tests learning, not retention on a platform or persuasion.
- The inconsistent evidence for animation plus the reliable evidence for diagram-with-words supports using motion where the change is the content (a build, a removal, a transfer) and a held still elsewhere.
- Segel and Heer's "visual structuring" group (establishing image, consistent platform, progress markers) and their note that repetition of key points and closing synthesis are under-used are cheap additions to a linear essay.

### Gaps
- Arijon (Grammar of the Film Language), Katz (Film Directing Shot by Shot), Bordwell and Thompson (Film Art) and Tufte (small multiples, sparklines) were not retrieved in this session; nothing specific is attributed to them. Segel and Heer cite Bordwell and Thompson and McCloud as sources for their film- and comics-derived tactics.
- I found no study that tests a function-to-treatment mapping directly (for example, whether a list sentence is better served by a staggered build than by a static list) in narrated essay video.
- McCloud's Making Comics (2006) may name the fourth category differently from the Yale summary's "additive"; I could not check the book.
- Block's story-intensity and visual-intensity graphs are widely described as the book's planning tool, but the pages I could reach did not show them, so they are not cited as fact.
- The full text of Bach's chapter was not opened; pattern definitions come from the 2020 study's summary table.

## 4. Visual pacing and variety as a design input

### Takeaway
The named whole-piece planning tools come from animation (the colour script) and from film visual theory (contrast and affinity), and both rest on the same idea: variety is rationed so that change marks the moments that matter. Explainer practitioners describe pacing in practice through sound and reveals (music changes, holds, delayed reveals) rather than through a formal chart, and I found no essayist who publishes a visual beat map.

### Cited Findings
- **[craft]** The colour script lays out the film's emotional arc as a strip of small colour studies, scene by scene, made early and consulted throughout production. — [MoMA audio guide](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/192/2581); [UFPE thesis on colour scripts](https://repositorio.ufpe.br/jspui/bitstream/123456789/48227/4/TCC_Beatriz%20Gondim%20Luna.pdf)
- **[craft]** Block's contrast-and-affinity principle: difference between successive images raises intensity and similarity lowers it, across space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement and rhythm. — [O'Reilly, The Visual Story ch. 2](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-visual-story/9781317745693/xhtml/Ch02.xhtml)
- **[craft, studio's own account]** Kurzgesagt's sketch stage explicitly decides which scenes get the most attention, alongside metaphors and transitions. — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/en/learn-english-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/)
- **[craft]** Joss Fong (2020) on one Vox video: music changed about every 20 seconds; the starts and ends of music cues were placed to mark main points and tell viewers when to pay attention; the opening was kept fast; the key image was withheld to create a reflective beat; music selection and editing can take a full day. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[craft]** Fong (2022) contrasts fact-dense explainers with a format that leaves more room for pacing and breathers, and plans for one memorable moment per episode. — [Video Consortium](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-1-joss-fong)
- **[craft]** Caswell: transitions and holds are pacing tools, not meaning; the interviewer's description of images held five to six seconds in her work went unchallenged. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft; from a search summary, page not opened]** The studio Brume's case study of a TED-Ed film describes setting the story's rhythm as alternating calm and intense passages and drawing a graphic to share those animation intentions with client and team, after building a visual research library. — [Behance](https://behance.net/gallery/145033083/The-myth-of-the-original-star-crossed-lovers-TED-ED)
- **[descriptive research]** Speed-up/slow-down and repetition are named flow patterns in Bach's set; consistent visual platform and progress markers are structuring tactics in Segel and Heer. — [Blount et al. 2020, Table 1](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/101216/101216.pdf); [Segel and Heer 2010](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)
- **[craft]** Murch gives rhythm 10% of a cut's weight, below emotion and story. — [UT course wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)
- **[opinion]** A third-party retention review of a YouTube video judged the variety of visuals good but criticised a fade to black used at almost every scene change, sometimes mid-sentence, as breaking momentum, and pointed to Kurzgesagt as the model for smooth visual flow. — [George Blackman, Retention Review 7](https://georgeblackman.substack.com/p/retention-review-7-he-fixed-retention)
- **[craft]** Runtime arithmetic tools exist at the script stage: a per-scene word count and running time at 130 words per minute. — [Boords](https://boords.com/video-script-template/documentary-template)

### Inferences
- A colour script translated to an essay is a one-row strip of thumbnails or coloured cells, one per beat, showing mode (illustration, graphic, type card, archival), dominant palette or light/dark, and intensity. It can be drawn from the two-column script before any asset exists, and it shows at a glance runs of the same treatment and where the peak falls.
- Contrast and affinity gives a rule for repetition that is not "never repeat": repeat a treatment to hold a section together, and change treatment at the boundary where the argument turns. The retention review's objection to a transition repeated at every seam is the same principle from the audience side.
- Practitioners pace with sound as much as with pictures (Fong's music cues); an audio cue sheet and a visual beat strip are two views of one pacing plan.
- B-roll planning as taught for documentary assumes footage and does not transfer; its equivalent here is deciding per beat which register carries it.

### Gaps
- No essayist or explainer studio was found publishing a visual beat map, energy chart or written rule about how often a treatment may recur. The search for such a document returned only general retention advice.
- The Brume account could not be opened (access error), so its details rest on a search summary.
- Motif planning (a recurring image that returns transformed) was not found as a documented method outside feature-animation colour and design practice.
- Pixar or Sony Pictures Animation primary writing on beat boards was not retrieved; Eggleston's own interview page returned an access error.

## 5. Reusable kits in small productions

### Takeaway
Practitioners who publish their process standardise the container (pitch format, editorial review, palette and type, parameterised templates, file organisation, tools) and deliberately leave the picture for each idea loose: Kurzgesagt keeps an illustration library but redraws most assets, Caswell wants repeatable formats but rejects plug-and-play, and Fong says attempts to standardise episodes failed. Code-based kits built by solo creators for their own channels (Manim, Motion Canvas) and Remotion's parameterised compositions are the closest precedents for a data-driven template library.

### Cited Findings

**What named practitioners say they standardise and what they leave loose**
- **[craft]** Kurzgesagt has an illustration library and a few time-saving animation plugins, but still draws most illustrations from scratch to keep each video's look distinct, and animates every moving element by hand; about 200 panels per video, two to three illustrators for 8 to 12 weeks, two to three animators for 8 to 10 weeks, at least 1,200 hours in total. — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/en/learn-english-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/)
- **[craft]** Caswell standardises the pitch format (headline, visual hooks, evidence, interviewees), story-editor review, and an animation hiring test based on communicating a script; she builds a visual language per series; she wants repeatable formats but says a plug-and-play model is not something she wants, and that uniqueness does not need polish. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft]** Fong: episodes are built case by case and standardising them failed because each story has its own needs; script editors review every Vox video. — [Video Consortium](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-1-joss-fong); [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[craft]** Zhou and Ramos edited every essay in Final Cut Pro X and relied on its keyword tagging to organise clips; they describe organisation as the way connections appear that would not otherwise; one project held more than 200 films of which 85 were used; they keep a dated notebook of ideas. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2017/12/rip-every-frame-painting-farewell-advice-tony-zhou-and-taylor-ramos)
- **[craft]** Vox's toolset as of 2020: Premiere Pro for edit and mix, After Effects for motion graphics, Photoshop and Illustrator for graphics, a licensed music library. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)

**Template mechanisms**
- **[vendor documentation]** After Effects Motion Graphics templates expose only chosen properties (text, colour, size, other values) so an editor can change them in Premiere without opening the project. — [Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/after-effects/web/create-motion-graphics?ntd=1)
- **[vendor documentation]** Remotion's parameterised rendering: default props define the shape of a composition's data; input props supplied at render time are merged over them; a metadata function can compute duration, size and frame rate from the props and fetch data first. — [Remotion docs](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/parameterized-rendering). Since version 4.0 props can be declared with a schema, which makes defaults type-safe and editable through a visual interface in the Studio, including nested JSON, with changes saved back to the project. — [Remotion 4.0 release post](https://remotion.dev/blog/4-0)
- **[craft, creator-built tools]** Manim began as Grant Sanderson's side project for 3Blue1Brown; his personal fork and the community edition are now separate. — [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/); demo dated Oct 12, 2024: [3Blue1Brown, "How I animate"](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/manim-demo/). Motion Canvas was written by the YouTuber aarthificial to aid his own video creation; it is a TypeScript library plus live-preview editor intended for informative vector animation synchronised to voice-over, and explicitly not a replacement for an editing program. — [Motion Canvas docs](https://motioncanvas.io/docs); [creator's sponsor page](https://sponsors.ecosyste.ms/accounts/aarthificial)
- **[craft guidance, generic; low-authority pages surfaced in search, and which page said which point was not verified]** Advice for creator template libraries: start from the elements that recur in most videos (intro, lower thirds, call to action, transitions, info boxes) and build variants because one template rarely fits every case — [StudyRaid, "Creating Reusable Motion Graphics Templates"](https://app.studyraid.com/en/read/54763/2628483/creating-reusable-motion-graphics-templates); file each downloaded asset against the project where it is used and keep its licence certificate with the edit files — [Joseph Nilo, "Envato Elements for YouTubers"](https://josephnilo.com/blog/envato-elements-for-youtubers/)

**Style guides and design systems as craft training**
- **[craft]** Design for Motion (Austin Shaw) defines style frames and design boards and their professional use; School of Motion's design course states its aim as teaching design systems rather than only polished frames, moving from brand research to mood boards to an art-directed visual language. — [Motionographer, Dec 2015](https://motionographer.com/2015/12/16/author-of-design-for-motion-talks-industry-education-and-the-state-of-design/); [School of Motion Design Bootcamp](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/products/design-bootcamp/) **[vendor]**

**Provenance and disclosure for generated assets**
- **[platform policy, 2024 reporting]** YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content at upload; clearly unrealistic content such as animation, and AI used for productivity (scripts, ideas, captions), is exempt. Check the current Help Center before relying on this. — [NBC News, March 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/youtube-says-will-require-creators-label-ai-content-rcna143937)
- **[vendor announcement]** OpenAI stated in May 2026 that images from ChatGPT and its API carry C2PA Content Credentials metadata and Google's SynthID watermark; C2PA metadata can be stripped by screenshots or re-encoding, which is why the pixel watermark is added. — [OpenAI, "Advancing content provenance"](https://openai.com/index/advancing-content-provenance)
- **[vendor]** AI-film guides recommend a continuity log of approved versions and attaching the same locked character and style references to every generation. — [Dreamina/CapCut guide](https://dreamina.capcut.com/ai-video/best-ai-animation-tools-for-creators)

### Inferences
- The consistent split across Kurzgesagt, Caswell and Fong is: fix the brand and the process, vary the picture. A template library is on the "fix" side when it encodes form (how a comparison or a build behaves) and leaves the content and the metaphor open; it tips into sameness when the metaphor itself is reused.
- Kurzgesagt's choice to redraw despite owning a library is a deliberate cost paid for distinctness by a team with thousands of hours per video. A solo maker cannot pay that everywhere, which argues for choosing a small number of hero beats per episode that get bespoke pictures and letting templates carry the rest.
- Remotion's schema-plus-default-props model, Manim and Motion Canvas all show the same pattern for a code kit: the reusable unit is a parameterised scene with a typed data contract and a visual preview, driven by a voice track for timing.
- For provenance, a sidecar record per generated asset (prompt, model, date, references, cost) plus keeping original files (which now carry embedded credentials from some vendors) covers both the platform's disclosure question and later regeneration.

### Gaps
- I found no solo essayist who publishes their template library, naming and folder conventions, or a pre-publish QA checklist. Searches returned marketplace listings and generic advice. Wendover, Polymatter, Lemmino, Nerdwriter, Patrick Willems and Polygon process material was not found.
- No public showcase of a YouTube essay channel built on Remotion templates was found; Remotion evidence here is documentation only.
- Whether Sanderson scripts before animating was not confirmed from his FAQ.
- Swipe files of techniques and prompt libraries: no named creator's practice was verified; only vendor guides describe prompt and reference reuse.
- The YouTube disclosure details date from 2024 coverage and were not re-checked against the current policy.

## 6. Planning with AI-generated imagery (2024 to 2026 workflows)

### Takeaway
Published AI-production workflows converge on lock the story, then lock the references (character and location sheets, style), then write a shot list, then generate frames, then video, with the same references attached to every generation; but nearly all detailed accounts are vendor marketing, and the only discard figures found (roughly one clip kept in four, and about a third of each kept clip used) come from an unnamed production reported by a tool vendor.

### Cited Findings
- **[vendor]** Order of work: settle the story, convert it into shot-level decisions, separate fixed character rules from per-shot variables, generate, check continuity, repair only what failed, assemble; one guide warns that generating before the story is clear only produces more inconsistent material. — [Dreamina/CapCut](https://dreamina.capcut.com/ai-video/best-ai-animation-tools-for-creators); [Higgsfield](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/script-to-ai-storyboard-shot-list)
- **[vendor]** Reference sheets: a character sheet fixes hair, clothing, build, key expressions and distinguishing features; a location sheet is anchored by at least one image that sets palette, scale and key architecture; a separate sheet is made for each costume or appearance change. — [invideo FAQ on reference sheets](https://invideo.io/faq/how-do-you-generate-all-character-reference-sheets-and/)
- **[vendor]** Sheet-in-one-image method: generate the character as a single multi-panel grid (a four-angle turnaround plus face and mid-angle close-ups), iterate on whole grids, then extract the best panels as anchors; the stated rationale is that panels made in one pass share one generation context and so match better than images made one at a time; generate about four options per asset and lock one; remove hand-held props before making turnarounds because they reproduce inconsistently. — [invideo FAQ on grid panels](https://invideo.io/faq/how-do-you-use-ai-character-sheet-grid-panels-to-keep/)
- **[vendor]** Boards versus shot list: for a short, lock characters and world and work from a shot list rather than full panels; board the key beats on longer or multi-character work; board fully for clients. Frames are approved before any video is generated. — [invideo FAQ on storyboarding](https://invideo.io/faq/do-i-need-to-storyboard-every-shot-before-generating-ai/); [invideo FAQ on storyboard-first workflow](https://invideo.io/faq/what-is-a-storyboard-first-ai-filmmaking-workflow-and/)
- **[vendor]** One storyboard tool offers an automatic mode (one prompt and a frame count, the model distributes the arc across panels) and a manual mode (each frame directed separately). — [Higgsfield](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/script-to-ai-storyboard-shot-list)
- **[vendor, unverifiable]** Discard figures: for a 3-minute animated episode made by two people in two days, 164 video generations yielded 41 clips in the cut (about 25%), about 5 seconds were used from each 15-second clip, and 17 of the 41 final shots were assembled from two or more generations of the same prompt; reported all-in cost about $950. The production is not named and no independent source is given (page updated July 27, 2026). — [invideo FAQ](https://invideo.io/faq/how-many-ai-video-generations-does-it-take-to-produce-a/)
- **[vendor, unverifiable]** The same vendor reports a roughly 90-second horror short needing about 400 video generations plus 30 image generations, and about five generations to lock each character's identity. — [invideo FAQ on budgeting](https://invideo.io/faq/how-many-ai-video-generations-do-you-need-to-budget-for/)
- **[vendor]** General-purpose image tools lose context between separate prompts, which is the stated reason for sheets and references. — [FinalBit](https://finalbitai.com/blog/how-to-create-a-storyboard-without-an-artist)
- **[creator tutorials, opinion]** 2025 to 2026 tutorials pair an image model for sheets and scene grids with a video model for animation, and differ on which image model they prefer; none is a controlled comparison. — [Video Highlight summary of a Veo 3.1 consistency tutorial](https://videohighlight.com/v/o9QDE-q16i8)

### Inferences
- The workflows above are built for moving, character-led fiction. For a narrated essay using stills with camera moves plus coded graphics, the transferable parts are the order (script lock before bulk generation), the locked style and reference sheets, the approve-frames-before-motion gate, and the continuity log; multi-shot video consistency matters much less.
- What to generate before script lock: reference material only (style block, recurring figure or setting sheets, a few look tests). Everything shot-specific waits for the shot list, since every source ties waste to generating before the story is fixed.
- A planning allowance of three to four generations per kept image or clip is the only figure available and should be treated as a single vendor data point, not a norm.
- Shot lists in these guides carry four fields (size, angle, movement, purpose); "purpose" is the field that connects back to what the sentence is doing.

### Gaps
- No independent filmmaker's or essayist's post-mortem with generation counts, discard rates or costs was found; all numbers are vendor-reported.
- No published workflow was found for a mixed shot list of still, moving and graphic shots in a narrated essay specifically.
- Nothing was found on "previsualisation with AI" as practised by a named documentary or essay maker (as opposed to tool marketing).
- Model names and capabilities in these guides change quickly and were not verified.

## 7. Feedback loops: how a technique earns or loses its place

### Takeaway
I found almost nothing published on tying retention to specific in-video visual treatments, and no creator who documents how often they retire a technique. What exists is platform tooling for testing packaging (thumbnails and titles), Vox producers' general statements that retention and engagement are their feedback, pre-production testing of the premise on people, and third-party retention critiques.

### Cited Findings
- **[craft]** Fong (2022): regular channel videos give clear feedback through views, retention and engagement; a YouTube-funded series was harder to read because promotion was outside the team's control; the team tracked audience gender balance and found a particular thumbnail subject improved it; she frames continual experiment as a guard against the channel's identity going stale. — [Video Consortium](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-1-joss-fong)
- **[platform]** YouTube's Test and Compare: announced June 2023, widened June 2024; up to three thumbnails shown evenly, the winner chosen by share of watch time, results in a few days to two weeks; title testing began as a limited experiment in July 2025 and later coverage reports wider availability. It tests packaging only. — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=187324); [YouTube blog, June 14, 2024](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-june-14-2024/); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-adds-title-testing-youtube-studio/753015/)
- **[platform]** YouTube's redesigned retention view surfaces four kinds of key moment: intro, continuous segments, spikes and dips. — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=151429)
- **[craft]** Zhou and Ramos tested each premise as a one-line statement on other people before production, and ended the series under their own rule that they would stop when they stopped enjoying it. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2017/12/rip-every-frame-painting-farewell-advice-tony-zhou-and-taylor-ramos)
- **[craft]** Fong's script editors act as a pre-publication comprehension test: where an editor is confused, the script has a gap. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)
- **[opinion]** A newsletter series reviews individual videos' retention alongside their visual choices from the outside. — [George Blackman, Retention Review](https://georgeblackman.substack.com/p/retention-review-7-he-fixed-retention)
- **[evidence, indirect]** In the 2025 meta-analysis of Mayer's corpus, effect sizes declined slightly with publication year; a commentator attributes this to novelty wearing off and contexts diversifying. — [NSF record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927); [Hendrick](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually)
- **[small empirical study]** Skilled data storytellers used a median of three narrative patterns per piece and novices often believed they had used a pattern when they had not. — [Blount et al. 2020](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2020/101216/101216.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because the platform cannot A/B test in-video treatments, the realistic loop for a small channel is observational: tag each segment in the manifest with its technique, then read retention dips and spikes against those tags after publishing. With one video at a time this yields hypotheses, not findings; several episodes are needed before a technique can be said to have earned or lost its place.
- The cheapest reliable test in the sources is before publishing, not after: a cold reader marking confusion (Fong) and a one-line premise test (Zhou and Ramos).
- The gap between claimed and actual pattern use in the 2020 study argues for auditing the rendered video against the catalogue rather than trusting the script's tags.
- The year-on-year decline in measured effects is weak, indirect support for expecting a technique's impact to fade with familiarity.

## 8. Risks of templating: formula and sameness, and how respected makers avoid it

### Takeaway
Critics locate sameness in the essay form in three places: pictures that only illustrate a finished text, a uniform fast-narration-over-montage delivery, and platform incentives that reward repeating a formula; commissioned explainer animation went through its own commoditisation a decade ago. The makers cited here keep a recognisable identity through a consistent platform (voice, palette, type, structure) while refusing to reuse the picture itself.

### Cited Findings
- **[opinion]** Noah Solomons (The Link, March 11, 2026) argues the video essay is reaching saturation and is probably becoming formulaic (sensational titles, filler footage, over-intellectual prose), but blames the attention economy that pushes creators toward formula and excess rather than the form. — [The Link](https://thelinknewspaper.ca/article/dont-blame-the-video-essay)
- **[opinion, academic]** Kiss and van den Berg (2016): the recurring weakness is video used as illustration for a traditional text. Kevin B. Lee (2014): the dominant popular mode is fast narration, fast montage and assertive opinion, with a treadmill effect on the viewer. — [Film Studies in Motion](https://scalar.usc.edu/works/film-studies-in-motion/what-makes-a-video-essay-great-by-kevin-b-lee)
- **[craft; from a search summary of the Dec 2017 postmortem, page returned an access error]** Zhou wrote that the channel's recognisable editing style was partly a product of working around YouTube's automated copyright matching, and that keeping a consistent format let them cover lesser-known subjects and still be watched. — [Tony Zhou, "Postmortem" (Medium)](https://medium.com/@tonyszhou/postmortem-1b338537fabc)
- **[craft]** Fong (2022): it would be a loss if new video journalists copied Vox or legacy TV formats; makers should notice when they are emulating something that exists, though emulation can be a starting point; the team keeps trying new things so its identity does not go stale. — [Video Consortium](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-1-joss-fong)
- **[craft]** Caswell: rejects a plug-and-play production model, values uniqueness over polish, and tests animators on communication rather than decoration. — [School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/estelle-caswell-vox-podcast)
- **[craft]** Kurzgesagt redraws most illustrations despite having a library, specifically to keep each video looking distinct. — [LingQ transcript](https://www.lingq.com/en/learn-english-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/)
- **[trade press]** A January 2015 trade article described flat, keep-it-simple design as the standard explainer look and said cut-out explainer videos were already over-commissioned. — [Digital Production](https://digitalproduction.com/2015/01/16/die-trends-der-erklaervideo-szene/)
- **[observation]** Template marketplaces sell many near-identical flat-design explainer packs, which shows the look is a commodity. — [Envato Elements listing](https://elements.envato.com/explainer-video-after-effect-template-2WEWSK3)
- **[descriptive research]** Hullman and Diakopoulos note that leaning heavily on a single rhetorical technique makes the framing more visible, and suggest spreading emphasis across several quieter techniques. — [paper PDF](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisRhetoric-InfoVis.pdf)
- **[descriptive research]** Segel and Heer list a consistent visual platform as a tactic that orients the viewer, i.e. consistency has a function. — [paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two different things get called a template. A consistent platform (palette, type, frame, voice, recurring structural markers) is endorsed by research and by Zhou's experience as what lets an audience follow unfamiliar material. A reused picture or a transition applied at every seam is what critics and practitioners object to. A technique catalogue is safe when each entry is a form with open content, and risky when entries become stock images.
- The illustration critique is the sharper risk for a script-first channel: a mapping from sentence function to technique can mechanise exactly the "picture illustrates the finished sentence" relation unless some beats are designed picture-first, with the sentence rewritten to depend on the image (Caswell's practice; McCloud's interdependent combination).
- The practical guard practitioners describe is rationing: a few bespoke, memorable images per episode (Fong's one moment that sticks; Kurzgesagt's scenes that deserve most attention) on top of a systematised base.
