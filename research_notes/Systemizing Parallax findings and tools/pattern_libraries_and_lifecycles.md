# Pattern libraries, choosers and lifecycles: how teams turn explored techniques into a usable system, and when not to

Research date: 2026-10-11. Verification level is marked on each finding: **[fetched]** = the page was opened and read; **[PDF read]** = the paper's text was extracted and read; **[search summary]** = the claim comes from a search-result summary and the page itself was not opened (treat as lower confidence). Opinion and vendor claims are labelled. Almost all sources assume a team or an organisation; where that matters for one person it is noted in the Inferences.

## 1. Anatomy of systems that work (design systems: layers, what one entry contains, how entries get a status)

### Takeaway
Mature design systems share a small, copyable skeleton: a few layers (style guide or tokens, component library, pattern library, plus the people who maintain it), a fixed page template per entry whose most useful parts are "when to use", "when not to use" and "how to decide between this and its siblings", and a three-to-five step status ladder with written entry criteria. The status ladder and the per-entry template transfer directly to a library of picture techniques; the governance apparatus mostly does not.

### Cited Findings

**Layers**
- NN/g defines a design system as a complete set of standards for managing design at scale with reusable components and patterns, and splits it into a repository (style guide; component library; pattern library) and the team that maintains it. Style guide = branding, content guidance, visual and interaction standards. Component library = reusable UI elements. Pattern library = reusable layouts, templates and content structures. Article dated 11 April 2021, marked as last reviewed 27 August 2026. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)
- Per NN/g, one component-library entry holds: name, description, attributes (customisable properties), states, code snippets, and the frameworks it supports. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)
- Tokens as the lowest layer are now near-universal in surveyed teams: 84% of respondents to zeroheight's 2025 report use design tokens, up from 56% in 2024 (294 self-selected respondents, collected September to November 2024; vendor report). **[fetched]** — [zeroheight Design Systems Report 2025](https://zeroheight.com/how-we-document)
- What surveyed systems actually contain (Sparkbox 2022, 219 respondents): component documentation 85%, content guidelines 45%, experience design principles 42%, release information 41%, code playground 34%, animation guidelines 19%. **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox Design Systems Survey 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/)

**What one entry contains (a real page, in order)**
- GOV.UK Design System "Accordion" page sections, in order: one-line introduction; When to use this component; When not to use this component; Decide between using accordions, tabs and details (a sibling-comparison section keyed on number of sections, how fast users switch, and amount of content); How it works (with sub-rules such as writing clear button text, when to add a summary line, do not disable sections); Research on this component; Known issues and gaps; Recent changes (version-tied, November 2022 to April 2025); Help improve this component (link to the discussion thread). **[fetched]** — [GOV.UK Design System, Accordion](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/accordion/)
- Carbon shows a status label ("Stable") and a last-updated date (18 August 2026) directly under the page title of its components overview, alongside "Accessibility status" and "Feature flags" tabs. **[fetched]** — [Carbon, Components overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/overview/components/)

**How entries are given a status**
- GitHub Primer uses three statuses: Experimental (a proof of concept or work in progress, possibly usable depending on maturity), Ready (no longer experimental; breaking changes mean a major version bump and a migration path), Deprecated (warns at use and points to an alternative). **[search summary]** — [Primer, Component status](https://primer.style/status)
- Carbon v9's status page used Ready, Under review and Deprecated, with a separate Experimental column; deprecated means replaced or no longer supported. **[search summary; the v9 host did not resolve when fetched]** — [Carbon v9, Component status](https://v9.carbondesignsystem.com/component-status)
- Shopify Polaris: Alpha = work in progress, fine for exploratory use, breaking changes expected; Beta = reached once the problem is clearly understood and the team is confident in the solution; then Stable and Deprecated. **[search summary; fetch failed twice]** — [Polaris, Component lifecycle](https://polaris-react.shopify.com/getting-started/components-lifecycle)
- SumUp Circuit UI adds a Legacy tier between stable and deprecated; its experimental components may not meet quality standards, are likely under-documented, and are exempt from semantic versioning. **[search summary]** — [Component Lifecycle for Circuit UI (link post)](https://jonathanstephens.us/links/component-lifecycle-for-circuit-ui)
- Morningstar uses Beta instead of Experimental: available for provisional use, may change with breaking changes. **[search summary]** — [Morningstar Design System, Component status](https://designsystem.morningstar.com/legacy/v/2.17.0/components/component-status.html)
- GOV.UK uses criteria rather than labels, in two stages. To *propose* a component or pattern it must be Useful (evidence it would help many teams, such as screenshots or links from different services) and Unique (does not duplicate something existing; if it replaces something, evidence it is better). To be *published* it must be Usable (shown to work in research with representative users), Consistent (reuses existing styles and components; follows the style guide and coding standards) and Versatile (flexible enough for a range of services; tested across browsers, devices and assistive technology). No fixed number of services is required. **[fetched]** — [GOV.UK Design System, Contribution criteria](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/contribution-criteria/)

**Team shape (relevant to a team of one)**
- Nathan Curtis (EightShapes), 17 September 2015, names three team models. Solitary: one team builds a system and makes it available but serves mainly its own product; useful for teams lacking design or front-end capacity, but adopters' needs are treated as a distraction. Centralised: a dedicated team makes and distributes components without designing products. Federated: designers from several product teams decide together while staying on their teams. **[fetched, via republication]** — [Curtis, Team Models for Scaling a Design System (Center Centre republication)](https://articles.centercentre.com/?p=860)
- NN/g recommends a minimum design-system team of one interaction designer, one visual designer and one developer plus an executive sponsor, and says a full design system is unlikely to pay off for a proof of concept or an early prototype that is likely to change; systems work best where years of replicable design work are expected. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)

### Inferences
- Structures that transfer to a library of picture techniques: (a) the layer split, read as tokens (palette, type, durations, easings) / techniques (single picture moves) / patterns (recurring combinations for a script situation) / guidelines (the doctrine); (b) the GOV.UK entry template, especially "when not to use" and "decide between X, Y and Z", which is the part a chooser cannot hold; (c) a three-status ladder (Experimental, Ready, Deprecated) with a visible last-reviewed date; (d) the GOV.UK two-stage criteria, where "Useful and Unique" is the bar to write an entry at all and "Usable, Consistent, Versatile" is the bar to call it a standard.
- Structures that do not transfer to one person: contribution processes, dedicated teams, sponsor roles, semantic versioning with migration paths. The solitary model's main risk (ignoring adopters) is absent when maker and user are the same person.
- "Known issues and gaps" and "Research on this component" map naturally to "what went wrong in the test reel" and "which episode used it and how it read", which is cheap to write at the moment of exploration and expensive to reconstruct later.

### Gaps
- Atlassian and Material component-page anatomy were not fetched; the anatomy above rests on GOV.UK (fetched) and NN/g's generic description.
- Polaris, Primer and Carbon v9 status definitions come from search summaries, not the pages themselves.
- Nathan Curtis's own writing on component status and on contributions could not be opened (Medium returned 403; the eightshapes.com domain redirected to a domain-auction page on 2026-10-11), so only the 2015 team-models piece is covered, via its republication.
- No source was found that documents a design system applied specifically to narrative or video picture techniques.

## 2. Choosers ("given what you want to say, pick the form")

### Takeaway
The choosers that last are entered by purpose (what relationship or point matters), not by data type, and stay shallow: FT has 9 purpose categories on one poster, From Data to Viz has 6 data-type entry points leading to about 39 chart types in 7 groups, Abela has 4. The standing criticism is that one-page trees are underspecified (several candidates, no way to pick among them), encode bare rules without reasons, and are only safe as a reminder for someone who already understands the options.

### Cited Findings

**FT Visual Vocabulary**
- A poster and companion site from the Financial Times Visual Journalism Team, inspired by the Graphic Continuum by Jon Schwabish and Severino Ribecca. Nine categories, each defined by the relationship the reader should see: Deviation, Correlation, Ranking, Distribution, Change over Time, Part-to-whole, Magnitude, Spatial, Flow. Each category carries a one-sentence description and a list of chart types. **[fetched]** — [FT Visual Vocabulary repository](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)
- The page lists 67 chart entries across the nine categories by the fetch tool's own tally (the page does not state a count), and several chart types recur in more than one category (for example slope and dot strip plot). **[fetched; count is a tally, not an FT figure]** — [FT Visual Vocabulary repository](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)
- Stated use: the core of a newsroom training session on chart literacy; meant to help people recognise when a chart can work alongside words, not to teach everyone to make charts. Sections for Uncertainty, Animation, Interactivity, Map projections and Colour are marked as to-do. Translations exist in at least Japanese, traditional and simplified Chinese, German, Spanish and French. Copyright FT, all rights reserved. **[fetched]** — [FT Visual Vocabulary repository](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)

**From Data to Viz**
- Built by Yan Holtz (data analyst) and Conor Healy (designer) in spare time; footer copyright 2018. Six entry buttons by data type: Numeric, Categoric, Num and Cat, Maps, Network, Time series. A decision tree leads to about twenty dataset formats; an overview lists 39 chart types grouped as Distribution, Correlation, Ranking, Part of a whole, Evolution, Map, Flow. Each chart page has a one-line summary and sections About, Common Mistakes, Code, Read More. A separate Caveats section shows 12 cards (ordering data, pie charts, overplotting, rainbow palettes and others) with a fuller gallery behind it. Also sold as a poster. **[fetched]** — [From Data to Viz](https://www.data-to-viz.com/)

**Abela's chart chooser and its critique**
- Abela's diagram ("Chart Suggestions: A Thought-Starter") branches first on four options: Comparison, Distribution, Composition, Relationship. Stephen Few's critique (21 July 2015, opinion) argues the four do not belong together (comparison is something every chart supports; relationship exists in nearly all data; only distribution names a data attribute), that the tree contradicts itself (time appears under both Comparison and Composition), that several branch conditions do not actually determine the chart (few versus many periods; item counts choosing bar orientation while ignoring label length), and that only the Relationship branch gives sound advice. **[fetched]** — [Few, Abela's Folly: A Thought Confuser](https://www.perceptualedge.com/blog/?p=2080)
- Few's prescription (opinion): use a matrix of chart types against purposes rather than a one-page tree; give the reasoning for each choice because bare do and don't rules teach nothing; treat the chooser as a reminder for people who have already learned the concepts, not as a standalone guide for novices. **[fetched]** — [Few, Abela's Folly](https://www.perceptualedge.com/blog/?p=2080)

**Bertini and Desbarats on why choosers fail**
- Enrico Bertini with Nick Desbarats (28 February 2024, opinion): choosers keyed on data type are underspecified, often offering several charts for the same data with no way to choose; choosers keyed only on purpose work only if you already know the specific point you want to make. The right starting question is what you want to say about the data, not how best to show it. Useful choosers add finer purpose questions (for example whether subtotals or fractions of the total matter more) and scalability criteria (overlapping lines suit a handful of cycles; a heat map suits many). **[fetched]** — [Bertini, Choosing chart choosers with Nick](https://filwd.substack.com/p/choosing-chart-choosers-with-nick)

**Evidence on effect**
- One study of novice designers reported that participants using a chooser tried more chart types (nine) than those without (seven) and did not feel the tool limited creativity, while experts with statistical backgrounds considered such tools oversimplifying and liable to steer novices wrong. **[search summary; chapter not opened]** — [Springer chapter, doi 10.1007/978-3-030-49713-2_6](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-49713-2_6)
- A practitioner piece argues most choosers consider only the data and overlook the audience. **[search summary; opinion]** — [UX Collective, Choosing a chart with your audience in mind](https://uxdesign.cc/choosing-a-chart-with-your-audience-in-mind-d6c2b839e8e6)

**Newsroom style guides and cookbooks**
- Amy Cesal (Nightingale) defines data-visualisation style guidelines as standards for how information is formatted and designed, covering the what (for example chart types) and the why (for example reasons for specific colours), usually accompanied by templates for the tools people actually use (Excel, R, D3, Tableau) to supply the how. She built guides for the Sunlight Foundation and, in 2017, the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. **[search summary]** — [Cesal, What Are Data Visualization Style Guidelines?](https://nightingaledvs.com/what-are-data-visualization-style-guidelines/)
- Jon Schwabish and co-authors: a data-visualisation style guide is distinct from a brand guide, should describe styles for graphs, charts and diagrams specifically, and typically covers colour, font and layout; brand colours that work on a billboard may fail in a chart. **[search summary]** — [Schwabish, Elevating Data Visualization: Building and Using Effective Style Guides](https://jschwabish.substack.com/p/elevating-data-visualization-building)
- A public collection of such guides (including the BBC, Dallas Morning News, Sunlight Foundation, CFPB) was started by Schwabish in May 2016. **[search summary]** — [The Data Visualization Style Guide Collection](https://responsibledata.io/?p=2914)
- BBC bbplot is the executable form of a style guide: an R package with two functions. `bbc_style()` takes no arguments and applies house text size, font, colour, axis and gridline styling to a chart; `finalise_plot()` left-aligns title, subtitle and source, adds the footer, and saves at a default 640 by 450 pixels. It is paired with a cookbook of worked chart examples. The repository shows about 1.6k stars and only 13 commits. **[fetched]** — [bbc/bbplot](https://github.com/bbc/bbplot); cookbook at [BBC Visual and Data Journalism cookbook for R graphics](https://bbc.github.io/rcookbook/) (title confirmed; body not retrievable)

### Inferences
- A chooser whose rows are "what the sentence in the script is doing" is a purpose-first chooser, which is the design the critics favour over data-first trees. Its known failure is underspecification: one sentence job will map to several techniques. The fix the sources point to is a second discriminator per row (how many things, how long the beat, is there a number, has this move already been used this episode) and a "when not to use" line, not more rows.
- Usable-chooser numbers from the examples: 4 to 9 top-level entries, one level deep, a sentence of definition per entry, and several named options under each. Fifteen families is above that range for a one-page poster but workable as the second level under 5 to 9 sentence jobs.
- FT repeats chart types under several categories. That is evidence that a single tree is the wrong shape for a purpose-keyed catalogue and that a technique should be allowed to appear under more than one sentence job.
- bbplot shows the lightest durable form of a style guide: two functions and a page of examples. Its 13 commits suggest that a guide encoded as defaults needs little upkeep, unlike prose documentation.
- Few's point that a chooser is a reminder, not a teacher, fits a solo creator well: the person who explored the 70 techniques already has the understanding, so the chooser only needs to trigger recall.

### Gaps
- The Data Viz Project (Ferdio) could not be fetched (HTTP 403); its count of chart types and its filter facets (family, input, function, shape) are not verified here.
- The original date and Abela's own description of his chooser were not retrieved; only Few's 2015 critique was read.
- Reuters, Economist, NYT, Guardian, Urban Institute and Dallas Morning News guides were not opened individually. The Economist's public self-critique of its charts and the Urban Institute guide are known to exist but are not verified in this pass.
- A reported Data Visualization Society discussion in which experts say chart taxonomies oversimplify and limit innovation, and a remark attributed to Leland Wilkinson that chart taxonomies are harmful, surfaced only in a search summary without a confirmed URL; treat as unverified.
- No controlled study was found comparing chooser designs (number of entry points, depth) for speed or quality of choice.

## 3. Motion and film equivalents (motion documentation; show bibles and shot vocabularies; explainer studios)

### Takeaway
Motion systems are documented in three tiers that copy cleanly: a few principles, a small token table for duration and easing (Carbon has 6 durations and 3 curves in 2 styles; Material has 16 durations, 7 easings and 6 springs), and a handful of named transition patterns each tied to a relationship between the two states. Animation productions codify look through a bible, a style guide and model sheets. No explainer studio or channel examined publishes an internal visual system; the public evidence is that consistency comes from a few fixed constraints (a colour script, a narrator, recurring characters) plus review by people.

### Cited Findings

**Carbon motion (IBM)**
- Two styles: Productive (subtle, efficient; for task-focused moments such as button states, dropdowns, tables) and Expressive (vivid; reserved for occasional significant moments such as opening a page, a primary action, a system alert). **[fetched]** — [Carbon, Motion overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/) (last updated 12 August 2026)
- Easing: three curves by what the element does, each in both styles. Standard (element visible throughout): productive cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0.38, 0.9), expressive (0.4, 0.14, 0.3, 1). Entrance: productive (0, 0, 0.38, 0.9), expressive (0, 0, 0.3, 1). Exit: productive (0.2, 0, 1, 0.9), expressive (0.4, 0.14, 1, 1). **[fetched]** — [Carbon, Motion overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)
- Duration tokens with intended use: fast-01 70 ms (buttons, toggles), fast-02 110 ms (fades), moderate-01 150 ms (small expansion, short moves), moderate-02 240 ms (expansion, toasts), slow-01 400 ms (large expansion, important notifications), slow-02 700 ms (background dimming). Durations should scale with distance or size of change. **[fetched]** — [Carbon, Motion overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)
- Carbon components ship their own micro-interactions, but choreography between components and page entrances and exits is explicitly left to product teams; large transitions should keep a continuous element to guide the eye; a static alternative should always exist. **[fetched]** — [Carbon, Motion overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)

**Material 3 motion (Google)**
- Duration tokens: short1 to short4 = 50, 100, 150, 200 ms; medium1 to medium4 = 250, 300, 350, 400 ms; long1 to long4 = 450, 500, 550, 600 ms; extraLong1 to extraLong4 = 700, 800, 900, 1000 ms. **[fetched]** — [Material Components Android, Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)
- Easing tokens: standard (0.2, 0, 0, 1); standard decelerate (0, 0, 0, 1); standard accelerate (0.3, 0, 1, 1); emphasized (a path, not a single bezier); emphasized decelerate (0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1); emphasized accelerate (0.3, 0, 0.8, 0.15); linear. **[fetched]** — [Material Components Android, Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)
- Spring tokens split by what moves: spatial (position, size) versus effects (colour, opacity), each at fast, default and slow. Damping and stiffness: spatial 0.9/1400 (small components), 0.9/700 (partial-screen), 0.9/300 (full-screen); effects 1/3800, 1/1600, 1/800. **[fetched]** — [Material Components Android, Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)
- Four named transition patterns, each chosen by the relationship between start and end: Container transform (elements that share a container, such as a card opening into a detail view); Shared axis (spatial or navigational relationship along x, y or z); Fade through (no strong relationship); Fade (elements entering or leaving within the screen). **[fetched]** — [Material Components Android, Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)
- Only 19% of surveyed design systems include animation guidelines at all (Sparkbox 2022). **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox Design Systems Survey 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/)

**Animation production documents**
- Model sheets: the animator's reference for a character, showing views from all angles, a range of poses and relative scale; used to check the intended look while drawing and to teach other artists. **[search summary]** — [Where Creativity Works, Model Sheets](https://wherecreativityworks.com/model-sheets/)
- Style guide: studios often compile model sheets into one booklet, which can also carry backgrounds, colour guides, and the logos and fonts for title cards. **[search summary]** — [Mad Dog Movies, Animation style guides (17 January 2025)](https://maddogmovies.com/2025/01/17/animation-style-guides/)
- Show or production bible: the wider reference. One guide describes model sheets as fixing how a character is drawn from every angle, style guides as fixing colour and line language, and world rules as fixing what is physically possible; sections run from premise and tone through characters, world and episode structure. Its stated purpose is that a studio sending episodes to different animation houses needs consistency written down or the show visibly drifts. **[search summary; vendor blog]** — [Storyflow, What is a production bible](https://storyflow.so/blog/what-is-a-production-bible-complete-guide)
- A pitch bible (to sell a show) and a production bible (for the crew) are different documents. **[search summary]** — [Robin Piree, pitch bible guide](https://robinpiree.com/blog/pitch-bible); archival example of separate show bible, style guide and production notes at [Jewel Riders Archive](https://www.jewelridersarchive.com/development/)

**Explainer studios and channels**
- Kurzgesagt publishes a five-phase process (research; script, about a dozen drafts; illustration, preceded by a sketching phase where visual metaphors are settled; voiceover and animation, with narration setting animation timing; music and sound) and a per-video source sheet. Its public page describes the style (vector, pixel art and infographics; recurring characters) but publishes no style guide, asset-reuse policy, hours per video or team size. **[fetched]** — [Kurzgesagt, Our video process](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- A reposted copy of Kurzgesagt's own process article says a typical video has about 200 illustrated panels and that, although an illustration library exists, most illustrations are still drawn from scratch to keep videos distinctive. **[search summary; repost, not the primary]** — [LingQ repost of Kurzgesagt's "how to make a Kurzgesagt video" article (exact title not verified)](https://www.lingq.com/de/englisch-lernen-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/)
- Vox: a job posting for an animator on the Unexplainable show asks the hire to develop a graphics package under the Art Director and to keep video packages in line with "the Vox style guide", confirming an internal written guide exists; its contents are not public. **[search summary]** — [Mediabistro, Vox Media animator posting](https://www.mediabistro.com/jobs/3539237225-animator-motion-designer-unexplainable-part-time-temporary)
- An outside tutorial observes that Vox graphics are often built at 12 fps inside a 24 fps edit (third-party observation, not confirmed by Vox). **[search summary]** — [PremiumBeat, Replicating Vox motion graphics](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/replicating-vox-motion-graphic/)
- The Pudding works as a collective where each member can do every step, has no central editorial planning, kills many ideas during production, and publishes process guides for beginners rather than a style manual. **[search summary]** — [Wikipedia, The Pudding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pudding); [Storybench, How The Pudding structures stories as visual essays](https://www.storybench.org/?p=8613); [The Pudding, About](https://pudding.cool/about)
- TED-Ed commissions outside directors per film. Director Henrique Barone deliberately varied style by script section and said a colour script for the main character's colours was close to the only direction he gave the team. **[search summary]** — [Stash, Henrique Barone for TED-Ed](https://www.stashmedia.tv/henrique-barone-explores-the-dangers-of-venturing-into-space-for-ted-ed/)

### Inferences
- The motion-token pattern is the most directly reusable structure in this whole survey: name durations and easings by role (what is moving and why), keep the table small, and split every curve into a quiet and a loud register. Carbon's productive versus expressive split is the same idea as an everyday register versus a hero register.
- Material's four transition patterns are a chooser in miniature: one question (how are the two states related) and four answers. That is a workable model for a cut and transition table keyed on the relationship between adjacent pictures.
- Carbon's explicit refusal to systemise choreography is a useful boundary: the system fixes the atoms and leaves sequencing to judgement.
- Animation bibles exist because episodes are farmed out to different hands. For one maker the drift risk is across time (episode 12 no longer matching episode 2), so the equivalent document is a small set of locked constants (palette, type, a colour script, a few "always" and "never" rules) rather than a full bible.
- The explainer studios' public record suggests they keep variety by drawing fresh and constrain only a few things. None of them publishes a technique catalogue, so there is no proven public model of a 70-entry visual system for a video essay channel.

### Gaps
- No primary published show bible or studio style guide was read in this pass; the descriptions of bibles, style guides and model sheets come from secondary and partly vendor sources, and colour scripts and layout or camera guides were not sourced at all.
- Material's own site could not be read (the page returned no body); values come from Google's Material Components Android documentation, which mirrors the tokens. Spotify and Lottie-based motion systems were not researched.
- No published internal visual system was found for Kurzgesagt, Vox, TED-Ed, Polygon or The Pudding. Polygon was not searched separately.
- Hours per Kurzgesagt video and team size are not on the page fetched.

## 4. Pattern languages (Alexander's form, its record, and pattern treatments of film grammar and visual explanation)

### Takeaway
The pattern form buys a shared vocabulary (often the name alone is enough), a rationale attached to each solution, and links between patterns; its record is that catalogues are achievable and useful while true generative languages are hard to produce, evidence of benefit in practice is thin, and lookup time grows with the size of the collection. For visual explanation there are several research catalogues organised by narrative purpose that are close analogues to "what the sentence is doing", including one that codes 50 data videos by narrative role.

### Cited Findings

**What the form is and what it buys**
- Dearden and Finlay's critical review (Human-Computer Interaction 21, 2006, pp. 49 to 102) summarises agreed characteristics of a pattern: it includes a rationale explaining why the solution is recommended and what the trade-offs are; it is visible in the finished artefact; it is part of a language (related to other patterns); it is validated by use, meaning found in a range of successful designs; it is grounded in a domain; and it captures a big idea, so that not every design problem warrants a pattern. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay, Pattern Languages in HCI: A Critical Review](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- In Alexander's A Pattern Language the patterns are organised two ways at once: grouped by physical scale (towns, then neighbourhoods, then rooms), and linked as a network in which each pattern points back to the larger patterns that set its context and forward to the smaller patterns that help realise it. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- Meaningful names matter: in Alexander's usage the pattern names alone are enough to carry a design discussion, and software authors report pattern names working as a standard technical vocabulary so that a name can explain a complex design. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)

**Where it has fallen short**
- Catalogue versus language: the Gang of Four described their book as a catalogue with some interrelations, not a pattern language in Alexander's sense, classified by area of concern (creational, structural, behavioural). Coplien and Schmidt (1995) expected catalogues to give most of the payoff because comprehensive languages are hard to produce. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- Evidence: the review found no published observational or empirical studies of software developers using patterns in practice, calls the lack of substantive evidence of benefit for actual design practice one of the most obvious weaknesses of HCI pattern research, and treats the three evaluation studies it found (Borchers 2002; Dearden and Finlay and colleagues 2002; Chung and colleagues 2004) as not conclusive. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- Findability: several authors warned that the time to find a pattern rises as more are published, and one argued that patterns only help in live design if they are internalised, since looking them up mid-task is not practical. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- Choosing between alternatives: Tidwell's interface pattern language offers distinct alternative patterns for similar problems without specifying how to select between them; van Duyne and colleagues add text on the contexts that suit each alternative. **[PDF read]** — [Dearden and Finlay 2006](https://research.cs.vt.edu/ns/cs5724papers/dearden-patterns-hci09.pdf)
- Alexander's own verdict on software patterns (OOPSLA 1996 keynote, published 1999): the talk reflects on where the software community hit and missed in adapting his ideas; Jim Coplien's introduction says the community had lost the system perspective and the human perspective and had barely touched the moral aim of building whole systems that improve quality of life. **[search summary]** — [Alexander, The Origins of Pattern Theory](https://www.patternlanguage.com/archive/ieee.html)
- Patterns as workarounds: Peter Norvig (Object World, 5 May 1996) argued that 16 of the 23 Gang of Four patterns are either invisible or simpler in Dylan or Lisp, because the language already provides the mechanism. **[search summary; slide mirror, not Norvig's own site]** — [Norvig, Design Patterns in Dynamic Programming (mirror)](https://slidetodoc.com/design-patterns-in-dynamic-programming-peter-norvig-chief/)

**Pattern-style treatments of visual explanation**
- Segel and Heer (IEEE InfoVis 2010) analysed 58 narrative visualisations and identified seven genres: magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, and film/video/animation. Their design space has three divisions: genre; visual narrative tactics (visual structuring, highlighting, transition guidance); narrative structure tactics (ordering, interactivity, messaging). Stories sit on a spectrum from author-driven (linear order, heavy messaging, no interactivity) to reader-driven; three hybrid structures are named: martini glass, interactive slideshow, drill-down story. They note that narrative messaging (repetition of key points, multi-messaging across text, image and audio, annotation) was under-used in their sample. **[PDF read]** — [Segel and Heer, Narrative Visualization: Telling Stories with Data](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-Narrative-InfoVis.pdf)
- Stolper, Lee, Henry Riche and Stasko (Microsoft Research technical report, 2016) coded 45 author-driven data stories and catalogued 20 techniques under four purposes: communicating narrative and explaining data; linking separated story elements; enhancing structure and navigation; providing controlled exploration. Named techniques include textual narrative, annotations, element highlighting, flowchart arrows, tooltips, linking through colour, animation or interaction, breadcrumbs, section header buttons, timeline, geographic map, scrolling, and next and previous buttons. **[PDF read]** — [Stolper et al., Emerging and Recurring Data-Driven Storytelling Techniques](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/MSR-TR-2016-14-Storytelling-Techniques.pdf)
- Bach, Stefaner, Boy, Drucker, Bartram, Wood, Ciuccarelli, Engelhardt, Koeppen and Tversky (chapter 5 of Data-Driven Storytelling, 2018) analyse 18 narrative design patterns, each serving a specific purpose such as engaging the audience, evoking empathy, or creating flow and rhythm, usable alone or combined; they assume the storyteller already knows the story, purpose and audience. The chapter groups patterns under argumentation, flow, framing the narrative, empathy and emotion, and engagement. **[fetched abstract; grouping from search summary of the table of contents]** — [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/narrative-design-patterns-for-data-driven-storytelling/)
- The same patterns were issued as a card deck (one technique per card) intended to give practitioners inspiration and structure; the patterns are not mutually exclusive. The project came out of a 2016 Dagstuhl workshop. **[fetched]** — [NAPA cards](http://napa-cards.net/)
- Amini, Henry Riche, Lee, Hurter and Irani (CHI 2015) coded 50 professionally made data videos using four narrative roles from visual-narrative theory: Establisher, Initial, Peak, Release. Findings: average length 3 minutes (range 1 to 7.5); average of six data visualisations per video (range 1 to 19); 48% of running time on data visualisations on average; the most common structure, in 34% of videos, was several establishers, several initials, one peak near the middle, then several releases; other common structures end on the peak with no release, leaving the viewer with a question. About 20% of videos open with a question and 30% close with a spoken or written take-away; 46% of new facts are accompanied by a repetition; visualisations are usually paired with an attention cue. Workshops with professional storytellers led the authors to recommend a non-linear creation process that allows going back to earlier phases. **[PDF read]** — [Amini et al., Understanding Data Videos](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/DataVideoStorytellingCHI2015_Revision_Final.pdf)
- Roth (The Cartographic Journal 58(1), 2021) extends Segel and Heer to maps: seven visual storytelling genres distinguished by the technique that enforces narrative order, and seven tropes, defined as design techniques used to advance the narrative rather than to represent information; one trope is attention, realised by highlighting, arrows, numbering, frames, leader lines, opacity masks and callouts. **[search summary]** — [Roth, Cartographic Design as Visual Storytelling](https://doi.org/10.1080/00087041.2019.1633103); attention techniques from a 2022 talk at [AutoCarto 2022 slides](https://cartogis.org/docs/autocarto/2022/docs/presentations/session7_roth_presentation.pdf)
- Film grammar as patterns: Wu, Palù, Ranon and Christie define Film Editing Patterns, a language that formalises editing practice as constraints over one or more shots (shot size, camera angle, on-screen layout of actors); user tests suggested the patterns help novice and intermediate users build sequences with little training. **[search summary]** — [Wu et al., Thinking Like a Director](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01950718)
- Shi, Lan, Li, Li and Cao (CHI 2021) published a design space for animated visual narratives in data videos, later used as a base by tools that link narration to animation. **[search summary of citing papers]** — [WonderFlow (arXiv 2308.04040), related work](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04040)

### Inferences
- The split that matters is catalogue versus language. A catalogue (name, when, why, example) is what the Gang of Four, Stolper and Bach produced and is what the record says pays off. A generative language (follow the links and a design falls out) is what Alexander wanted and what almost nobody has delivered. For 70 techniques the realistic target is a catalogue with good names and "see also" links, not a grammar.
- The strongest benefit claimed for patterns is vocabulary. For one person who also briefs an automated pipeline, stable technique names are the part of the pattern form most likely to earn its keep; the forces and context prose is the part most likely to rot.
- The research catalogues are organised the same way the creator's map is: by what the device does for the narrative (Stolper's four purposes; Bach's five groups; Roth's tropes; Amini's four narrative roles). Amini's Establisher, Initial, Peak, Release coding is a ready-made coarse facet for "where in the beat does this technique belong".
- Alexander's two-way organisation (grouped by scale and linked as a network) suggests a cheap structural check: each technique entry should say which larger pattern it sits inside and which smaller moves complete it.
- Norvig's point translates to templates: if a "technique" is just a parameter of an existing template, it does not need its own entry. Dearden's "captures a big idea" criterion says the same thing from the other side.
- The findability warning is the main argument against writing 70 full pattern entries: beyond what can be held in the head, lookup has to be faster than re-deriving the choice.

### Gaps
- The individual names of Bach et al.'s 18 patterns, Roth's seven genres and seven tropes, and Wu et al.'s pattern vocabulary were not retrieved.
- The corpus size and the counts of animation techniques and narrative strategies in Shi et al. (2021) could not be verified and are deliberately not stated.
- No source was found that applies Alexander's full pattern form to film grammar for essay video, and no explicit pattern-language treatment of explorable explanations or scrollytelling was retrieved (Stolper et al. covers scrolling stories as techniques, not as a language).
- Alexander's specific criticisms of software patterns rest on a search summary of the keynote, not a reading of the text.
- Empirical evidence that using a pattern catalogue improves the quality of visual stories was not found; Dearden and Finlay's 2006 conclusion that such evidence is thin has not been checked against later work.

## 5. Classification (keeping about 70 techniques findable)

### Takeaway
Library science and UX sources agree on the shape: use a single hierarchy only when entities have one dimension, a grid for two, and facets for three or more; keep each facet a short controlled list; and prefer broad, shallow structures over deep ones. No authoritative source gives a magic number of top-level categories, and the well-known seven-plus-or-minus-two limit is explicitly not a rule for menu breadth.

### Cited Findings

**Hierarchy, grid or facets**
- William Denton's how-to (November 2003, last modified 28 March 2009): a hierarchy or tree suits entities with one dimension of classification and should be used when it works; a paradigm (two-dimensional grid) suits two independent aspects; facets handle three or more dimensions. **[fetched]** — [Denton, How to Make a Faceted Classification and Put It On the Web](https://www.miskatonic.org/library/facet-web-howto.html)
- Denton's seven steps: collect a representative sample of the domain (all of it if small); break descriptions into basic concepts; create facets by finding high-level categories that cut across entities, narrowed to be mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive; arrange terms under facets and test-classify; choose a default facet order; classify each entity; then revise, test with users and maintain. **[fetched]** — [Denton 2003](https://www.miskatonic.org/library/facet-web-howto.html)
- Criteria for a good facet (after Spiteri): differentiation, relevance to the purpose of the scheme, ascertainability (the value can be determined reliably), permanence (a lasting quality of the item), homogeneity, mutual exclusivity (one characteristic of division per facet). Facets should be more than yes or no switches, and the scheme should leave room to add subjects, facets and terms. **[fetched]** — [Denton 2003](https://www.miskatonic.org/library/facet-web-howto.html)
- Number of facets is domain-dependent: cited examples range from five (Ranganathan's PMEST) to thirteen (Bliss BC2) to eighteen (Vickery's soil classification). The design of a classification must follow its purpose. **[fetched]** — [Denton 2003](https://www.miskatonic.org/library/facet-web-howto.html)
- NN/g (Page Laubheimer, 3 July 2022): a taxonomy is a closed, hierarchically arranged list of acceptable terms used to describe and classify content, and is a kind of controlled vocabulary. A hierarchical taxonomy offers one organising principle at a time; a faceted taxonomy keeps a small separate hierarchy per attribute, so people can combine filters instead of walking a deep tree. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Taxonomy 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/taxonomy-101/)

**When classification becomes a burden**
- NN/g's cautions: start small, since a taxonomy need not be a multi-year project and a small set of consistently applied topics is a fine start; skip concepts that apply to only one item; do not build branches that will not help users; ontologies are large projects usually done by teams, not individual practitioners. The build process ends with ongoing governance: periodic review to add, rename, merge or remove terms, and spot checks of how items are tagged. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Taxonomy 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/taxonomy-101/)

**Breadth, depth and the number of categories**
- Miller (1981) held the target count at 64 items across four menu shapes and found response time rose with depth. Larson and Czerwinski (1998) arranged 512 items as 8x8x8, 16x32 and 32x16 and found the two-level structures gave reliably faster search than the three-level one. Kiger (1984) and Zaphiris and Mtei (1997) likewise found broad, shallow structures faster. **[search summary]** — [Human Factors International, Breadth vs. depth](https://www.humanfactors.com/newsletters/breadth_vs_depth.asp); [Zaphiris et al., Expandable indexes versus sequential menus](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/papers/Zaphiris2002Expandable.pdf)
- The advantage of breadth has a limit: a Microsoft Research web study found depth harmed search, but a medium breadth and depth condition outperformed the broadest, shallowest one. **[search summary]** — [Larson and Czerwinski, Web Page Design: Implications of Memory, Structure and Scent](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/web-page-design-implications-memory-structure-scent-information-retrieval/)
- Menu breadth is not governed by the seven-plus-or-minus-two memory limit; for N items at breadth B the depth is log N over log B, and total time has one term that grows with breadth and one that shrinks, so an optimum exists. **[search summary]** — [Dix et al., HCI book online: menu breadth](https://hcibook.com/e3/online/menu-breadth)

**Tags**
- Vendor guidance on reference libraries: tags let one item sit in several categories without duplicating it, where folders force duplication; suggesting existing tags as you type keeps the vocabulary consistent. **[search summary; vendor]** — [Eagle, How tags boost design asset organisation](https://www.eagle.cool/blog/post/streamline-file-management-how-eagle-tags-boost-design-assets-organization)

### Inferences
- Seventy techniques described by more than two independent properties (what the sentence is doing, which visual family, cost to produce, register, status) are a faceted problem by Denton's rule. The 15 families and the sentence jobs are two facets of the same catalogue, not rival hierarchies; forcing one to be the parent of the other is what makes single-tree catalogues hard to search.
- Arithmetic check using the breadth and depth formula: 70 items fit in two levels at about 8 or 9 per level, or one level of 15 families averaging under 5 each. Both are inside the "broad and shallow" range the studies favour, so 15 families is not too many for browsing, but it is too many for a one-page chooser's first question (compare 4 to 9 in section 2).
- A decision table from situation to technique is Denton's "paradigm" (a two-dimensional grid): rows are sentence jobs, columns are a second discriminator (for example quantity present or not, or register), cells hold one to three techniques. This is also the matrix form Few recommends over a tree.
- Facet hygiene worth copying: each facet should be ascertainable from the script line alone (so the choice can be made at scripting time), permanent (not "currently fashionable"), and never a yes or no switch.
- The burden threshold in the sources is the move from controlled lists to relationships between terms (an ontology). For one person the practical stopping point is a few short closed lists plus free-text notes.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source gives a recommended number of top-level categories; the breadth and depth studies are about menu search time on screens, not catalogues consulted by their own author, and are reported here from secondary summaries.
- Heather Hedden's The Accidental Taxonomist and primary library-science texts on controlled vocabulary were not consulted.
- No evidence was found on how well a person retrieves from a self-built classification over months or years (the relevant case here), as opposed to first-time users of someone else's scheme.

## 6. Lifecycle and governance (from exploration to standard, ownership, retirement, staleness)

### Takeaway
Independent traditions converge on the same rule: do not standardise on first sight, wait for about three real uses, and make promotion depend on evidence of use rather than on having explored something. The copyable instruments are a four-ring radar (try, use with care, default, avoid), a one-page decision record that is superseded rather than edited, a short status ladder, and freshness dates with an owner; all were designed for organisations, and the parts that survive for one person are the rings, the record and the date.

### Cited Findings

**Rule of three and the cost of early abstraction**
- The rule of three is attributed to Don Roberts and popularised by Martin Fowler's Refactoring: do it the first time, tolerate the duplication the second time, refactor the third time. The stated risk of going earlier is choosing the wrong abstraction, which then has to be redone as requirements emerge. **[search summary]** — [Wikipedia, Rule of three (computer programming)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(computer_programming))
- Sandi Metz (20 January 2016; from a 2014 talk): duplication costs far less than a wrong abstraction. Her failure sequence: someone extracts shared code into an abstraction; a new requirement almost fits; the next person adds a parameter and a conditional rather than undo it; this repeats until the code is unreadable, held in place by sunk cost. Her remedy is to inline the abstraction back into each caller, delete what each caller does not use, and only then look again for the real common part. **[fetched]** — [Metz, The Wrong Abstraction](https://sandimetz.com/blog/2016/1/20/the-wrong-abstraction)
- Kent C. Dodds's AHA ("avoid hasty abstractions", a name he credits to Cher Scarlett) sets no count: wait until the abstraction is well-informed, and optimise for change first because you cannot know how things will evolve. He reports a codebase where code reused in far more than three places was still a bad abstraction he wished had stayed duplicated. **[search summary]** — [Dodds, AHA Programming](https://kentcdodds.com/blog/aha-programming)
- Dan Mall, "Three Times is a Pattern" (opinion): if one team needs a component, wait; at two, still wait; three or more teams needing it now or within the current quarter make it a candidate for the system, because a design system exists to solve common problems. **[search summary; the page returned 404 when fetched on 2026-10-11]** — [Mall, Three Times is a Pattern](https://danmall.com/posts/three-times-is-a-pattern) (archived copy listed at [v5.danmall.com](https://v5.danmall.com/posts/three-times-is-a-pattern/))

**Design-system governance and contribution**
- Brad Frost's governance process (4 November 2019): use what exists; if nothing fits, talk to the system's maintainers; decide whether the new work is a one-off "snowflake" (it stays in the product's backlog) or belongs in the shared system; prototype; review; build and test; document and add to the changelog; release under semantic versioning. One-offs are tracked so they can be folded into the system later if they prove broadly useful. **[fetched]** — [Frost, A design system governance process](https://bradfrost.com/blog/post/a-design-system-governance-process/)
- GOV.UK's two gates (propose: useful and unique; publish: usable, consistent, versatile), as in section 1. **[fetched]** — [GOV.UK contribution criteria](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/contribution-criteria/)
- How rare a working contribution process is: 36% of surveyed systems have no defined contribution process, only 33% of maintainers are satisfied with theirs, and 44% have a governance model or shared roadmap (Sparkbox 2022). In organisations over 500 people it is common for about 1% of the wider company to contribute, and about a third restrict contribution to the system team (zeroheight 2025, vendor). **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/); [zeroheight 2025](https://zeroheight.com/how-we-document)

**Technology radar**
- Thoughtworks' radar has four rings. Adopt: proven, should be seriously considered. Trial: ready for use but less proven; a blip reaches Trial only after production use by Thoughtworks. Assess: worth exploring and watching, not yet ready to trial. The fourth ring is for things that are widely accepted but with which Thoughtworks has had poor experiences. Adopt is reserved for cases where not using the thing would be a poor choice. **[fetched]** — [Thoughtworks Technology Radar FAQ](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/faq)
- Naming conflict: the FAQ as fetched on 2026-10-11 names the fourth ring "Caution"; the long-standing name, still used in third-party guides and in a Thoughtworks podcast that glosses Hold as "proceed with caution", is "Hold". No announcement of a rename was found. **[fetched versus search summary]** — [Thoughtworks FAQ](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/faq); contradicted on the name by [InfoQ podcast on the Technology Radar](https://www.infoq.com/podcasts/technology-radar-reality-software-development)
- Mechanics: items are nominated from real project work; a board of about 20 senior technologists meets twice a year and debates inclusion and ring; the radar is published twice a year; quadrant placement is treated as minor and ring as what matters; a blip normally appears for one edition unless it moves, and older blips stay in an archive but are no longer updated. Thoughtworks encourages organisations to build their own and offers a tool for it. **[fetched]** — [Thoughtworks FAQ](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/faq)

**Decision records**
- Michael Nygard's architecture decision record (15 November 2011): sections are Title (a short noun phrase, numbered), Context (the forces, stated as facts), Decision (active voice, "We will"), Status (proposed, accepted, deprecated or superseded), Consequences (positive, negative and neutral). One or two pages; stored in the repository as numbered files; numbers never reused; old records are not edited or deleted but marked superseded with a pointer to the replacement. The motive is that large documents are not kept up to date and few people read them, while small modular records are more likely to be both. **[fetched]** — [Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)

**Golden path**
- Spotify (2020) defines the golden path as the opinionated and supported way to build something, delivered with a tutorial; it was a response to "rumour-driven development", where the only way to learn how to do something was to ask a colleague. Goals: the recommended tooling is easy to find, the route is clear, instructions are good, and it is obvious where to get help. The first golden path began as a Hack Week project about six years before the post. **[search summary]** — [Spotify Engineering, How We Use Golden Paths to Solve Fragmentation](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2020/8/how-we-use-golden-paths-to-solve-fragmentation-in-our-software-ecosystem); coverage at [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2021/03/spotify-paved-paths)

**Retirement and staleness**
- Status ladders retire entries explicitly: Primer's Deprecated warns at use and points to an alternative; Circuit UI inserts a Legacy tier before deprecation. **[search summary]** — [Primer status](https://primer.style/status); [Circuit UI lifecycle (link post)](https://jonathanstephens.us/links/component-lifecycle-for-circuit-ui)
- Google attaches freshness dates to documents, recording when each was last reviewed, and metadata triggers a reminder when a document has not been touched for a set period (three months is given as an example). Documents have owners, are version-controlled and reviewed like code, and anyone is expected to fix a document they find wrong. **[search summary of the book chapter]** — [Software Engineering at Google, ch. 10 Documentation](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/software-engineering-at/9781492082781/ch10.html); quote record at [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/11341657-at-google-we-often-attach-freshness-dates-to-documentation-such); summary at [dev.to](https://dev.to/rockandnull/glimpse-of-google-documentation-5e5n)
- Carbon and NN/g both print a last-updated or last-reviewed date at the top of the page. **[fetched]** — [Carbon components overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/overview/components/); [NN/g Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)
- What users of stale systems report: 35% say it is unclear what is old, broken or coming soon; 19% say the system is out of date; 39% say it is poorly documented (Sparkbox 2022, subscriber respondents). Outdated or legacy components are described as a common trait of low-trust systems (zeroheight 2025, vendor). **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/); [zeroheight 2025](https://zeroheight.com/how-we-document)

### Inferences
- Translating "three teams" for one person: the unit of independent demand is a distinct real use, not a test reel. A workable ladder that combines the radar with the rule of three: Assess = explored in a reel; Trial = used once in a real script; Adopt = used in three separate script situations and still liked; Hold or Caution = tried and found to read badly or cost too much. The fourth ring is the one a solo creator is most likely to skip and most likely to regret, because it stores negative results that are otherwise re-learned.
- Metz's failure sequence has a direct visual analogue: a template built from one use accretes switches and variants until choosing its settings is harder than building the picture. Her remedy (inline it back, then look again) is the retirement procedure for an over-general template.
- The decision record is the cheapest instrument that prevents re-litigating: one short dated note per standardisation decision, never edited, superseded by a new one. It also covers the "experiment log" role: context, what was tried, what was decided, consequences.
- Golden path for one person means one default route per common sentence job that is known to work end to end, with everything else allowed but unsupported. "Opinionated and supported" is a higher bar than "documented": an entry should not be called a standard unless the path to produce it works without fiddling.
- Staleness controls that scale down: a status and a last-reviewed date on each entry, and documentation that is generated from or sits next to the thing it describes. Controls that do not scale down: owners, reviewers, reminder systems, a twice-yearly board.
- Frost's snowflake rule is the permission to not systemise: a one-off stays a one-off, tracked only by a note, until it recurs.

### Gaps
- RFC processes were not researched separately from decision records.
- Lab notebooks and experiment logs as a named practice in design or motion work were not researched; no source is offered for them.
- "Paved road" (Netflix) was not sourced separately from Spotify's golden path.
- The Google documentation practices come from summaries of the book chapter, not the chapter text.
- Dan Mall's piece could not be opened; its date is not recorded.
- No source was found describing how a single creator governs a personal technique library over years.

## 7. Evidence on cost and benefit

### Takeaway
The measured benefit of a design system is speed on repeated, well-covered tasks (34% faster in a vendor study with no stated sample; a median of 2 hours versus 4.2 in an eight-person agency test), and the measured costs are maintenance, staffing and documentation that goes unclear or out of date. On creativity, the review evidence says moderate constraints help and heavy ones hurt, examples cause copying of their features even when flawed, and shared libraries coincide with measurable visual convergence. None of this evidence was collected on a single creator.

### Cited Findings

**Speed and consistency**
- Figma (Clancy Slack, 19 December 2019; vendor study): designers completed two comparable tasks, one with a design system and one without, order alternated; those with the system finished 34% faster. The number of participants is not stated. The author expects 34% to be an upper bound because the system used was directly relevant and current. The article's worked example (7 designers, about 20 focused hours each, described as 212 hours of output) does not match a simple 34% uplift and should be read as a rough estimate. **[fetched]** — [Figma, Measuring the value of design systems](https://www.figma.com/blog/measuring-the-value-of-design-systems/)
- Sparkbox (agency study): eight developers coded a contact form from scratch and then again with IBM Carbon; median time 4.2 hours from scratch versus 2 hours with Carbon, reported as 47% faster; a separate group of eight rated accessibility and visual consistency. Limitations noted by commentators: tiny internal sample, and every developer did the scratch version first, so time spent understanding the design counts against the scratch condition. **[search summary]** — [Sparkbox, Design systems improve developer efficiency and design consistency](https://sparkbox.com/foundry/design_system_roi_impact_of_design_systems_business_value_carbon_design_system); critique at [CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/?p=355888)
- NN/g's stated benefits (expert opinion): faster and more consistent work at scale, freeing designers for harder problems, a shared language across functions, visual consistency across products, a teaching reference for juniors. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)

**Maintenance burden, adoption and documentation rot**
- Sparkbox 2022 (219 respondents: 183 maintainers, 37 subscribers), top maintainer challenges: overcoming technical or creative debt 43%; parity between design and code 37%; adoption 36%; staffing 35% (the largest gap between priority, 13%, and difficulty); expanding the system 34%. **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/)
- Same survey, subscribers: 65% say the system meets their needs; 39% say it is poorly documented; 35% cannot tell what is old, broken or coming soon; 19% say it is out of date; 19% were never trained on it. Metrics are tracked by only 16% (a second list on the same page shows 30%; the source is inconsistent). **[fetched]** — [Sparkbox 2022](https://designsystemssurvey.seesparkbox.com/2022/)
- zeroheight 2025 (vendor; 294 self-selected respondents): average system team size 3 people in companies under 100 staff, 4 at 100 to 499, 9 at 500 and above; 41% of smaller and 54% of larger companies say they do not have enough people; 68% document in more than one place; over half have little or no automation. Well-adopted systems report much higher trust (96%) and stability (90%) than others. **[fetched]** — [zeroheight Design Systems Report 2025](https://zeroheight.com/how-we-document)
- zeroheight's 2026 report is promoted with the findings that documentation is both the biggest pain point and the clearest opportunity and that adoption remains the existential challenge. **[search summary of promotional text; vendor]** — [zeroheight 2026 findings webinar](https://webinars.zeroheight.com/register/zeroheight-design-systems-report-2026-the-findings)
- NN/g's stated costs: building and maintaining a system is time-intensive and needs a dedicated team; teaching it takes time; it is unlikely to pay off for early or changing work. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Design Systems 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-101/)

**Constraints and creativity**
- Acar, Tarakci and van Knippenberg, Journal of Management 45(1), 2019, pp. 96 to 121: an integrative cross-disciplinary review that builds a taxonomy of constraints (such as rules and regulations, deadlines, scarce resources) and of the mechanisms through which they affect creativity and innovation, reconciling conflicting findings. **[search summary of abstract]** — [City Research Online record](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/20459/)
- The authors' own summary for managers (Harvard Business Review, November 2019) reports that they reviewed 145 empirical studies and found that individuals, teams and organisations benefit from a healthy dose of constraints, and that creativity is stifled only when constraints become too high. Secondary commentators describe this as an inverted U. **[search summary; the 145 figure and the inverted-U wording come from the HBR piece and commentary, not the abstract]** — [Why Constraints Are Good for Innovation (open-access record)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/23261/); [IEDP summary](https://iedp.com/articles/innovation-despite-constraints)

**Examples, templates and sameness**
- Design fixation (Jansson and Smith, Design Studies 12(1), 1991): designers shown a picture of an example solution reproduced its key features in their own designs, including deliberately flawed features and even when told to avoid them; a control group with no example showed less of this. **[search summary of later reviews]** — [TU Delft repository, 2017 Design Studies review](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_6a026941-2298-42fd-bf7a-dc08eaa73bd5); [Leahy et al., University of Limerick record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/design-fixation-from-initial-examples-provided-versus-self-genera/)
- Limits and mitigations: a replication found clear fixation only when the designers' discipline and the example matched the original experiment; a later experiment with 12 student teams found that teaching teams about fixation reduced fixated elements relative to control (p = 0.025). **[search summary]** — [Replication, Environment and Planning B](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.1068/b200333); [Howard et al., Overcoming design fixation through education and creativity methods](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/79504)
- Homogenisation of web design (Goree, Doosti, Crandall and Su, CHI 2021): computer-vision analysis of nearly 200,000 images from about 10,000 websites, 2003 to 2019, found designs became significantly more similar after 2007, with average layout distance between sites falling by over 30%; the authors point to shared source code and libraries, colour standardisation and mobile support as possible causes, supported by interviews with 11 web design professionals. **[search summary]** — [Goree et al., Investigating the Homogenization of Web Design](https://vision.soic.indiana.edu/papers/websimilarity2021chi.pdf); [project page](https://vision.soic.indiana.edu/web-project/)

**Checklists**
- The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist study (Haynes and colleagues, NEJM 2009): a 19-item checklist introduced at eight hospitals in eight cities; 7,688 patients (3,733 before, 3,955 after); major complications fell from 11% to 7% and inpatient deaths after major operations from 1.5% to 0.8%, with similar effect at high-income and lower-income sites. **[search summary]** — [Harvard Gazette, 2009](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2009/01/surgical-safety-checklist-drops-deaths-and-complications-by-more-than-one-third/)

**Practitioner opinion on systemising for one**
- A solo developer's framework (opinion, single practitioner): tokens from day one, patterns once something repeats three times, a formal system never for solo projects; the reasoning is that tokens are values rather than structure and so survive changes of direction. **[search summary]** — [Blake Crosley, Design Systems for Startups: How I Built Mine Backwards](https://blakecrosley.com/en/blog/design-systems-startups)
- Other practitioner essays (opinion): build the system when the cost of not having one is visible (people rebuilding the same things; a rebrand that is hard), and until then write the basics down and spend the time on the product; before product-market fit a design system can be wasted effort; a solo designer should keep scope to core elements and avoid over-engineering. **[search summary]** — [Design Systems Collective, You don't need a design system, you need design discipline](https://www.designsystemscollective.com/you-dont-need-a-design-system-you-need-design-discipline-72ed84854bed); [Lago, How soon should you have a design system?](https://getlago.com/blog/how-soon-should-you-have-a-design-system); [UX Collective, How to build a design system if you're the only designer in a startup](https://uxdesign.cc/how-to-build-a-design-system-if-youre-the-only-designer-in-a-startup-f4695d2f4b7f)

### Inferences
- The benefits in the evidence divide into two kinds. Coordination benefits (consistency across teams, shared language, onboarding) need more than one person and largely vanish for a solo creator. Repetition benefits (speed on a covered task, fewer decisions, a stable look over time) remain, but only for techniques that actually recur. That argues for systemising the handful of techniques used in most episodes and leaving the long tail as a reference shelf.
- The costs do not shrink with team size in the same way: debt, parity between the description and the implementation, and unclear status are all present for one person, and there is no second person to notice a stale entry.
- The creativity evidence points to form, not just amount. Constraints on the frame (palette, type, durations, a small default set) are the "healthy dose"; a library of finished example frames is the thing most likely to produce fixation and sameness. A catalogue that leads with the purpose and the rule, and keeps the example one click away, is the safer design. Rotating which example is shown, or keeping two contrasting ones, is a plausible mitigation given that awareness of fixation reduced it in one study.
- The checklist result is evidence for placing a short list at one high-stakes pipeline stage (for example a pre-render check), not for documenting everything. Its conditions (a fixed, short list; a moment where omissions are costly) are narrow.
- The Goree finding is a warning specific to template-driven video: when every picture comes from the same component set, convergence is the default outcome, so a deliberate allowance for one-off pictures per episode is a counterweight the evidence supports.
- Context from the project's own instructions, not from web research: the repository already has a large formal layer (multiple doctrine registers, lint rules, template selectors), and the owner's recorded preference is shipping episodes over more tooling cycles. On the evidence above, the marginal entry in a system that size carries mostly maintenance cost unless it is one of the frequently repeated techniques.

### Gaps
- No study of design-system return on a single practitioner, and no study of any visual system's effect on video or motion production speed, was found.
- Both speed studies are small, run by parties with an interest (a tool vendor and an agency that sells design-system work), and measure one task; neither measures quality or long-run maintenance cost.
- Acar et al.'s findings are reported from an abstract and secondary summaries; the turning point of the constraint curve, if the paper estimates one, was not retrieved.
- Jansson and Smith's original sample sizes and effect sizes were not retrieved.
- No direct evidence was found that checklists or templates reduce originality in creative media work; the sameness evidence is from web layouts.
- zeroheight's 2026 report figures were not read, only its promotional summary.

## 8. Minimal versions (lightest forms that still pay off for one person)

### Takeaway
The lightest proven forms are each a single artefact with a single job: a one-page purpose-keyed poster (FT), a two-function style package with a cookbook of worked examples (BBC), a four-ring radar, a one-to-two-page decision record, a tagged swipe file with a note on why each item was saved, and a short checklist at one stage. Each works because it is small enough to be kept current or needs no upkeep at all.

### Cited Findings
- One-page chooser: the FT Visual Vocabulary is a single poster of nine purpose categories with a sentence each and named options underneath, designed as the centre of a training session and as a prompt for when a chart helps alongside words. **[fetched]** — [FT Visual Vocabulary](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)
- Poster plus caveats: From Data to Viz pairs its decision-tree poster with a short list of common mistakes (12 cards on the front page) and puts "Common Mistakes" on every chart page. **[fetched]** — [From Data to Viz](https://www.data-to-viz.com/)
- Cookbook and defaults: BBC bbplot reduces a house style to two functions (apply the style; finish and export at fixed dimensions) and a cookbook of worked recipes; the repository has 13 commits in total. **[fetched]** — [bbc/bbplot](https://github.com/bbc/bbplot)
- Guide plus templates: Cesal describes the standard pairing as a guide for the what and why with templates in the working tools for the how, so applying the standard is easier than not. **[search summary]** — [Cesal, What Are Data Visualization Style Guidelines?](https://nightingaledvs.com/what-are-data-visualization-style-guidelines/)
- Radar: four rings on one page, republished on a fixed cadence, with items dropping off after one edition unless they move, and an explicit invitation to build your own. **[fetched]** — [Thoughtworks Technology Radar FAQ](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/faq)
- Decision record: five headings, one or two pages, numbered, never edited, superseded by a later record. **[fetched]** — [Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)
- Three-status label with a date: Experimental, Ready, Deprecated (Primer) with a last-updated date under the title (Carbon). **[search summary; fetched]** — [Primer status](https://primer.style/status); [Carbon components overview](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/overview/components/)
- Swipe file or reference library (practitioner advice, opinion): save only examples you genuinely like and add a short note on why each caught your attention; organise by recurring pattern or by type, or loosely by mood; use tags so one item can sit under several headings; browse it before starting new work. **[search summary]** — [Ridd, The ultimate design swipe file](https://ridd.substack.com/p/design-tactics-10-the-ultimate-design); [SitePoint, Swipe file for inspiration](https://www.sitepoint.com/swipe-file-for-inspiration/); [Out of Scope newsletter](https://out-of-scope-product.beehiiv.com/p/how-i-never-run-out-of-design-ideas-even-under-pressure)
- Contact sheet: the book Magnum Contact Sheets (edited by Kristen Lubben) reproduces 139 contact sheets from 69 photographers alongside the selected frames, showing the whole run of attempts next to the one chosen. **[search summary of publisher description]** — [Thames and Hudson, Magnum Contact Sheets](https://thamesandhudson.com.au/?p=70319)
- Card deck: the narrative-patterns project publishes its patterns as cards, one technique per card, for inspiration and structure, and notes they can be combined. **[fetched]** — [NAPA cards](http://napa-cards.net/)
- Checklist at one stage: a 19-item list at defined pause points produced the outcome changes reported in section 7. **[search summary]** — [Harvard Gazette, 2009](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2009/01/surgical-safety-checklist-drops-deaths-and-complications-by-more-than-one-third/)
- Tokens first (opinion, single practitioner): fix values on day one, promote a pattern only after three repeats, and never build a formal system for a solo project. **[search summary]** — [Blake Crosley, Design Systems for Startups](https://blakecrosley.com/en/blog/design-systems-startups)

### Inferences
- A minimal stack assembled from the sources, in rising cost: (1) a one-page grid, sentence job by one discriminator, with one to three technique names per cell; (2) a radar of the 70 techniques in four rings, which doubles as the status field; (3) the existing test reels kept as a contact sheet, one frame per technique with its name, so the full run of attempts stays visible next to the chosen defaults; (4) full entries (when to use, when not to use, decide-between, known issues) only for techniques in the innermost ring; (5) a dated one-page decision note whenever something is promoted or retired.
- What makes each minimal form survive is that it is either generated from the work (defaults in code; frames pulled from reels) or small enough to rewrite in one sitting (a poster; a radar). Prose entries for all 70 techniques have neither property.
- The contact sheet has a property the other forms lack: it preserves rejected and unpromoted work without endorsing it, which answers the fear that not systemising something means losing it.
- A swipe file's "why I saved this" note and a pattern entry's rationale are the same thing at different costs; writing the one-line why at the time of exploration is the cheapest moment to capture it.
- Promotion from the shelf to a full entry can be tied to the rule of three from section 6, so that documentation effort follows actual use.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found on which minimal form gives the most benefit for one person; the list is assembled from examples built for newsrooms and engineering organisations, and from practitioner opinion.
- "Cookbook" as a named practice in motion or video work was not sourced; the only cookbook examined is the BBC's for charts in R, and its body could not be retrieved (only the package README was read).
- Contact-sheet practice is sourced from a publisher's description of one book, not from a method description.
- Reference-library advice comes from newsletters and a tool vendor, not from research.
