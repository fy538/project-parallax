# Motion, depth and drawn overlays on a single still illustration

Scope: three technique families (motion inside a still; depth and travel; graphics layered on a picture) for flat, stylised, non-photoreal artwork rendered in Remotion at 1920x1080, 30 fps. Research date 2026-10-11.

Source markers: **[F]** = page opened and read; **[S]** = taken from a search-engine excerpt, page not opened (where the excerpt's originating page was ambiguous, the candidates are listed). Dates are publication dates where the page showed one; Wikipedia, MDN and Remotion docs are living pages accessed 2026-10-11. Statements marked *opinion* are one practitioner's view, not a standard.

Wording note: sources are paraphrased throughout. Names in quotation marks are labels, titles or tool settings, not quotations. One short verbatim quotation is kept (Ken Burns, section 3). Check the source page before quoting anyone in a script.

---

## 1. What is the correct name for each technique and its variants, and which names are tool-brand terms rather than craft terms?

### Takeaway
Most of these techniques have one neutral craft name (line boil, cut-out animation, 2.5D parallax, rostrum pan-and-zoom, annotation layer) and several nicknames that are really product, feature, film or studio names (Ken Burns Effect, Squigglevision, Plotagraph, Puppet tool, Content-Aware Fill, Duik, the Kid Stays in the Picture effect, telestrator). The notes below give a definition and the conventional hand-made method for each, so the report can use the craft term and mention the brand term once.

### Cited Findings

**Family 1: motion inside a still**

- **Cinemagraph.** A still photograph in which one small movement repeats, published as a GIF or video. Made by shooting a series of photos or a video and compositing the frames into a seamless loop so that one part moves against a frozen background. The word was coined by US photographers Kevin Burg and Jamie Beck, who began using the technique in early 2011 (first example: model Coco Rocha, New York Fashion Week). [F] — [Wikipedia, Cinemagraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinemagraph)
- Cinemagraph, hand method: draw a mask around the area that should move; the moving clip generally sits as the base layer with the frozen still frame on top and the mask cutting a hole through it. [S] — [ACMI, How to make a cinemagraph](https://www.acmi.net.au/education/school-program-and-resources/how-to-make-a-cinemagraph/)
- Cinemagraph, Photoshop method: trim the clip to the stretch containing the motion, play it to check the loop, then pick the frame that becomes the frozen still. [S] — [Boris FX, How to make a cinemagraph in Photoshop](https://borisfx.com/blog/how-to-make-a-cinemagraph-in-photoshop/)
- **Plotagraph** is a product (Plotagraph Pro), not a craft term. It animates a single still with no video source: the user places animation points or arrows showing which way pixels should flow (a longer arrow moves faster) and anchor or stabiliser points on pixels that must stay put; masks are optional. Reviewers' advice (*opinion*): fewer animation points look better, and too many stabiliser points look unnatural. [S] — [Photofocus, drifting clouds with Plotagraph Pro](https://photofocus.com/software/creating-drifting-clouds-in-a-single-image-with-plotagraph-pro/); [Stuck in Customs, Plotagraph review](https://stuckincustoms.com/plotagraph-review); [Improve Photography](https://improvephotography.com/49221/plotagraph-future-or-fleeting-trend/)
- **Flixel Cinemagraph Pro** is likewise a product; its loop vocabulary (bounce versus repeat, crossfade) is widely borrowed. [S] — [Flixel blog, Trim, Loop and Crossfade](https://blog.flixel.com/trim-loop-crossfade-flixel-cinemagraph-pro-mac/)
- **Line boil** (also boil, wiggle). PremiumBeat defines it as the visible shimmer that appears when successive frames of an animation do not line up exactly; it is typical of hand-drawn or painted animation and can be added in After Effects for a handmade feel (Charles Yeager, 23 Dec 2020). [F] — [PremiumBeat, line boil](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/wiggle-text-line-boil/)
- **Squigglevision** is a named studio method, not the generic term. Invented by Tom Snyder (Tom Snyder Productions / Soup2Nuts): animators loop five slightly different drawings, called a "flic", in Autodesk Animator for MS-DOS. Used on *Dr. Katz, Professional Therapist* (1995), *Science Court*, the first season of *Home Movies* and *O'Grady*. [F] — [Wikipedia, Squigglevision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squigglevision)
- Snyder's own account: an illustrator drew the outline once and the computer redrew it five times with randomness added; it was cheap because no real animation was involved, and characters in those shows do not walk or reach for things. [S] — [Mental Floss, Creatively Speaking: Tom Snyder](https://mentalfloss.com/article/18336/creatively-speaking-tom-snyder)
- "On ones / twos / threes" is the craft vocabulary for how long each drawing is held: at 24 fps, on twos means a new drawing every other frame (12 per second) and on threes means 8 new drawings per second. [S] — [iD Tech, animating on ones, twos and threes](https://www.idtech.com/blog/what-does-animating-on-ones-twos-and-threes-mean)
- **Puppet tool / puppet pins** is Adobe's feature name for pin-driven mesh warping. Placing the first pin divides the area inside the layer's outline into a mesh of triangles tied to the image pixels. Three pin types: Deform pins move parts, Overlap pins say which part sits in front when the warp folds over itself, Starch pins stiffen areas so they distort less. Pin positions keyframe automatically and can be driven by expressions. [S] — [Adobe Help, Animating with Puppet tools](https://helpx.adobe.com/ca/after-effects/desktop/animate-in-after-effects/animate-with-puppet-tools/animating-puppet-tools.html)
- Typical puppet-pin use on a still is small organic motion such as a plant swaying. [S] — [Pixel Bakery, Puppet tool basics](https://pixelbakery.com/education/tutorials/puppet-tool-basics)
- **Turbulent Displace** (After Effects effect) and **feTurbulence / feDisplacementMap** (SVG filter primitives) are tool names for the same craft idea, noise displacement. MDN describes feTurbulence as generating an image from the Perlin turbulence function, for synthetic textures such as clouds or marble. [F] — [MDN, feTurbulence](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG/Reference/Element/feTurbulence)

**Family 2: depth and travel**

- **Ken Burns effect** is a feature name that became the popular label. The technique is panning and zooming over still images, and it predates Burns. "Ken Burns Effect" was Apple's working title for iMovie's pan-and-zoom feature, and Steve Jobs asked Burns's permission to ship it under that name. Earlier uses: the NFB's *City of Gold* (1957) and NBC's *Meet Mr. Lincoln* (1959); NBC used the label "stills in motion" by the early 1960s; a 1961 letter by Louis Clyde Stoumen listed uses back to 1936. Most software calls it pan and zoom; the physical device is the rostrum camera. Burns credits Jerome Liebling with teaching him to use stills. Related Wikipedia entries: photoanimation, still image film, multiplane camera. [F] — [Wikipedia, Ken Burns effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Burns_effect)
- Dates for the Apple naming: Jobs phoned in November 2002, the demonstration meeting was December 2002, and the feature shipped on Macs from January 2003. [F] — [CineD, Nino Leitner, 9 Dec 2025](https://www.cined.com/?p=406363)
- **Multiplane camera** is the hardware ancestor of parallax. Timeline: Lotte Reiniger's 1923 design used several layers of glass with the camera fixed above; used with Carl Koch on *The Adventures of Prince Achmed* (1926); Berthold Bartosch used a similar set-up for *L'Idée* (1932); Ub Iwerks built one in 1933 partly from old Chevrolet parts; Fleischer's 1934 Stereoptical Camera, or Setback, used miniature 3D sets; William Garity's Disney camera was finished in early 1937 and tested on *The Old Mill*; Disney's version used up to seven layers of oil-painted glass under a vertical camera and could need up to a dozen technicians; *The Little Mermaid* (1989) was the last Disney film to use one; Disney's CAPS system then offered a digital multiplane feature. Planes further from the camera move more slowly; moving foreground and background in opposite directions gives a rotation effect (the Evil Queen's potion scene in *Snow White*). [F] — [Wikipedia, Multiplane camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplane_camera)
- Reiniger's fixed-camera set-up gave separation between layers but not true parallax. [S] (originating page among the search results not individually verified) — [Wikipedia, Lotte Reiniger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotte_Reiniger); [Animation School Daily, Multiplane cameras](https://animationschooldaily.com/multiplane-cameras/)
- **2.5D parallax** (also parallax animation, photo animation, the Kid Stays in the Picture effect). ProVideo Coalition names *The Kid Stays in the Picture* as the first well-known showcase of the effect (Iain Anderson, 24 Oct 2022). [F] — [ProVideo Coalition, Animating parallax images](https://www.provideocoalition.com/animating-parallax-images-in-after-effects-and-apple-motion/)
- The film's own account: directors Brett Morgen and Nanette Burstein tested scanned stills in After Effects and found they could split a photograph into spatial planes and move a camera through it; each photograph and piece of text was treated like a set to be blocked; a rack focus blurs the background as the camera approaches the subject (Kathy McDonald, 1 Jun 2002). [F] — [Documentary.org / IDA](https://www.documentary.org/feature/kid-stays-picture-robert-evans-world-and-welcome-it)
- Joe Fellows's widely shared tutorial describes layering stills in imaging software and names the film as the effect's nickname. [S] — [Vice Creators Project](https://thecreatorsproject.vice.com/blog/joe-fellows-teaches-us-how-to-master-25D-photography); [Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/how-to-create-motion-in-still-photographs-using-the-parallax-effect/)
- Hand method (Photoshop, then After Effects): cut each plane to its own layer (Select Subject or pen tool, then refine the mask); make each layer overlap the one behind it; fill the hole behind each cut with Spot Healing (which uses Content-Aware Fill), the Healing Brush or Clone Stamp; import the PSD as a composition; switch the layers to 3D; push them apart in Z; scale distant layers back up to fill the frame; add a camera and keyframe a small move; optionally keyframe focus distance. [F] — [ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/animating-parallax-images-in-after-effects-and-apple-motion/)
- Same method, second source: pen-tool selection with feather 0.5 to 1; cut and paste to a new layer; expand the hole selection by 10 to 15 before Edit > Fill > Content-Aware; a 28 mm camera; slow position keyframes; for people and animals add Puppet pins (page dated 09/02/2021, no author shown). [F] — [Motion Array, Animate flat 2D images (2.5D parallax)](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/after-effects/animate-flat-2d-images-after-effects/)
- **3D Ken Burns effect** is the title of an Adobe Research paper (Niklaus et al., 2019), so it is a research name for depth-estimated parallax: a network estimates depth, a virtual camera moves through the resulting point cloud, and colour-and-depth inpainting fills the areas the move uncovers. [S] — [arXiv 1909.05483](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.05483v1); [Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/3d-ken-burns-effect-from-a-single-image)
- **Content-Aware Fill** is Adobe's feature name; the craft terms are inpainting, paint-out and clean plate. Research usage: the occluded regions of each layer are inpainted before the layers are used for parallax. [F] — [Illustrator's Depth, arXiv 2511.17454](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17454v1)
- **LeiaPix / Immersity AI** is a product for depth-map animation; its controls include edge dilation, a depth picker and painter, and a levelling tool. [S] — [Immersity (LeiaPix) tutorial](https://leiapix.com/tutorial/how-to-use-leiapix-features)
- **Scrollytelling** is the data-journalism craft term for a format where visual and text elements appear or change as the reader scrolls. An Information Design Journal paper by the NZZ graphics team analysed 50 examples and named five standard techniques: graphic sequences, animated transitions, panning and zooming, scrolling through movies, and showing or auto-playing animated content. The format's origin is usually traced to the New York Times's "Snow Fall" (2012). [S] — [Oesch et al., Information Design Journal](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/idj.22005.oes); [EU data visualisation guide, scrollytelling introduction](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-introduction)
- Scrollytelling layouts: either the graphic fills the window and text scrolls over it, or the graphic sits to one side with text beside it. Text blocks map to graphic states one-to-one (the usual case), many-to-one or one-to-many. Scrolling can be flipped so the story travels horizontally (a Bloomberg piece is the example given). [F] — [EU data visualisation guide, scrollytelling design variations](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/scrollytelling-design-variations)
- **Droste effect / infinite zoom.** Works because the frame contains a smaller copy of itself. The size ratio of the small copy to the whole fixes the magnification (and any rotation) needed to arrive at an identical frame, so a limited number of frames plays as an endless zoom when looped. [S] — [Jos Leys, Mathematical Imagery](https://www.josleys.com/article_show.php?id=82)
- Infinite zoom, After Effects method: parent the footage to precomps and scale them so each level hands over to the next. [S] — [Envato Tuts+, Endlessly zoom into your own Droste effect](https://photography.tutsplus.com/tutorials/endlessly-zoom-into-your-own-droste-effect--ae-12897)

**Family 3: graphics and animation layered on a picture**

- **Cut-out animation** (cutout animation). A form of stop-motion using flat characters, props and backgrounds cut from paper, card, fabric or photographs. Joints are made with a rivet or pin; on a computer the joint is an anchor point. Sub-variants: **silhouette animation** (Reiniger) and the **collage or photomontage** style built from ready-made imagery (Gilliam). History: Quirino Cristiani's *El Apóstol* (1917); Reiniger's shorts from 1917 and *Prince Achmed* (1926); Soyuzmultfilm features (1964, 1966); Gilliam for *Monty Python's Flying Circus* (1969); the *South Park* pilot in paper, then PowerAnimator and Maya; *Blue's Clues* (1996 to 2006); *Angela Anaconda* (1999 to 2002, photographed faces on computer-made bodies); *Charlie and Lola* (2005 to 2008, Flash with paper, fabric and photomontage). [F] — [Wikipedia, Cutout animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutout_animation)
- Reiniger's own method (Sight and Sound, Spring 1936; republished online 1 May 2020): figures are cut from black cardboard and thin lead, each limb cut separately and joined with wire hinges; figures and backgrounds lie on a glass table lit strongly from below, which makes the hinges vanish; the camera hangs overhead and the film advances one frame at a time, with the figures nudged between exposures; backgrounds are cut from layers of transparent paper; she names judging how far to move the figures as the important skill; shots are counted out against the measured soundtrack. [F] — [BFI, Scissors make films](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/scissors-make-films-lotte-reiniger-creating-her-magical-animations)
- Reiniger: the more a character had to do in the story, the more hinges it was given; some figures were made in several sizes for close and distant shots; in her later colour work the joints were deliberately concealed. [S] — [BFI, Reiniger's cut-out figures for Cinderella](https://www.bfi.org.uk/inside-the-archive/features/object-week-lotte-reiniger-cut-out-figures-backgrounds-cinderella)
- Gilliam's method, as shown in 1974 on Bob Godfrey's do-it-yourself animation programme: a glass sheet is laid over the cut-outs, and they are moved along a scale under the camera to keep the action in shot; he worked alone and supplied most voices and sound effects himself (write-up dated 3 Jan 2014). [F] — [Reactor](https://reactormag.com/terry-gilliam-monty-python-animation/); programme identified in [Laughing Squid, 12 Nov 2020](https://laughingsquid.com/terry-gilliam-explains-monty-python-cutout-animation/) [F]
- Modern rigged cut-out tool names: **Joysticks 'n Sliders** is a pose-based rigging script for After Effects (a joystick is built from five consecutive keyframes: origin, right, left, top, bottom; sliders accept any number of poses and suit hands, eyes and mouths). **Duik** supplies bones, inverse kinematics and controllers. School of Motion's review (Morgan Williams) compares them on simple 2.5D face rigs with a head turn, eye aim, smile and frown, and blink. [S] — [aescripts, Joysticks 'n Sliders](https://aescripts.com/joystick-n-sliders); [School of Motion review](https://schoolofmotion.com/blog/after-effects-tool-review-joysticks-n-sliders-vs-duik-bassel)
- **Annotation layer** is the newsroom craft term. Amanda Cox, then a New York Times graphics editor, is quoted (second-hand) as calling it the most important thing the desk does, because without it the reader is handed a chart and left to work it out alone. [F] — [EU data visualisation guide, visual annotations](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-annotations-introduction)
- Cox in a 2012 interview: the words on a graphic should point to the pattern that matters rather than merely announce that data is present. Notes from a 2015 Columbia talk describe annotation as ideally minimal: pointers, outlines or brief notes laid over a graphic, whether image or video. [S] — [Simply Statistics, 1 Jun 2012](https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/); [Ambriente blog, 24 Feb 2015](https://www.ambriente.com/blog/?p=889)
- Annotation anatomy and type names (d3-annotation, Susie Lu): Label, Callout, Elbow callout, Curve callout, Circle callout, Rect callout, XY Threshold, Badge. Connector types: line, elbow, curve. Connector ends: arrow, dot, none. A note has an optional rule line and alignment settings. [F] — [d3-annotation](https://d3-annotation.susielu.com/)
- **Telestrator** began as a specific device and is now a generic verb (telestration). Developed by physicist Leonard Reiffel for science programmes on WTTW Chicago in the late 1950s; spread through weather and sports broadcasting; John Madden's use at Super Bowl XVI (1982) made it famous; Reiffel received a technical Emmy in 2004. [S] — [Wikipedia, Telestrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telestrator); [Boston Globe, 28 Sep 2014](https://www.bostonglobe.com/sports/2014/09/28/father-telestrator-drew-right/O6Pn3rgtZd9XykOYvFQ8dM/story.html)
- **Isotype** is a named system (the name dates from about 1935; before that, the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics). Otto Neurath was the theorist; Gerd Arntz, recruited in 1926, drew the pictograms, which were cut in linoleum and printed in multiples for pasting into charts. First rule: a larger quantity is shown by repeating a same-sized pictogram, not by enlarging one. Pictograms almost never use perspective. The canonical statement is Neurath's *International Picture Language* (1936). [S] — [Wikipedia, Isotype (picture language)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language))
- The EU guide files icons and images as one of three categories of visual annotation, alongside highlights and aggregation or distribution marks. [F] — [EU data visualisation guide, visual annotations](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-annotations-introduction)
- **Integrated typography (type in the scene).** For *Panic Room* (2002), David Fincher's question was whether the credits should look projected onto the shot or look physically present in it. ComputerCafe and The Picture Mill (designer William Lebeda) spent over a year on the sequence; the white letters cast shadows and reflections on the buildings. [S] — [Art of the Title, Panic Room](https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/panic-room/)
- In *Catch Me If You Can* (2002) the credit lettering is worked into the design of each small scene (essay dated 24 Aug 2012). [S] — [Cooper Hewitt, Never fast forward the credits](https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2012/08/24/never-fast-forward-the-credits/)
- Academic term for on-picture text that sits inside the composition: integrated subtitles or creative subtitles, which vary placement, typeface, colour, size and timing. [S] — [Media for All paper](https://mapaccess.uab.cat/node/2654)
- An early case of type inside the dramatic action is *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* (1919). [S] — [cinegraphic.net, 23 Apr 2011](https://cinegraphic.net/article.php/20110423074339896)
- **Mixed-media animation** is the trade name for combining materials and techniques in one piece (live action, 3D, watercolour, paper cut-outs, claymation). The same source says it is sometimes called collage animation and lists photomontage animation as a sub-type. The source is a studio's marketing explainer. [S] — [FEVR, What is a video collage (or mixed media animation)?](https://wearefevr.com/what-is-video-collage-mixed-media-animation)
- The Vox-style paper-collage look is described by third parties (not by Vox) as hand-cut paper shapes, torn edges, tape, halftone dots, newspaper clippings, one flat colour per beat and large cut-out headlines. One such pipeline notes that the look is established in the still image and the motion is a separate, later step. Low-authority sources. [S] — [vox-director repository](https://github.com/Alisa0808/vox-director); [videoai.me explainer](https://videoai.me/blog/what-is-vox-style-video-explained-2026)
- Kurzgesagt describes its own style as vector illustration, pixel-based illustration and infographics, animated as flat 2D with 3D elements where needed. [S] — [Kurzgesagt, Our video process](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)

### Inferences
- Suggested house vocabulary for the report (craft term first, brand term in brackets once): masked ambient loop (cinemagraph); noise displacement (Turbulent Displace, feTurbulence); line boil (Squigglevision); mesh warp (Puppet tool); rostrum move or pan-and-zoom (Ken Burns effect); layered parallax or 2.5D (multiplane, the Kid Stays in the Picture effect); depth-map parallax (3D photo, 3D Ken Burns, LeiaPix/Immersity); paint-out or clean plate (Content-Aware Fill); cut-out rig (Duik, Joysticks 'n Sliders); annotation layer or callouts (telestrator); pictogram units (Isotype); integrated type versus overlay type.
- Cinemagraph and Squigglevision sit in a grey zone: each was coined by identifiable people for their own work, so they read as proper names even where used generically. Line boil and masked loop are the safe generic terms.
- Parallax names the optical result and multiplane names the rig; in a code pipeline they are the same thing (separate layers translated by different amounts).

### Gaps
- Trademark or patent status of the words cinemagraph and Squigglevision was not stated on the pages read; do not assert either.
- Camera projection, or camera mapping (projecting the still onto simple 3D geometry), and CapCut's 3D zoom were named in the brief, but no source was found in this pass; they are not defined here from evidence.
- No authoritative naming source was found for the styles of Polyphonic, Nerdwriter or Patrick Willems, and no first-party Vox description of its collage style.
- Tableau pan and scroll-painting move did not turn up as established motion-design terms; the nearest documented craft terms are scrollytelling's panning and zooming, and the museums' painting animation.

## 2. What numbers do practitioners give?

### Takeaway
Hard numbers exist for a few things: boil rate and displacement amount, Z-spacing in an After Effects parallax rig, selection feather and expansion for cut-and-fill, Squigglevision's five-drawing loop, the multiplane's seven planes, and typical pan-and-zoom scale ranges. For the two questions the brief most wanted answered, percent of parallax per layer and a Ken Burns speed rule, practitioners mostly say to keep it subtle and give no figure; the numbers below are worked examples, not standards.

### Inferences
- **Converted to this channel's 30 fps.** Holding each boil variant for 2, 3 or 4 frames gives 15, 10 or 7.5 changes per second. PremiumBeat's band of 4 to 12 changes per second corresponds to holds of roughly 2.5 to 7.5 frames at 30 fps. A 3- or 4-frame hold with a cycle of 3 to 5 variants sits inside every sourced figure.
- **What the ProVideo rig implies as a ratio.** Camera distance to each layer goes from 3100 / 3600 / 4100 / 4600 px at the start to 1200 / 1700 / 2200 / 2700 px at the end. Apparent growth is therefore about 2.58x for the nearest layer, then 2.12x, 1.86x, and 1.70x for the sky: the foreground grows roughly 1.5 times as much as the background. That is a showy demonstration push; the camera's sideways travel in the same move is only 40 px in each of X and Y.
- **A usable parallax formula for code.** From Clark's correction formula, a layer placed a depth *d* behind the reference plane under perspective *P* moves at `P / (P + d)` of the reference layer's speed. With P = 2000 and layers at d = 0, 500, 1000, 1500, the factors are 1.00, 0.80, 0.67, 0.57. Taken with the 1:3 community example, a defensible starting band is that the farthest layer moves about one third to two thirds as far as the nearest.
- **Pan-and-zoom rate.** The sourced scale ranges (2% at the subtle end, 10 to 20% typical, 30% at the top) spread over the sourced 5-second minimum give roughly 0.4% to 6% of scale change per second, with 2% per second (100% to 110% in 5 s) in the middle. On a 1920 px frame, a 10% scale change about the centre moves an edge pixel 96 px.
- **Resolution headroom.** The forum's 5x on a 4,000 px still was for standard-definition output. For 1080p the same arithmetic says a still should be at least (zoom factor x 1920) px wide at its tightest framing: a 1.2x push needs 2304 px, and a 2x zoom-to-detail needs 3840 px.

### Gaps
- No source gave a percent-of-parallax-per-layer standard or a pixel budget; every tutorial read says to keep it subtle and that it depends on the image.
- No source gave a seconds-per-photo rule or a maximum duration before a move reads as drift. Burns's 33-second zoom shows that long moves can work; it is not a rule.
- Boil "on fours" was not found in a source; only twos and threes were sourced, plus PremiumBeat's 4 and 12 changes per second.
- No source quantified how different successive boil drawings should be, beyond the After Effects displacement Amount of 5 to 10.
- No sourced loop length for cinemagraphs as delivered (only recording length), and no sourced crossfade duration.
- No sourced numbers for cut-out joint rotation ranges, annotation line weights, or how far to dim the rest of the frame in a spotlight.

## 3. Which techniques are safe on flat colour-block art and which depend on photographic depth or texture and tend to fail on it?

### Takeaway
Anything that treats the picture as authored flat shapes (cut-out, layered parallax with hand-made layers, line boil, annotation, pictogram nodes, integrated type, pan-and-zoom, panorama travel) is native to flat art, because those techniques were invented on flat art. Anything that asks software to infer depth or motion from the pixels (depth-map 3D-photo tools, parallax that relies on texture inside a layer, content-aware fill over large areas, video-sourced cinemagraphs) is where flat illustration goes wrong.

### Cited Findings
- Depth estimators are structurally wrong for illustration. Adobe Research (Maruani et al., 21 Nov 2025) reports that monocular depth models cannot see content that has no real volume, such as a printed poster or a pattern on clothing, that they are in effect trained to ignore flat media, and that they struggle to recover the correct layer order in illustrations (a drawn shadow, for example, sits above the object it falls on in the artist's layer stack). The paper proposes predicting a discrete layer index, which it calls illustrator's depth, in place of physical depth, giving piecewise-flat regions; once binned and inpainted, those layers can be used directly for parallax. [F] — [Illustrator's Depth, arXiv 2511.17454](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17454v1)
- Comics: depth models trained on natural images fail on comics, producing vague, overlapping or missing objects; lettering creates false depth edges and had to be handled separately (WACV 2022). [S] — [Bhattacharjee et al., Estimating Image Depth in the Comics Domain](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2022/papers/Bhattacharjee_Estimating_Image_Depth_in_the_Comics_Domain_WACV_2022_paper.pdf)
- Paintings: a 2025 evaluation of zero-shot depth models on paintings found that none produced output usable without correction. [S] — [Eurographics Digital Library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/dh20253048)
- Highly abstract styles (ink wash is the example given) are reported to be predicted as flat planes by current depth models. [S] (attribution to this specific paper not verified) — [arXiv 2606.12368](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.12368)
- The characteristic artefact of depth-map animation is stretching at strong depth transitions. LeiaPix/Immersity's edge-dilation control exists to counter it; without it, foreground colour bleeds along the edge into the background and the reverse. The same tool offers manual depth painting and levelling. [S] — [Immersity (LeiaPix) tutorial](https://leiapix.com/tutorial/how-to-use-leiapix-features)
- Even on photographs, one reviewer of the released 3D Ken Burns code found the result slightly uncanny and likened it to a View-Master (*opinion*, 16 Sep 2019). [S] — [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2019/09/16/3d-ken-burns-effect-on-standar.html)
- Hand-made photo parallax also assumes texture. ProVideo's image-selection rule is to avoid flat colour, because each layer needs texture for its movement to show; also avoid transparency such as fly-away hair or glass, and make each layer overlap the one behind or gaps will show. [F] — [ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/animating-parallax-images-in-after-effects-and-apple-motion/)
- Photo parallax tutorials say success depends above all on the image chosen, and that blurry backgrounds are easiest because the gaps behind moved layers are simpler to fill. [F] — [Motion Array](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/after-effects/animate-flat-2d-images-after-effects/)
- Cinemagraphs proper need video: even subjects with repeating motion tend to deform or drift within the frame, so a seamless loop is not trivial; the start and end must be matched or the crossfade ghosts. [S] — [NTNU, automatic cinemagraphs (ACM MM 2012)](https://web.ntnu.edu.tw/~myeh/papers/mm12-short.pdf); [Flixel blog](https://blog.flixel.com/trim-loop-crossfade-flixel-cinemagraph-pro-mac/)
- Loop type must match the motion: bounce (ping-pong) loops suit back-and-forth motion (trees, hair, garments); repeat loops suit one-directional motion (water, flames, smoke); a bounce loop on directional motion visibly runs backwards. [S] — [Flixel support, Creating a perfect loop](https://support.flixel.com/articles/creating-a-perfect-loop-in-cinemagraph-pro) (returned 403 on direct fetch); [Flixel blog](https://blog.flixel.com/trim-loop-crossfade-flixel-cinemagraph-pro-mac/)
- Animating feTurbulence through its `baseFrequency` changes the density of the noise; it suggests motion but gives no direction, so it does not read as flow or wind (W3C SVG mailing list, Nov 2011). [S] — [www-svg mailing list](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-svg/2011Nov/0087.html)
- Line boil comes from flat drawn art, and the advice is to keep it small: lower displacement values are recommended for animation. [F] — [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/wiggle-text-line-boil/)
- Squigglevision's premise is that boil stands in for movement; its inventor frames this as economy and claims it has almost no drawbacks (*inventor's opinion*). [F] — [Wikipedia, Squigglevision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squigglevision)
- Cut-out is native to flat art and its joints are hidden by design: Reiniger under-lit black figures so the wire hinges disappeared, and concealed the joints in her later colour figures. [F]/[S] — [BFI, Scissors make films](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/scissors-make-films-lotte-reiniger-creating-her-magical-animations); [BFI, Cinderella figures](https://www.bfi.org.uk/inside-the-archive/features/object-week-lotte-reiniger-cut-out-figures-backgrounds-cinderella)
- Isotype pictograms almost never use perspective and stay one size, which is the same flat idiom as the channel's artwork. [S] — [Wikipedia, Isotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language))
- Automated pan-and-zoom fails editorially, not technically. Burns regards the consumer feature as a weak imitation of his team's craft and calls an unmotivated move "a zoom to nowhere", one that drifts toward the wrong part of the picture. [F] — [CineD](https://www.cined.com/?p=406363)
- Digital zoom on low-resolution sources makes edges crawl (aliasing), and upsampling small scans adds nothing. [F] — [DV Info forum, Dec 2003](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-18106.html)
- Puppet warps need Starch pins to keep rigid areas from bending and Overlap pins where parts cross. [S] — [Adobe Help](https://helpx.adobe.com/ca/after-effects/desktop/animate-in-after-effects/animate-with-puppet-tools/animating-puppet-tools.html)

### Inferences
Ratings for flat, constructivist colour-block art with faceless figures (my judgement, reasoned from the findings above):

| Technique | Rating on flat art | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rostrum pan-and-zoom | Safe | No dependence on texture. Risks are aliasing on hard edges at very slow speeds, and the unmotivated move. |
| Layered parallax with authored layers | Safe, and easier than on photographs | A flat field behind a figure is trivial to fill (extend the colour block), which removes the hardest photographic step. Depth has to come from overlap and edges, because ProVideo's point holds: inside a flat field nothing visibly moves. Needs strong silhouettes that cross layer boundaries. |
| Depth-map 3D-photo parallax | Avoid | The estimator sees a poster. Expect near-flat depth, wrong layer order, and stretched or bleeding edges at exactly the crisp outlines flat art is made of. |
| Layer-index decomposition (research) | Promising, not yet a tool | The Adobe paper targets exactly this case, but it is a paper, not something to depend on. |
| Line boil | Safe in small doses | It works on edges, which flat art has in abundance. Risk: with large flat blocks the whole silhouette shimmers, and after YouTube compression on a phone a fine boil may read as codec noise. Better on one hero element than on the full frame. |
| Procedural region motion (gradient sweep, glow pulse, flicker) | Safe | These are shape-and-colour operations and match the idiom. |
| Noise displacement for water or smoke | Conditional | Displacing a flat field shows nothing; only edges and internal stripes move. It needs drawn bands, highlights or an added gradient to push around. Animating `baseFrequency` makes the surface breathe; it does not flow. |
| Video-sourced cinemagraph | Not available without a clip | It would need a generated or filmed clip of the still. If one exists, the mask-and-freeze has a real benefit: the frozen layer hides any drift everywhere outside the masked region. |
| Mesh or puppet warp | Risky | Warping bends straight constructivist edges and geometric shapes into rubber. Acceptable for cloth, flags, smoke and foliage; wrong for buildings, type and figures built from rigid blocks. |
| Cut-out rig | Safe, native | Faceless block figures split cleanly at the joints; a few degrees of rotation per part reads as intent. |
| Annotation layer, spotlight, x-ray | Safe | Vector marks over vector-like art. The risk is contrast: saturated flat blocks compete with thin leader lines, and dimming the rest of the frame solves that. |
| Pictogram nodes in diagrams | Safe, native | The same visual language as Isotype. |
| Integrated type | Safe | There is no lighting or grain to match, so a perspective transform and a multiply blend are usually enough; photographic integration needs tracking, shadows and reflections. |
| Panorama travel | Safe | Depends on composition, not texture. |
| Zoom-through and infinite zoom | Safe technically | Needs artwork drawn with a portal (window, doorway, screen) and enough resolution at the portal. |

- Flat art is better suited than photographs to layered parallax and cut-out for a historical reason: the multiplane camera, Reiniger's silhouettes and Gilliam's cut-outs were all flat painted or cut artwork. Photo parallax is the later and harder adaptation.
- The strongest single recommendation that follows: do not derive layers from a finished flat picture; author them as layers (a background plate without figures, a mid-ground, the figures, a foreground). This is the alternative of authoring layers separately that the brief asks about. An AI image tool can supply the clean plate or the isolated figure, but the layer split is an authoring decision.

### Gaps
- No source tested LeiaPix/Immersity, CapCut 3D zoom or Depth Anything specifically on flat vector-style illustration; the failure claim rests on the research papers above and on the tool's own artefact documentation.
- No source discusses line boil or cut-out specifically on constructivist colour-block art, or how either survives phone viewing and YouTube compression.
- No source on perceptual thresholds: how small a boil or a parallax shift can be before it disappears on a phone screen.

## 4. Which can be done deterministically in code without any AI video model, and roughly how?

### Takeaway
Nearly everything in the brief is deterministic in Remotion, because the underlying operations are transforms, masks, blend modes, SVG strokes and seeded noise. The two real constraints are Remotion's rule that every animated value must be a pure function of the frame number (no CSS animations, no unseeded randomness), and that true mesh warps need WebGL while video-sourced cinemagraphs need a source clip.

### Cited Findings
- Remotion's rule: animation that runs independently of `useCurrentFrame()` breaks at render time, so all animation should be computed purely from that value. A component must show the same picture every time it is asked for a given frame, must not depend on frames being rendered in order, and must not use randomness other than Remotion's seeded `random()`. The page links a separate note against CSS animations, and its asset-loading section advises against the `background-image` and `mask-image` CSS properties. [F] — [Remotion docs, Flickering](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/flickering)
- `@remotion/noise` (available from v3.2.32) provides noise utility functions; the official demo drives a grid of dots with `noise3D`, using two dimensions for space and the third for time. [F]/[S] — [Remotion docs, noise](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/noise); [Noise visualization](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/noise-visualization)
- Remotion Effects (available from v4.0.464) are passed through an `effects` prop on canvas-based components including `<Img>`, `<CanvasImage>`, `<Video>`, `<Solid>`, `<HtmlInCanvas>`, `<AnimatedImage>`, `<Gif>` and, from v4.0.474, the `@remotion/shapes` components. Multiple effects apply in the order given. Custom effects are written with `createEffect()`, which supports 2D canvas, WebGL2 and WebGPU. The page names `blur` as one built-in. [F] — [Remotion docs, Effects](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/effects)
- `noiseDisplacement()` from `@remotion/effects/noise-displacement` (added v4.0.474) distorts a circular region with deterministic procedural noise and leaves pixels outside the radius unchanged. Options: `center`, `radius`, `strength` (default 36), `seed`, `grainSize` (8), `passes` (6), `blur`, `feather` (0.25), `biasDirection`, `biasAmount`. It is animated by deriving `seed` from `useCurrentFrame()`, and it requires a WebGL2 backend. Sibling effects `noise()` and `wave()` are linked from the page. [F] — [Remotion docs, noiseDisplacement](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/effects/noise-displacement)
- SVG route with no extra package: `<feTurbulence>` generates Perlin noise and `<feDisplacementMap>` uses it to push the pixels of the source graphic; MDN's example displaces the edge of a circle. [F] — [MDN, feTurbulence](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG/Reference/Element/feTurbulence)
- The same two primitives are the standard web recipe for water, wave and fragment distortions on images. Codrops flags the approach as experimental, with browser-support caveats. A low-authority guide suggests `type="turbulence"` for water and `fractalNoise` for clouds and smoke. [S] — [Codrops, Image distortion effects with SVG filters](https://tympanus.net/codrops/?p=38971); [Sara Soueidan's Codrops series, via the viewBox newsletter](https://buttondown.com/viewBox/archive/box-2-ripples-droplets-and-squiggles)
- Real perspective parallax in CSS: a container with `perspective`, children pushed back with `translateZ()`, each corrected by `scale = 1 + (translateZ * -1) / perspective`. Grouped layers need `transform-style: preserve-3d` and must not use `overflow: hidden`. [F] — [Keith Clark, Pure CSS parallax](https://keithclark.co.uk/articles/pure-css-parallax-websites/)
- Annotation primitives map directly onto SVG: a note, a connector (line, elbow or curve), a connector end (arrow or dot), and subject shapes (circle, rectangle, threshold line, badge). [F] — [d3-annotation](https://d3-annotation.susielu.com/)
- Local check in this repository: `remotion-templates/package.json` pins `remotion` at 4.0.457, with `@remotion/noise`, `@remotion/three`, `@remotion/paths`, `@remotion/shapes` and `@remotion/transitions` installed. `@remotion/effects` is not installed and needs 4.0.464 or later (4.0.474 for `noiseDisplacement`). — file read 2026-10-11 at `/Users/feihuyan/project-parallax/remotion-templates/package.json`; version requirements per [Remotion docs, Effects](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/effects)

### Inferences
Rough recipes. All are my own sketches built on the primitives cited above. None is copied from a source, and none has been tested in this repository.

| Technique | Deterministic in code? | Sketch |
|---|---|---|
| Rostrum pan-and-zoom | Yes | One `<Img>` inside an overflow-hidden frame; `transform: translate(x, y) scale(s)` from `interpolate(frame, ...)`. The start and end rectangles are chosen by a person. |
| Layered parallax | Yes | N transparent PNG layers stacked in `<AbsoluteFill>`. Either (a) fake it: each layer gets `translate(dx * f, dy * f) scale(1 + dz * f)` with `f` taken from the `P / (P + d)` factors in section 2; or (b) real 3D: a parent with CSS `perspective`, layers at `translateZ(-d)` with Clark's scale correction, animating the parent's translate or `perspective-origin`; or (c) textured planes in `@remotion/three`. Oversize each layer so its edges never enter the frame. Optional rack focus: an interpolated `filter: blur()` per layer. |
| Masked ambient loop (procedural cinemagraph) | Yes | The still as base; a second element clipped to the moving region with an SVG `<clipPath>` or `<mask>` (the docs caution against CSS `mask-image`). Inside the clip, drive something from the frame number: a gradient whose stops slide (light sweep, water shimmer), an opacity or brightness value from `noise2D(seed, frame * k)` (lamp or sign flicker), a blurred duplicate with `mix-blend-mode: screen` (glow pulse). |
| Seam-free loop | Yes | Make the driver periodic: `sin(2 * PI * frame / loopFrames)`, or sample noise around a circle, `noise3D(seed, cos(a), sin(a))`, so the last frame equals the first. Choose loop lengths that divide the segment length. |
| Noise displacement (water, heat, smoke edge) | Yes | An SVG `<filter>` with `<feTurbulence>` feeding `<feDisplacementMap>`, applied to the clipped region only. Set `seed`, `baseFrequency` or the displacement `scale` as React props computed from the frame; never use SMIL `<animate>`. For directional flow, move the noise instead of re-seeding it (translate a noise tile and feed it in, or write a small `createEffect()` shader). On Remotion 4.0.474 or later, `noiseDisplacement()` does a circular region in one call. |
| Line boil | Yes | Two routes. (1) Pure code: the displacement filter above with a small scale and a stepped seed, `seed = Math.floor(frame / hold) % N`, with N = 3 to 5 and hold = 3 or 4 frames. (2) Asset route: N separately made variants of the same picture, swapped with the same stepped index. Route 1 is cheaper and perfectly registered; route 2 looks more hand-made but needs variants that differ only at the edges. |
| Mesh or puppet warp | Partly | There is no CSS equivalent of a triangle-mesh warp. It needs a canvas or WebGL mesh (for example a subdivided plane in `@remotion/three` with vertices displaced by seeded noise). Cheap substitutes: put the element on its own layer and rotate or skew it about a pivot by 1 to 3 degrees, or apply a displacement effect to that layer only. |
| Cut-out rig | Yes | One `<Img>` or inline SVG group per body part; `transform-origin` at the joint; nest children inside parents so the DOM hierarchy is the bone hierarchy; rotations from `interpolate` or `spring`. Pose sets (the Joysticks 'n Sliders idea) become a lookup of joint angles blended by a single 0-to-1 value. |
| Panorama travel | Yes | A very wide image translated along X with eased stations (hold, move, hold), optionally as two or three parallax layers, with annotations triggered at each station. |
| Zoom-to-detail | Yes | Scale and translate toward a region. Per the project overview, this repository already has `ScrollyReveal` and `AnnotatedZoom` doing this. |
| Push-through and zoom-through | Yes | The outgoing picture scales up about the portal's centre while the incoming picture, clipped to the portal's shape, scales from portal size to full frame; hand over with a cut or a short cross-fade once the portal is larger than the frame. For constant perceived speed, scale must be exponential in time: `s(t) = k^(t / T)`. Infinite zoom is the same thing with picture N+1 embedded in picture N. |
| Annotation layer | Yes | An SVG overlay in image coordinates. Draw lines on with `stroke-dasharray` and `stroke-dashoffset` (or `@remotion/paths`), then fade in the label. Per the project overview, this repository already has `AnnotationSVGLayer` (dot, leader, label). |
| Spotlight (dim the rest) | Yes | A full-frame dark rectangle with an SVG mask hole over the subject, or a dimmed copy of the image beneath a clipped full-brightness copy. Soften the hole's edge with a blur on the mask. |
| Cutaway or x-ray | Yes | A second illustration of the interior, clipped by an animated shape (circle wipe, sliding panel) over the exterior. The interior has to be drawn; code cannot invent it. |
| Pictogram nodes | Yes | Use spot illustrations as `<Img>` or inline SVG inside the existing diagram templates; repeat units instead of scaling them. |
| Integrated type | Yes | Text in a div with a `matrix3d()` transform solved from the four corners of the target wall or sign, plus `mix-blend-mode: multiply` (dark type on a light wall) or `screen` (light type). Give it the same transform as its layer so it stays attached during parallax. |
| Video-sourced cinemagraph | No (needs a clip) | Only the mask, the freeze and the loop are code. The moving pixels must come from a filmed or generated clip. |

- Cost ranking for one person: transform-only effects (pan-and-zoom, parallax, cut-out, panorama, zoom-through) and SVG overlays are nearly free at render time; SVG filters and canvas effects cost render time on every frame at 1080p; WebGL meshes cost authoring time.
- Because Remotion renders frames independently, stepped effects (boil, flicker) must take their state from `Math.floor(frame / hold)` and a seed, never from state accumulated across frames.

### Gaps
- The render-time cost of full-frame SVG filters (`feTurbulence`, `feDisplacementMap`) in Remotion's headless Chrome at 1920x1080 was not found; it needs a local timing test before a long segment is committed to it.
- The list of built-in `@remotion/effects` could not be read (the docs index renders client-side); only `blur`, `noise`, `wave` and `noiseDisplacement` were confirmed by name.
- No Remotion community example of layered-illustration parallax, cut-out rigging or boil was found beyond one low-authority snippet; these recipes are untested here.
- The GSAP, three.js and CSS-Tricks example pages named in the brief were not reached in this pass.

## 5. What are the canonical examples to watch for each, and when does each earn its place?

### Takeaway
There is a clear reference work for almost every technique, and the practitioners who made them agree on one editorial test: the move has to be chosen by someone for a reason. Burns asks for human judgement behind every start and end frame, Gilliam says technique matters less than the story, joke or idea it serves, and Cox wants the annotation to say what to see. Motion added only to stop a still from being still is what those same people describe as the failure.

### Cited Findings

**Examples**
- Rostrum pan-and-zoom: *City of Gold* (NFB, 1957); *Meet Mr. Lincoln* (NBC, 1959); Ken Burns's films, including the 33-second zoom into John Roebling's portrait in *Brooklyn Bridge*. **When it earns its place (practitioners' own tests)**
- Burns treats each photograph as a master shot that contains long shots, medium shots, close-ups, tilts, pans, reveals and detail inserts, and he insists that a person, not a default, chooses the first and last frame of every zoom (the article paraphrases the first point). [F] — [CineD](https://www.cined.com/?p=406363)
- Burns on duration and sound: he holds that meaning builds up over the time a shot is held, and he lays sound under photographs (marching troops, cannon, a bat hitting a ball) so the viewer listens to the picture as well as looking at it. [F] — [CineD](https://www.cined.com/?p=406363)
- The standard motivated moves: pan across a group photograph and come to rest on the person being discussed; zoom out from one person to reveal another as a transition. [F] — [Wikipedia, Ken Burns effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Burns_effect)
- A working editor's checklist (*opinion*, forum): ask what the best move is for that particular picture (some want a push in, some a pull out, some a pan, some both); keep it subtle enough that viewers register the feeling and not the effect; check each move against the pictures before and after it to avoid repetition; fit it to the music. [S] — [DV Info forum](https://dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-507952.html)
- Direction suggestions (low-authority blogs, *opinion*): travel left to right across a wide vista, drift down a newspaper clipping, move up to reveal a face; if the motion does not improve clarity or focus it is probably unnecessary. [S] — [ShortGenius blog](https://shortgenius.com/blog/what-is-the-ken-burns-effect); [Frameo blog](https://frameo.ai/blog/use-ken-burns-effect-images/)
- Morgen and Burstein used parallax because it let them stage photographs as dramatic scenes and immerse the audience in their subject's own world; the film's opening curtains signal that this reality is constructed. [F] — [Documentary.org](https://www.documentary.org/feature/kid-stays-picture-robert-evans-world-and-welcome-it)
- Gilliam chose cut-out because it was the quickest and easiest kind of animation he knew, and he holds that the technique matters little next to the story, joke or idea it serves (article of 24 Aug 2011 quoting the 1974 programme). [F] — [Make](https://makezine.com/article/craft/how-to_cut-out_animation_with/)
- Gilliam framed the choice as a budget question: what could be made for 400 pounds in two weeks. [S] — [Variety, 2024](https://variety.com/2024/film/news/terry-gilliam-annecy-animation-festival-lifetime-achievement-award-masterclass-1236030328)
- Snyder on boil as a budget device: a helicopter scene costs the same as a living-room scene. [F] — [Wikipedia, Squigglevision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squigglevision)
- Cox: annotation exists so that a graphic does not leave the reader to work out its point alone. [F] — [EU data visualisation guide](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-annotations-introduction)
- Fincher's test for type: does it look projected onto the shot, or physically present in it? [S] — [Art of the Title](https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/panic-room/)
- Neurath's reason for repeating pictograms instead of enlarging them is comparability: with a scaled symbol it is unclear whether height, length or area is being compared. [S] — [Wikipedia, Isotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotype_(picture_language))

### Inferences
- **What a good pan-and-zoom looks like**, assembled from Burns and the editors above: (1) a chosen start frame and a chosen end frame, each a composition in its own right; (2) the end frame is the thing the narration is about at that moment; (3) direction follows the content (toward a face, along a reading direction, out to reveal context); (4) slow enough that the move is felt more than seen, and held long enough for meaning to build; (5) variety across consecutive stills; (6) sound underneath. A move that fails (2) is Burns's zoom to nowhere, and that, not its duration, is what reads as drift.
- **How panorama travel avoids being a longer Ken Burns.** The museum scroll examples differ from a pan in three ways that can be copied: the picture is composed as a sequence of scenes, not one view; time or state changes along the route (day to night, before to after); and the travel stops at stations where something is pointed out. A wide pan with none of these is just a slow pan.
- **Editorial fit for this channel** (my judgement): annotation, spotlight and pictogram nodes carry argument and so earn the most screen time in an essay; cut-out gesture and masked ambient loops carry mood cheaply during narration-heavy holds; layered parallax and zoom-through are punctuation for arrivals and transitions and lose force if used on every still; line boil is a texture choice to be made once per episode, not per shot.
- The Close Read pattern (one picture, a sequence of framed details, a sentence per detail) is the closest published model to an essay beat built on one AI still, and it needs no motion inside the picture at all.
- For phone viewing, techniques that change framing (zoom-to-detail, spotlight, stations) survive small screens better than techniques that add small internal motion (fine boil, slight parallax, subtle shimmer), which can fall below what is visible.
