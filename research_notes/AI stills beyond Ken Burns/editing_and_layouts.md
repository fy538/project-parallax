# Editing techniques and multi-image layouts for getting more out of still images

Research date: 2026-10-11. Scope: Family 1 (techniques that raise pictures per minute) and Family 2 (more than one picture on screen), for a narrated 1920x1080 essay video built from flat AI illustrations plus code-rendered motion graphics. Out of scope by assignment: AI tools, and motion inside a single image (cinemagraph, parallax / 2.5D).

Tags used on findings:
- **[S]** = seen only in a search-result summary; I did not open the page itself. Treat wording and numbers as needing a check before quoting. Where one summary covered several pages, the page linked is my best match for the claim, not a confirmed attribution.
- **[O]** = one person's or one vendor's opinion or house convention, not a study or an industry standard.
- No tag = I opened the page (or the PDF) and read the passage.

Direct quotation has been kept to a minimum on purpose; almost everything below is paraphrase. Words in quotation marks are names of techniques, not quotations.

## 1. What is each technique called by working editors and animators, and what are the common wrong or ambiguous names?

### Takeaway
Most of these techniques have a settled name, but three words are overloaded and should never appear bare in a production script: "montage" (Soviet collision cutting vs. the Hollywood time-compression sequence), "split screen" (strictly two images; more than two is a multi-image or multi-panel frame), and "Ken Burns" (a brand name for a rostrum-style pan and zoom). Two things the brief asks about have no single industry name that I could find: the A-to-B "state swap" between two versions of one frame, and the "panel build" where one frame becomes two then four.

### Cited Findings

#### Family 1: raising pictures per minute

**Montage (three different things)**
- Soviet montage: meaning is produced by juxtaposing shots; two unrelated images generate a third idea that is in neither. [S] — [Explaining History, 19 Nov 2025](https://explaininghistory.org/2025/11/19/the-politics-of-the-cut-how-soviet-montage-theory-revolutionized-cinema-and-challenged-hollywood/)
- In English usage the word "montage" came to mean specifically the rapid shock cutting associated with Eisenstein. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Montage (filmmaking)"](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Video_Reel)
- Hollywood montage / "montage sequence": from the 1930s to the 1950s, a set piece that strings many short shots together with optical effects (dissolves, split screens, double and triple exposures) and music, usually to compress time or summarise. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Montage (filmmaking)"](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Video_Reel)
- Slavko Vorkapich is the figure most identified with the Hollywood form (*Crime Without Passion*, 1934; *Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde*, 1941). [S] — [Wikipedia, "Slavko Vorkapich"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavko_Vorkapich)
- Patrick Vonderau argues the "Vorkapich effect" is better understood as compositing than as a variety of cutting, and notes the term was used so loosely in studios that an executive in 1934 could not define it. [S] — [Vonderau, Montage AV 25/2, 2016 (German)](https://montage-av.de/wp-content/uploads/pdf/2016_25_2_MontageAV/montageav16-2-Vonderau.pdf)
- Vorkapich stressed connection and continuity between shots where Eisenstein stressed collision. [S] — [Magiran abstract, "Theories of Montage by Eisenstein and Vorkapich"](https://magiran.com/paper/1601798/?lang=en)

**Limited animation (synonym: "planned animation")**
- Definition: a traditional animation process that reuses frames of character animation rather than redrawing everything. Named devices: looping only the mouth, eyes, an arm or a shadow; characters held still; "smear frames" for very fast moves (*The Dover Boys*, 1942); cels over a still background (first seen in *The Sinking of the Lusitania*, 1918); Syncro-Vox (filmed lips over a still drawing, *Clutch Cargo*). — [Wikipedia, "Limited animation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_animation)
- Hanna-Barbera's version: backgrounds reused across scenes, cloud shapes that work for motion in any direction, characters designed with muzzles so only the mouth animates, frequent blinks to keep a held pose alive. Written by Bill Burnett (Creative Director, Hanna-Barbera, 1993–96); posted 18 Jan 2009. [O: an insider's defence of the studio] — [Burnett, "Limited Animation… Unlimited Imagination"](https://fredseibert.com/post/71257648/limited-animationunlimited-imagination)
- UPA's use was a stylistic choice (stylisation and storytelling over fluid motion; *Gerald McBoing-Boing* won an Oscar); Hanna-Barbera's was economic necessity after MGM closed its cartoon unit in 1957. [S] — [GarageFarm, "Limited Animation: What is it?"](https://garagefarm.net/blog/limited-animation-what-is-it); [Pixune, "What Is Limited Animation?"](https://pixune.com/blog/what-is-limited-animation/)
- Anime version (Mushi Production, *Astro Boy*, 1963): take the manga frame as the basis for the shot and move only a section of it; keep the body on a fixed cel and animate the arm on a separate one; reuse banked cels and sequences (for example one flying cycle over different backgrounds); "three-frame shooting" as a standard. Article by "Rich", 1 Jul 2015. — [Tofugu, "Anime vs. Cartoons"](https://www.tofugu.com/japan/anime-vs-cartoons/)
- Tezuka's collaborator Sadao Tsukioka says Tezuka was attracted to limited animation as an aesthetic idea, not only as a saving; Matt Alt describes lingering freeze-frames and theatrical held poses as hallmarks that grew out of cost-saving workarounds. 23 Aug 2021. — [Animation Obsessive, "When Osamu Tezuka Redefined Anime"](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/when-osamu-tezuka-redefined-anime)
- "Postcard memories" (Osamu Dezaki): a frame of animation crossfades into a detailed, repainted still of itself at a dramatic peak; efficient as well as dramatic. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Osamu Dezaki"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osamu_Dezaki)

**Pose-to-pose, key poses, breakdowns, in-betweens**
- Pose-to-pose: draw the key poses first (usually first and last drawing of an action plus an important moment between), then fill in. Straight-ahead: draw from frame 1 onward in order; suits smoke, water and other fluid things; hard to control where it ends. [S] — [Toon Boom Learn, "Straight Ahead and Pose to Pose Principle"](https://learn.toonboom.com/modules/animation-principles/topic/straight-ahead-and-pose-to-pose-principle)
- Key poses (keyframes, "keys"): the significant positions that define an action. Breakdowns: drawings between two keys that define how the move travels. In-betweens ("tweens"): the drawings that carry the action from key to key. [S] — [Adobe, "Animation key poses"](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/animation/discover/animation-key-poses)
- More in-betweens make the action read slower; too few make it choppy. [S] — [Toon Boom Learn](https://learn.toonboom.com/modules/animation-principles/topic/straight-ahead-and-pose-to-pose-principle)
- Working order in one practice guide: block the key poses, add breakdowns, then in-between. [S][O] — [Playbooks, "straight-ahead-pose-mastery"](https://playbooks.com/skills/dylantarre/animation-principles/straight-ahead-pose-mastery)

**Animatic (synonyms: "Leica reel"; timed storyboard)**
- An animatic is a storyboard given duration, motion and sound and cut together as video; its job is pacing. "Leica reel" is the older name, from the Leica cameras used to shoot storyboard panels. [S] — [Moonb, "How to Make an Animatic"](https://www.moonb.io/blog/how-to-make-an-animatic); [Dream Farm Studios, "Animatic in a nutshell"](https://dreamfarmstudios.com/animatic-in-a-nutshell-the-storyboard-made-animated/)
- As a finished style: YouTube's Culture & Trends report on animation names animatics as one of three dominant formats (with memes and full episodes) and says creators often treat rougher animatics as the final product. [S] — [Cartoon Brew, YouTube animation report](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/distribution/youtube-animation-report-259897.html)
- In the first six months of 2025 more than 4,000 fan videos about *EPIC: The Musical* were uploaded, including hundreds of animatics; the composer then commissioned fan artists to make official animatics. 9 Apr 2026. — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/04/09/youtube-culture-trends-original-animated-series-digital-circus-hazbin-hotel/)

**Still image film (synonyms: "picture movie"; "photo-roman" for *La Jetée*)**
- A film made mostly or wholly of still images, which gives up the illusion of motion for aesthetic or practical reasons while keeping a normal soundtrack plus dissolves, zooms and pans. — [Wikipedia, "Still image film"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_image_film)
- *La Jetée* labels itself a photo-roman (photo-novel) in its credits, a popular French print genre. [S] — [TCM, *La Jetée*](https://tcm.com/tcmdb/title/648488/wd)

**Ken Burns effect (better script terms: pan and zoom on a still; rostrum move; push-in / pull-out)**
- Burns credits Jerome Liebling and the NFB film *City of Gold* (1957, Colin Low and Wolf Koenig) as prior art; the name attached to Burns the way the dolly zoom attached to Hitchcock. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Ken Burns effect"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Burns_effect)
- The machine is a rostrum camera: a camera on a vertical column pointing down at a table; the camera moves up and down, the table moves and rotates; moves are exposed frame by frame. — [Videomaker, "Use of Stills in Video"](https://www.videomaker.com/article/c3/14088-use-of-stills-in-video/)
- Burns himself says the label is a shorthand that reduces a layered method (energetic camera plus sound effects, period music and first-person voices). [S] — [Big Think, "What Makes Ken Burns's Films Unique?"](https://bigthink.com/videos/what-makes-ken-burnss-films-unique/)

**State swap, before-and-after, A/B comparison**
- Blink comparator: an astronomers' viewing device that switches rapidly between two photographs of the same field so that anything that moved or changed appears to jump while everything else stays still. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Blink comparator"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blink_comparator)
- Flicker paradigm (Rensink, O'Regan and Clark, 1997): the psychology-lab version, where an image alternates with an altered copy of itself. [S] — [Rensink, "The Need for Attention to See Change"](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rensink/flicker/)
- Before/after slider: Knight Lab's JuxtaposeJS (announced 24 Sep 2014) overlays two frames with a draggable divider; Knight Lab frames the two uses as then/now (slow change) and before/after (one dramatic event). [S] — [Knight Lab, "Introducing JuxtaposeJS"](https://knightlab.northwestern.edu/2014/09/24/introducing-juxtaposejs-an-easy-way-to-compare-two-frames/index.html)
- Replacement animation: a stop-motion method in which a different puppet, head or limb is swapped in for each frame instead of bending one puppet; George Pal's Puppetoons are the standard example. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Puppetoons"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetoons)

**Match cut and sub-types**
- Match cut: any transition, in picture or sound, that uses an element of the outgoing scene to carry the viewer into the next. Sub-types named: graphic match (shape, colour, composition), match on action (movement), sound bridge (includes J-cuts and L-cuts), repeat cut (overlapped frames for a stutter). Brent Dunham, 14 Dec 2024. — [StudioBinder, "Match Cuts & Creative Transitions"](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/match-cuts-types-of-cuts/)
- "Match on action" and "cutting on action" are the same thing. [S] — [Adobe, "Match cut"](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/discover/match-cut.html)
- "Match dissolve" is a graphic match executed as a dissolve rather than a hard cut; StudioBinder treats it as a way of executing a graphic match, not a separate type, while FilmDaft uses the term directly for the Xanadu opening of *Citizen Kane*. — [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/match-cuts-types-of-cuts/); [S] [FilmDaft](https://filmdaft.com/?p=36584)
- "Form cut" and "visual rhyme cut" appear as synonyms for the graphic match (two unrelated shots joined on a shared geometric shape, such as a round clock to a round planet). [S][O: vendor glossary] — [Morphic glossary, "Match cut"](https://morphic.com/es/ai-glossary/match-cut)

#### Family 2: more than one picture on screen

**Split screen, multi-image, multi-panel**
- Strict usage: a split screen has two images; more than two is a "multi-image screen"; in practice people say split screen and qualify it ("four-way split screen"). [S] — [Kinolab film-language glossary, "split screen"](https://www.kinolab.org/browseTags.php?id=289)
- Historical names: "Polyvision" (Abel Gance, three projectors), "multi-dynamic image technique" (Christopher Chapman, *A Place to Stand*, Expo 67), "multi-screen" (the 1964 and 1967 fair films). — [Wikipedia, "Split screen (video production)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_screen_(video_production))
- *American Cinematographer* (Feb 1969) calls it the "multiple-image technique" and the units "panels". — [ASC, "Multiple-Image Technique for The Boston Strangler"](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/)
- Pablo Ferro called them "multiples". Will Perkins, 4 Aug 2015. — [Art of the Title, *The Thomas Crown Affair*](https://artofthetitle.com/title/the-thomas-crown-affair)
- On *24* the units were "boxes"; the editor proposed small boxes instead of a plain split so that different shots of one scene could sit together. [S] — [JoBlo, "24: Gone But Not Forgotten"](https://www.joblo.com/24-gone-but-not-forgotten/amp/)
- Picture-in-picture (PiP; "inset" on some hardware): a small frame layered over a larger one, as opposed to a split, which divides the frame between sources of similar size. [S] — [Clueso glossary](https://www.clueso.io/glossary/picture-in-picture); [Tella, "Split screen"](https://www.tella.com/definition/split-screen)
- "Brady Bunch" layout or effect: editors' slang for a grid of several videos in one frame, after the show's titles; not a formal term. [S] — [WeVideo, "The Brady Bunch effect"](https://blog.wevideo.com/news/the-brady-bunch-effect/)
- What the device has been used to say: both ends of a phone call (*Suspense*, 1913; *Pillow Talk*, 1959); reality beside inner desire (*The Queen of Spades*, 1916); real-time simultaneity (*24*; *Timecode*, 2000). — [Wikipedia, "Split screen (video production)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_screen_(video_production))
- Compressing time: the polo sequence in *The Thomas Crown Affair* was cut from fifteen minutes to two as a multi-image montage. Michael Maher, 2 Sep 2022. — [PremiumBeat, "The Art of Split Screen"](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/split-screen-editing-and-composing/)
- Contrast and anticipation: Fleischer put ordinary action in one panel and what is about to happen in another. — [ASC](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/)
- How it was made before digital: mattes, an animation camera and an optical printer; Ferro drew a layout with a sketch artist to fix where each image would sit, checked it on a Moviola, then sent it to an optical house. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/the-thomas-crown-affair)

**Triptych, diptych, polyptych**
- Diptych = two parts, triptych = three, tetraptych or quadriptych = four, polyptych = any multi-part work (some sources reserve it for four or more). [S] — [National Portrait Gallery glossary, "Polyptych"](https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/explore/glossary-of-art-terms/polyptych)
- A triptych is usually a central panel with two hinged wings or shutters; the centre carries the main image. [S] — [National Gallery glossary, "Triptych"](https://nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/triptych)
- In altarpieces the lower register (the predella) often carries small narrative scenes while the upper panels are static. [S] — [Italian Renaissance Learning Resources](https://www.italianrenaissanceresources.com/?p=3452)
- Polyvision in *Napoléon* (1927): three synchronised reels side by side, total aspect ratio about 4:1; the name was coined by critic Émile Vuillermoz. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Polyvision"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvision)
- Gance used the three panels in several ways: one continuous panorama; three panels of contrasting or simultaneous action; mirrored side panels framing the centre; and tinting the three screens blue, white and red. [S] — [Decent Films, *Napoléon* review](https://decentfilms.com/reviews/napoleon1927); [BFI, "Napoleon: 10 unmissable highlights"](https://bfi.org.uk/features/napoleon-highlights-abel-gance-silent-film)

**Motion comic (synonym: "animated comic")**
- A hybrid of print comics and animation: individual panels are expanded to a full shot; voice, music, sound effects and some animation are added to the original art; balloons and caption boxes are usually removed. Warner's *Watchmen: Motion Comic* (2008) is the first major one and the first use of the term. — [Wikipedia, "Motion comic"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_comic)
- Made by manipulating the static comic art in software such as After Effects to give an effect close to paper cut-out animation (Craig Smith). [S] — [Smith, "Motion comics: the emergence of a hybrid medium"](https://repository.canterbury.ac.uk/item/87685/motion-comics-the-emergence-of-a-hybrid-medium)
- In the *Watchmen* motion comic heads and arms shift and figures come forward off the background as if the picture were stacked paper on separate planes. 1 Mar 2009. [S][O: reviewer] — [Comics Worth Reading](https://comicsworthreading.com/2009/03/01/watchmen-the-complete-motion-comic/)

**Comic-panel grammar (McCloud)**
- McCloud's six panel-to-panel transitions: moment-to-moment, action-to-action, subject-to-subject, scene-to-scene, aspect-to-aspect, non-sequitur. "Closure" is the reader's work of joining the gap between panels into continuous meaning; the amount of closure demanded rises through the list. [S] — [Middlebury MediaWiki, "Closure in comics"](https://mediawiki.middlebury.edu/wiki/MIDDMedia/Closure_in_comics)
- Subject-to-subject is the comics equivalent of shot / counter-shot; aspect-to-aspect tours facets of one place or mood while time stands still. [S] — [Middlebury MediaWiki](https://mediawiki.middlebury.edu/wiki/MIDDMedia/Closure_in_comics); [Comics Cube glossary, Dec 2010](https://www.comicscube.com/2010/12/comic-book-glossary-transitions-part-1.html)

**Contact sheet, filmstrip**
- A contact sheet is a same-size print of a whole roll of negatives, made by laying the film on the paper; photographers mark frames on it as rejected, selected or needing work. [S] — [Skylum, "Contact sheet photography"](https://blog.skylum.com/contact-sheet-photography/)
- The Whitney describes it as a photographic proof and names repetition, seriality and sequencing as its inherent qualities. [S] — [Whitney Museum, "A Few Frames: Photography and the Contact Sheet"](https://whitney.org/exhibitions/a-few-frames)
- The term survives in post-production software: Nuke's "Contact Sheet" view lays the shots of a sequence side by side for review. [S] — [Foundry Nuke docs](https://learn.foundry.com/nuke/16.0v1/content/timeline_environment/contact_sheet/contact_sheet_pickmode.html)

### Inferences
- Suggested unambiguous script vocabulary, derived from the above: "montage sequence" (time compression, many images under one stretch of narration) vs. "rapid-cut montage" (speed is the point) vs. "juxtaposition cut" (two images whose collision is the argument); "graphic match" or "match dissolve" for the analogy-carrying cut on shape; "held pose", "pose swap" and "pose-to-pose sequence" for figure stills; "two-up / three-up (triptych) / four-up (quad)" plus "inset (PiP)" for layouts; "multi-panel build" for one-to-two-to-four.
- "Replacement animation" is a stop-motion term. Borrowing it for swapping one flat still for a slightly different version of the same still is defensible (the mechanism is the same: substitute a whole new object per state) but an animator would more naturally read that as pose-to-pose or as a held-pose swap.
- The limited-animation literature is the closest precedent for this channel's situation: a fixed background, a figure on its own layer, and change expressed as a small number of strongly designed poses rather than as movement. The difference is that here extra stills are nearly free, so the constraint is consistency between poses, not drawing count.
- The triptych convention (dominant centre, subordinate wings) and Gance's "mirrored sides framing the centre" give a ready grammar for a bounded analogy: source case left, target case right, the claim or the break in the centre.

### Gaps
- I found no standard industry term for the on-screen "state swap" between two versions of one frame. The nearest named things are the blink comparator (astronomy), the flicker paradigm (psychology), the before/after slider (web journalism) and replacement animation (stop motion).
- I found no standard term for the "panel build" (one frame becoming two, then four). Practitioner sources just say panels or multiples appear, multiply and change shape.
- "Cut on shape" is not attested as a term of art in anything I opened; "graphic match" is the standard name, and "form cut" came only from a vendor glossary.
- I did not find a citable source for "storyboard strip" or "filmstrip recap" as a named on-screen device; the contact-sheet sources are about photography, not video.
- UPA is under-sourced here: Wikipedia's limited-animation page mentions it only as a see-also, and the claims about UPA's motives and the *Gerald McBoing-Boing* Oscar were seen only in a search summary covering several pages, so the two pages linked for them are best matches rather than confirmed sources.
- I did not open McCloud's book; all McCloud material is via secondary summaries.

## 2. What concrete numbers do practitioners give (seconds per image, images per sequence, frames of overlap, maximum simultaneous moving panels)?

### Takeaway
Hard numbers exist for film cutting rates (about 10 s per shot in the 1930s–40s, under 4 s after 2000, a practical floor near 1.5–2 s), for limited-animation drawing counts (roughly a tenth of full animation), and for classic multi-image films (panels lasting one to two seconds, up to a dozen images at once but never peaking together). For how long a still may hold under narration, and for how many panels may move at once, there are only software defaults, forum opinion and lab analogues.

### Cited Findings

**Shot length in cinema (the best-measured baseline)**
- Bordwell (Film Quarterly, Spring 2002): 1930–1960, most features had 300–700 shots and an average shot length (ASL) of roughly 8–11 s, and A-features rarely went below 6 s; mid-to-late 1960s, many films at 6–8 s; 1970s, mostly 5–8 s; 1980s, mostly 5–7 s with double-digit ASLs almost gone; by 1999–2000 a typical film ran about 3–6 s, with 3,000–4,000 shots in the fastest. — [Bordwell, "Intensified Continuity" (Yumpu copy)](https://www.yumpu.com/en/document/view/36517206/intensified-continuity-visual-style-in-contemporary-american-film/2)
- Fastest examples in that essay: *The Crow*, *U-Turn*, *Sleepy Hollow* at 2.7 s; *El Mariachi*, *Armageddon* at 2.3 s; *Dark City* (1998) at 1.8 s, the fastest he had found. He suggests a ceiling: a feature averaging under 1.5 s per shot is hard to imagine. — [Bordwell 2002 (Yumpu copy)](https://www.yumpu.com/en/document/view/36517206/intensified-continuity-visual-style-in-contemporary-american-film/2)
- Bordwell, 27 May 2007: the café scene in *The Shop Around the Corner* (1940) uses 2 shots outside (about 82 s each) and 14 inside (about 21 s each); the equivalent scene in *You've Got Mail* (1998) uses 41 and 84 shots at about 4.1–4.3 s each, for nearly identical scene lengths (8:48 vs 8:42). He says 2–4 s averages are not uncommon in recent films and about 5 s was common in American silents. — [Bordwell, "Intensified continuity revisited"](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/)
- Cutting et al. (i-Perception, 2011), 160 English-language films 1935–2010: ASL about 10 s in the 1930s–40s, below 4 s after 2000; about 1,100 shots per film; extremes were *The Seven Year Itch* (1955) at 26.2 s and *Rocky IV* (1985) at 2.2 s. — [Cutting, Brunick, DeLong, Iricinschi, Candan, "Quicker, faster, darker"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- Stephen Follows (3 Jul 2017, Cinemetrics data, films 1997–2016): 1,045 shots per film on average; documentaries had the fewest of any genre at 491; action films averaged 4 s per shot, horror 15.7 s. — [Stephen Follows, "How many shots are in the average movie?"](https://stephenfollows.com/p/many-shots-average-movie)
- Shot lengths are strongly right-skewed, with a mode around 2 s across regions and periods, so the mean overstates the typical shot. [S] — [LMU Munich e-pub, PsyCh Journal paper](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/74376/1/pchj.384.pdf)
- A Google Video AI pass over US television news estimated about 4.8 s per shot. Kalev Leetaru, 3 Jun 2019. [S] — [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/kalevleetaru/2019/06/03/using-googles-video-ai-to-estimate-the-average-shot-length-in-television-news/)

**How long a still holds**
- Software defaults: iMovie places a still for 4 s; imgix's video-assembly API for 5 s (paired with a pan/zoom). [S] — [iMovie help note](https://helpdesk.wsesu.net/support/solutions/articles/1000005559); [imgix docs](https://docs.imgix.com/apis/video/assembly/video-assembly-dur)
- An editing-teaching source states the principle rather than a number: each shot has an optimum duration set by its content and the complexity of its form. [S] — [CUNY Journalism, visual storytelling notes](https://vsw.journalism.cuny.edu/?p=686)
- Forum practice: some stills 2 s, others 6 s, judged by eye; a photo montage holding 30 s on a single idea is probably too long; one filmmaker's informal survey put documentary clips at 6–8 s. [S][O] — [Vegas forum, "Transitions and Timing"](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.info/us/forum/transitions-and-timing--70094); [IndieTalk, "How long is too long for a photo montage?"](https://indietalk.com/threads/how-long-is-too-long-for-a-photo-montage.41915/latest)
- A software-tutorial site says Burns held a 33-second zoom on a portrait of John Roebling in *Brooklyn Bridge* (1981). [S][O: single commercial blog, unverified] — [tryuncle.com Resolve tutorial](https://tryuncle.com/learn/davinci-resolve/davinci-resolve-how-to-create-a-photo-slideshow-with-ken-burns-effect)

**Animatic and storyboard timing**
- Toon Boom Storyboard Pro gives every new panel 1 s by default; the artist then retimes to action and soundtrack. [S] — [Toon Boom Storyboard Pro docs, "About Timing"](https://docs.toonboom.com/help/storyboard-pro-25/storyboard/timing/about-timing.html)
- Rough starting values from a vendor glossary: establishing shot 3–6 s, ordinary dialogue 2–4 s, quick reaction 0.5–1.5 s. [S][O] — [Morphic glossary, "Animatic"](https://morphic.com/es/ai-glossary/animatic)

**Limited animation economics**
- Full MGM-era animation: 20,000–30,000 drawings for a cartoon, about six months per seven-minute short, often over $60,000; a "planned" cartoon: 2,000–3,000 drawings, on roughly half the budget of one theatrical short for a weekly half hour. [O: Burnett] — [Burnett essay](https://fredseibert.com/post/71257648/limited-animationunlimited-imagination)
- *Astro Boy*: about 1,500–1,800 drawings per 25-minute episode, roughly a tenth of full animation; full animation uses about 12–18 unique images per second, limited averages about 8 and can go lower. — [Tofugu](https://www.tofugu.com/japan/anime-vs-cartoons/)
- Tezuka pitched *Astro Boy* at 550,000 yen per episode. — [Animation Obsessive](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/when-osamu-tezuka-redefined-anime)
- *A Charlie Brown Christmas*: $76,000 and four months for 30 minutes. — [Wikipedia, "Limited animation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_animation)
- A typical Puppetoon needed about 9,000 individually carved figures or parts. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Puppetoons"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetoons)

**Illustrated explainer production**
- Kurzgesagt, by its own account: about 200 illustrated panels per video on average, drawn by two or three illustrators over 8–12 weeks, animated by two or three animators over 8–10 weeks, 1,200 hours or more in total. Transitions and visual metaphors are planned at the sketch stage, before illustration. (Transcript copy of the channel's video.) — [LingQ transcript, "How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours"](https://www.lingq.com/en/learn-english-online/courses/689474/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-120-4887128/)

**Multi-image films**
- *The Thomas Crown Affair* (1968): Ferro put 66 images on one 35mm frame for the swing shot; the polo match went from fifteen minutes to two. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/the-thomas-crown-affair); [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/split-screen-editing-and-composing/)
- Jewison estimated that the robbery sequences showed close to 15 minutes of straight-cut material in about 3.5–4 minutes of multi-image screen time. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/the-thomas-crown-affair); [S] [AFI Catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/19845-THE-THOMAS-CROWN-AFFAIR?cxt=ymal)
- *The Boston Strangler* (1968): the technique fills about 35% of the film; as many as 12 images at once, with six in a typical worked example; panel shots usually ran about three feet of film and more often about a foot and a half. — [ASC, Feb 1969](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/); contradicted on the maximum by a rare-book listing that says as many as seven [S] — [Walter Film](https://www.walterfilm.com/shop/film-category_all/crime-suspense-thriller/boston-strangler-the-1968-revised-second-draft-screenplay/)
- *Nowa Książka* (1975): nine screens in one frame; *Timecode* (2000): four real-time takes in four quadrants for the whole film. — [Wikipedia, "Split screen (video production)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_screen_(video_production))
- *Woodstock* (1970): 16 cameras, more than 100 miles of film, single and multiple images filling a 2.20:1 frame. [S] — [DVD Talk review](https://www.dvdtalk.com/reviews/review/37642); [NZIFF programme note](https://nziff.co.nz/2017-autumn-events/film/woodstock-three-days-of-peace-and-music-directors)

**A/B alternation**
- Tombaugh's blink comparator switched plates about three times a second; the two plates were taken six days apart. Amir Alexander, 12 Feb 2005. [S] — [Planetary Society, "The Discovery of Pluto"](https://planetary.org/articles/pluto-discovery-1)
- Flicker paradigm: with no gap between the two images the change is seen easily; with a blank of about 80 ms or more between them, detection can take over 40 alternations even for a large change. [S] — [Rensink flicker page](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rensink/flicker/)
- Accessibility limit: no part of the content should flash more than three times in any one second, unless the flashing area is small (the W3C reference block is 341 x 256 px, about a 10-degree field at typical viewing distance). [S] — [W3C, Understanding SC 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold)

**Comic-panel proportions**
- McCloud's count for Jack Kirby as the typical American mainstream: about 65% action-to-action, 20% subject-to-subject, 15% scene-to-scene; Japanese comics use subject-to-subject and moment-to-moment about as often as action-to-action and add aspect-to-aspect to set mood. [S] — [Course Hero study guide, ch. 3](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Understanding-Comics-The-Invisible-Art/chapter-3-summary/)

**How many things can move at once**
- Multiple-object tracking: the classic figure is about four moving targets (Pylyshyn and Storm, 1988), but there is no sharp limit; performance falls gradually with target count. [S] — [Wikipedia, "Multiple object tracking"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_object_tracking)
- Capacity depends on speed: up to eight objects can be tracked when they move slowly, only one when they move fast (Alvarez and Franconeri, 2007). [S] — [Alvarez & Franconeri 2007 (PDF)](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/AlvarezTracking2007.pdf)
- Motion-design house rules: only one thing, or one group acting as a unit, should move at a time. [S][O: informal skill document] — [Editframe, motion-design "Attention"](https://editframe.com/skills/motion-design/3-attention)
- Further informal rules: with three or more elements no more than a third in motion together; start the next element before the previous one finishes; per-item stagger offsets of roughly 20–90 ms depending on the guide; total stagger kept under 0.5–2 s; stagger in reading order. [S][O: informal skill documents that disagree with each other] — [Playbooks, "orchestrated-sequences"](https://playbooks.com/skills/dylantarre/animation-principles/orchestrated-sequences)

**Text, margins and safe areas**
- Legacy broadcast convention: title-safe 80% of the frame, action-safe 90%. [S] — [Autodesk Maya help, "Safe display regions"](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2017/ENU/Maya/files/GUID-13D613FF-994F-480A-AF0E-DFE06418A351.htm); [Square brand guidelines](https://brand.squareup.com/video-and-film)
- One team's 1920x1080 convention: titles inside 90%, captions and supers inside 93%; captions at 28 px and supers at 24 px; text at 18–20 px turns to mush under streaming compression, especially on dark grounds. [S][O] — [Towbook video style guide](https://styleguide.towbook.com/video/typography)
- Larry Jordan: for HD avoid type smaller than about 18–20 points; for web video keep text inside action-safe. [S][O] — [Larry Jordan, "Make Your Text Look Great"](https://larryjordan.com/articles/make-your-text-look-great/)

### Inferences
- Converting the *Boston Strangler* figure: 35mm runs 16 frames per foot, so at 24 fps a three-foot panel shot is 2 s and a foot-and-a-half shot is 1 s. Panels in the canonical multi-image feature therefore lasted about one to two seconds each.
- Jewison's estimate implies roughly a 4:1 compression of screen time for the robbery; the polo sequence is about 7.5:1. Multi-panel is the strongest time-compression device in this survey.
- *Astro Boy*'s 1,500–1,800 drawings over 25 minutes works out to 60–72 new drawings per minute, about one fresh drawing every 0.8–1.0 s on average. That is the picture-change rate of the form that made held poses acceptable to a mass audience.
- If a Kurzgesagt video runs about ten minutes (my assumption; the source gives no length), 200 panels is about 20 panels a minute, or one new illustrated scene every 3 s. That sits inside Bordwell's 3–6 s band for contemporary features.
- If the documentaries in Follows's sample run about 90 minutes (my assumption), 491 shots is about 11 s per shot, back at the 1930–1960 Hollywood norm.
- Project context for scale (from the repo's own status notes, not a web source): the current `ai-care-motives` manifest is 53 segments over roughly 11 minutes, about 12.5 s per segment, with 12 stills. To reach a 3–6 s picture-change rate over the still-driven stretches would take a several-fold increase in distinct images or states, which is feasible here because stills are nearly free.
- Phone legibility, by arithmetic rather than from a source: a 16:9 video on a phone held upright is about 70 mm wide and 39 mm tall, so 28 px type on a 1080-line frame is about 1 mm high; held sideways (about 150 mm wide) it is about 2.2 mm. The Towbook sizes are a floor for larger screens, not for upright phone viewing. A quad split on an upright phone gives panels of roughly 35 x 20 mm, which is enough for one bold flat shape per panel and not enough for any in-panel text.
- Stagger offsets of 20–90 ms are one to three frames at 30 fps; those numbers come from interface animation, where items are small. For full panels of an essay frame the classic film sources point to much larger offsets (panel events close together but never coincident).

### Gaps
- No source gave a tested number for how long a still can hold under narration before viewers read it as static. Everything found is a software default, a forum opinion or a vendor heuristic.
- No practitioner source gave "frames of overlap" for a match dissolve or a cross-dissolve between stills.
- No source gave a maximum number of simultaneously moving panels for video. The nearest are the lab tracking figures and informal motion-design rules above.
- No measured average shot length for video essays, YouTube explainers or named channels (Vox, Kurzgesagt, Lemmino, Johnny Harris, Every Frame a Painting) was found; a targeted search returned nothing relevant. No ASL for any Ken Burns film was found either.
- No source gave a minimum panel size for phone viewing, and no official YouTube specification for overlay margins on standard 16:9 playback was found (the published "safe zone" figures are for Shorts and banners, and third-party tools disagree with each other).
- The reported count of images in *Thomas Crown* varies (66 per Ferro; one search summary said up to 60).

## 3. Which named films, series, title sequences, YouTube channels or documentaries are the canonical examples of each?

### Takeaway

## 4. What are the documented failure modes (visual overload, strobing, loss of narration focus, "slideshow" feel)?

### Takeaway
The failures practitioners actually document are: the technique becoming the subject (multi-panel as gimmick), panels competing instead of taking turns, cutting so fast that each shot stops carrying a point, flashes or blanks that hide the very change an A/B swap is meant to show, and stills that are merely shown rather than integrated. "Slideshow" is the standing criticism of still-image work.

### Cited Findings

**Multi-panel and split screen**
- Harpman on animating panels across the frame or opening them up for effect: "the mechanics become the most important thing." Fleischer called using the technique simply as a gimmick a serious error. — [ASC, Feb 1969](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/)
- Design rules given in the same article, each of which names a failure: do not load too much information into one panel or tell a complete story inside one; never let two panels reach their climax at the same instant (close together, never together); balance colour and density because a brighter or more vivid panel drags the eye from the others; keep the matte edge visible in dark scenes (they lit all four sides of each panel; a white matte was tried and overpowered the pictures); ask of every frame where the centre of interest is and whether the viewer is meant to read all panels at once or follow one. — [ASC, Feb 1969](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/)
- Harpman's distinction: the Expo 67 films did not have to advance a story, so they could afford to be mechanical for effect; a narrative film cannot. — [ASC, Feb 1969](https://theasc.com/article/multiple-image-technique-for-the-boston-strangler/)
- Saul Bass came to think the device had lost its currency and suited abundance better than depth or contemplation; by the end of the 1960s it was regarded as run into the ground. — [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/split-screen-editing-and-composing/)
- Ferro on the brief he set himself: make it simple to watch and to understand. — [Art of the Title](https://artofthetitle.com/title/the-thomas-crown-affair)
- *Hulk*'s panels remain divisive: Luke Buckmaster argues Lee used them too sparingly to commit to the idea, and other reviews in the same search set split on whether the panels add information cleanly or sit badly with material Lee treated as tragedy. [S][O] — [Flicks, Luke Buckmaster](https://www.flicks.com.au/features/ang-lees-hulk-is-a-bad-movie-but-it-couldve-changed-cinema/); [Rolling Stone review, 20 Jun 2003](https://www.rollingstone.com/movies/reviews/the-hulk-20030620)
- When one panel carries essential information, gaze goes to it almost regardless of its size or position, with only brief glances at the rest (eye-tracking of sign-language users watching a documentary with an interpreter panel; 2018; small sample). [S] — [Soler, Bosch-Baliarda and Orero, UAB repository](https://ddd.uab.cat/record/195241)
- A small 2025 eye-tracking study of split-screen short videos found higher engagement and satisfaction but no change in recall, and no sign of overload. [S] — [iMotions publication summary](https://imotions.com/blog/publications/short-form-videos-an-exploratory-study-on-the-impact-of-subtitles-and-asmr-split-screen-format-options-using-eyegaze-and-facial-expression-data/)

**Why simultaneous motion fails**
- Staging principle: only one idea should register at a time; the eye goes to motion in a still frame and to stillness in a busy one. [S] — [UIC animation notes, "Staging"](https://www.evl.uic.edu/ralph/508S99/staging.html); [ACM SIGGRAPH education, "Staging"](https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/staging.htm)
- Film viewers show "attentional synchrony" (most people look at the same place at the same time); gaze is pulled by faces, moving hands and bodies, and sudden appearances; the film's structure largely overrides differences in what viewers understand. [S] — [Smith, Loschky, Larson, Magliano, PLoS ONE 2015 (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/ba3cabb1432346b98486af93cf52489e); [NPR, "What We Really See When We Go See A Movie", Aug 2014](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/344036617)
- Tracking capacity drops as objects speed up, to a single object at high speed. [S] — [Alvarez & Franconeri 2007](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/AlvarezTracking2007.pdf)
- Multimedia-learning principles that bear on layout: put words next to the part of the picture they describe (spatial contiguity); do not make viewers read on-screen text while studying a graphic that is also being narrated (split attention, redundancy); cue what matters (signaling); cut decorative material (coherence). [S] — [Ohio State, "Multimedia Learning Principles"](https://u.osu.edu/multimedialearning/?p=11); [EdTech Books, "Principles of Multimedia Design"](https://edtechbooks.org/designing_transmedia_learning_environments/principles_of_multimedia_design)

**Fast cutting**
- Some action sequences are cut so fast and staged so awkwardly that they cannot be followed; fast-cut dialogue needs unambiguous eyelines and a strictly respected axis because it drops establishing shots. — [Bordwell 2002 (Yumpu copy)](https://www.yumpu.com/en/document/view/36517206/intensified-continuity-visual-style-in-contemporary-american-film/2)
- Giving nearly every line its own shot produces redundancy and loses the gradation of a scene that builds toward its high point; Bordwell is not against fast cutting as such (he cites *Zodiac* as intelligent use). [O: Bordwell's critical judgement] — [Bordwell, 27 May 2007](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/)
- Unnecessary cuts become distracting or tiring; movement should serve the message rather than cover weak material. [S][O] — [RouteNote, "Why movement matters in video"](https://licensing.routenote.com/blog/?p=10630)
- Over-editing is said to lower retention for older audiences. [S][O: vendor claim, no data cited] — [Vidpros, "Video Editing Strategies for Retention"](https://vidpros.com/video-editing-strategies-for-retention-keeping-your-audience-hooked/)
- Cognitive model (LC4MP): production pacing raises effort and encoding up to the point of overload, after which both fall. [S] — [Wikipedia, "LC4MP"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)

**A/B swaps and strobing**
- A blank or flash of about 80 ms or more between two versions of a frame makes even a large change hard to find, because it swamps the local motion signal that would otherwise pull attention to the change. [S] — [Rensink flicker page](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rensink/flicker/)
- More than three flashes a second over a large area is the seizure-safety threshold. [S] — [W3C, Understanding SC 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold)

**Stills, slideshows and limited animation**
- Critics describe some still-image films as closer to a slideshow than to cinema. — [Wikipedia, "Still image film"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_image_film)
- Videomaker's warning is the same: the aim is to integrate stills into a motion picture, not to make a slideshow, and making it flow takes deliberate craft. — [Videomaker](https://www.videomaker.com/article/c3/14088-use-of-stills-in-video/)
- In MOOC video, slide-based tutorials (build up a slide, then talk over it for a while) held viewers about half as long as continuous hand-drawn tutorials, and high production value did not rescue them. — [Guo, Kim, Rubin, L@S 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
- Limited animation was widely adopted as a cost cut rather than as the artistic choice UPA intended; Miyazaki blamed Tezuka's low *Astro Boy* price for decades of under-budgeted television anime. [S] — [GarageFarm](https://garagefarm.net/blog/limited-animation-what-is-it); and — [Animation Obsessive](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/when-osamu-tezuka-redefined-anime)
- Hollywood montage sequences were dismissed by historian Richard Koszarski as strings of wipes, dissolves and superimpositions. [S][O] — [Wikipedia, "Montage (filmmaking)"](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Video_Reel)

**Motion comics**
- The limited-animation moves in *Watchmen: Motion Comic* can come off as cheesy. [S][O: reviewer] — [Comics Worth Reading, 1 Mar 2009](https://comicsworthreading.com/2009/03/01/watchmen-the-complete-motion-comic/)
- Turning panels into shots that unfold in real time removes what comics rely on: the space between panels and the reader's control of pace. [S] — [The Digital Review, "Timeframing" script](https://thedigitalreview.com/issue01/timeframing/timeframing-script.pdf)
- A media scholar cited as Morton calls the form an uneasy mix of comics, animation and film. [S][O] — [Akademisk Kvarter (AAU)](https://journals.aau.dk/index.php/ak/article/view/5849/5188)
- Artist John Cassaday said the quality of motion comics he had seen varied. — [Wikipedia, "Motion comic"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_comic)
- McCloud's taxonomy is hard to apply consistently: moment-to-moment and action-to-action blur, and aspect-to-aspect can be indistinguishable from subject-to-subject (Chris Gavaler). [S][O] — [Hooded Utilitarian, "Analyzing Comics 101: Closure"](https://hoodedutilitarian.com/?p=67985)

### Inferences
- The 1969 *Boston Strangler* rules transfer almost directly to a multi-panel build made from stills: one centre of interest per moment, panel events offset in time, panels matched in brightness and saturation, a visible but quiet gutter (on this channel's palette, an ink or paper gutter rather than white), and no flying panels for their own sake.
- For an A/B state swap the change-blindness result gives a firm rule: hard-cut or directly cross-fade between two pixel-registered frames with nothing in between. A dip to black, a white flash or a glitch transition between the two states is exactly the condition under which viewers fail to see the change. Alternating more than twice also needs to stay well under three large-area luminance flips per second.
- "One moving element" has good support from three independent directions (the staging principle, attentional synchrony, tracking limits). In a multi-panel still layout the cheapest way to say "look here" is to make one panel the only thing that changes; everything else holds.
- *Timecode* suggests narration can do the pointing: if the voice names what is in one panel, that panel is where the viewer looks, so panel reveals should follow the order of the sentence. This matches the channel's existing per-element anticipatory-reveal doctrine.
- The motion-comic criticism applies to this channel's risk too: adding small cut-out wiggles to a still tends to read as cheap, whereas a clean substitution of one strong pose for another (the limited-animation solution) does not.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that measures comprehension or recall for narrated multi-panel layouts with several simultaneously moving panels; the split-screen studies found are small and about other formats.
- No source documents "strobing" as a named failure of rapid stills montage beyond the general flash-safety threshold.
- I found no first-hand source for where in the frame the one moving element should sit; the centre-of-screen bias is mentioned only loosely in a radio interview.

## 5. What does the evidence say about how often the picture should change in narrated educational or essay video?

### Takeaway
There is no validated seconds-per-picture figure for narrated essay video. The research that exists says three things: related visual change raises attention and memory at little cognitive cost, continuous visual flow holds viewers much longer than build-a-slide-then-talk, and transient, fast-vanishing material hurts novices unless it is segmented and signalled. The "change something every N seconds" rules in circulation are unsourced creator and vendor heuristics, and they disagree with each other by a factor of ten.

### Cited Findings

**Experimental and observational research**
- Lang, Zhou, Schwartz, Bolls and Potter (Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media 44(1), 2000): as the rate of edits (camera changes within the same visual scene) rose from slow to very fast, physiological arousal, self-reported arousal and memory all rose; the authors conclude edits can raise attention and encoding without much added cognitive load. — [CityU Scholars record of the paper](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)
- The same research programme distinguishes related from unrelated cuts (Lang et al., 1993), and its model predicts a turn-over point: pacing raises effort and encoding until overload, then both fall. [S] — [Wikipedia, "LC4MP"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP)
- A textbook excerpt reports that frequent edits improved recognition while cuts to new scenes helped only up to a point, with recognition falling sharply beyond about ten cuts in two minutes. [S; unverified against the original study] — [Scribd document](https://www.scribd.com/document/641122251/Untitled)
- Cutting et al. (2011): across 75 years, films paired shorter shots with more motion; the authors read this as filmmakers gaining tighter control of viewers' attention, building on evidence that motion and motion onsets capture attention. — [Cutting et al., i-Perception 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- Cutting, DeLong and Nothelfer (Psychological Science, 2010): films made after 1980 have shot-length rhythms closer to a 1/f pattern matching fluctuations of human attention, but that match has no relationship to IMDb ratings. [S] — [National Geographic coverage, Feb 2010](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/100225-foxnews-science-of-hollywood-films-chaos-theory)
- Cutting has pointed out that the decline in shot length shows no inflection at the arrival of MTV in 1982, and that older films packed more characters into a shot and so needed more time per shot. [S; which page in the search set carries this is unconfirmed, the link is a best match] — [TVWriter, "How Film (and TV) Audiences Have Changed in the Last 100 Years"](https://tvwriter.com/how-film-and-tv-audiences-have-changed-in-the-last-100-years/)
- Guo, Kim and Rubin (L@S 2014; 6.9 million edX viewing sessions, four courses): median engagement time was at most 6 minutes whatever the video length; tutorials held viewers only 2–3 minutes; Khan-style tablet drawing held viewers 1.5–2 times as long as slides or code screencasts; their recommendation is to introduce motion and continuous visual flow. — [Guo, Kim, Rubin 2014 (PDF)](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
- Same study: after a Khan-style tutorial 40% of sessions went on to attempt the problem, against 31% for other tutorials; students paused slide and code tutorials at specific landmarks, because those instructors build up a slide and then explain it for a while, whereas Khan-style videos flow continuously. — [Guo, Kim, Rubin 2014 (PDF)](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
- Same study: intercutting an instructor's head with slides beat slides alone; speaking rates ran 48–254 words per minute and the slowest group may have done well because the instructor was writing continuously on a board. — [Guo, Kim, Rubin 2014 (PDF)](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)
- Transience: animation and video content disappears as new content arrives, so viewers must hold earlier material in working memory while processing the new; this can cancel the advantage of dynamic over static graphics. [S] — [University of Reading research briefing on instructional videos, Feb 2021](https://research.reading.ac.uk/design-research-for-testing-diagnostics/wp-content/uploads/sites/178/2021/02/Research-Briefing_Instructional-videos_february-2021.pdf)
- Segmenting: a meta-analysis of 56 investigations found small-to-medium benefits for retention and transfer and lower cognitive load, including when the system (not the learner) sets the pace; the benefit is clearest for novices and disappears for learners with high prior knowledge. [S; author names not confirmed from the pages] — [segmenting-effect meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review (DOI)](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4); [review of segmentation in dynamic visualizations, Educational Psychology Review (DOI)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-010-9135-6)
- Mayer's principles relevant to change rate: present narration and its picture together (temporal contiguity); cue what matters (signaling); cut interesting-but-irrelevant material (coherence); break continuous presentations into parts (segmenting); do not duplicate narration as on-screen text (redundancy). [S] — [Ohio State, "Multimedia Learning Principles"](https://u.osu.edu/multimedialearning/?p=11); [EducationalTechnology.net, "Mayer's Principles"](https://educationaltechnology.net/?p=1878)
- Lillard and Peterson (Pediatrics, 2011): 60 four-year-olds; nine minutes of a fast-paced fantasy cartoon was followed by worse executive-function scores than nine minutes of a slow-paced educational cartoon or of drawing; immediate effect only, small sample, and pace is confounded with fantasy content. — [Medical News Today report](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/234246)

**Platform guidance**
- YouTube's own help page gives no per-second rule. It defines the intro metric as the share still watching after 30 seconds, tells creators to compare a video against their own last ten of similar length, reads dips as places where viewers leave or skip and spikes as rewatched or shared moments, and notes that retention generally tapers over a video. [S] — [YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)

**Practitioner and vendor heuristics (all [O], none with data attached)**

The first three figures below came from a single search summary spanning several creator and vendor pages; each link is the best-match page from that set and the exact page-to-number pairing needs re-checking before any of them is quoted. I opened the AIR Media-Tech page but its body was truncated, so I could not confirm its numbers.
- Change something visual every 10–15 s near the start. [S] — [Pixflow, retention editing](https://pixflow.net/blog/?p=93140)
- Every 15–30 s in the body, and never the same frame for more than 30 s. [S] — [AIR Media-Tech, Olha Yezerska, updated 14 Sep 2026](https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/advanced-retention-editing-cutting-patterns-that-keep-viewers-past-minute-8)
- Every 15–25 s for younger viewers, 20–40 s shots for older audiences when the message carries itself. [S] — [Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/video-editing-strategies-for-retention-keeping-your-audience-hooked/)
- A freelancer's sales listing promises cuts and pattern interrupts every 3–4 s. [S] — [Fiverr listing](https://fiverr.com/lost_armaan/edit-fast-paced-youtube-videos)
- "Award-winning documentaries average 15 s per shot" appears on a marketing blog with no study behind it. [S] — [Vidpros, "Video Clip Length"](https://vidpros.com/video-clip-length/)
- The leaked MrBeast production manual (36 pages, posted Aug 2024; two former producers told Passionfruit it is genuine) works at the scale of minutes, not seconds: most viewers are lost in the first 60 seconds, so the best material is front-loaded, and a "re-engagement" beat is placed at about 3 and 6 minutes. [S] — [Gigazine, 17 Sep 2024](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production); [Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/mrbeast-leaked-document-81151/)

### Inferences
- The most defensible reading of the evidence for this channel: what matters is not the cut count but whether the picture keeps doing something that is tied to the sentence being spoken. Lang's result is about related edits within a scene; Guo's is about continuous flow; Mayer's is about synchrony and signalling. A new still, a pose swap, a panel reveal or a state change all count as "related visual change" if they land on the word they illustrate.
- Lang's related / unrelated distinction maps onto this channel's choices. A pose-to-pose sequence, a state swap or a panel build inside one scene is a run of related edits (cheap for the viewer, good for memory). Jumping to a wholly new illustration is an unrelated cut (costlier). That argues for spending extra images on more states of the same scene before spending them on more scenes.
- The slide finding is the clearest warning for a stills-led essay: "show a complete picture, then talk over it" is the pattern that halved engagement. Building a picture up in step with the narration (the Khan-style property) is the pattern that doubled it. A multi-panel build or an accumulating pose sequence is the stills equivalent of drawing while talking.
- Transience and segmenting pull the other way from raw speed: when the argument is dense (as in an alignment or ethics essay) each rapid sequence should end on a held, signalled summary frame, and a multi-panel layout has the advantage that earlier pictures stay on screen instead of vanishing. A contact-sheet or strip recap is a direct answer to the transient-information problem.
- A reasonable working band, offered as inference only: feature films and television news cluster around 3–6 s per picture; documentaries appear to sit nearer 10 s; vendor heuristics for talking-head YouTube run 10–30 s. A narrated stills essay that changes something related every 3–8 s during argument, drops to 1–2 s for short montage bursts, and allows deliberate holds of 10 s or more on a summary or emotional frame would be inside every evidence-backed range found here. The current project average of about 12.5 s per segment is at the slow edge of that.
- The Lillard study should not be cited as evidence about adult essay viewers; it concerns four-year-olds and confounds pace with fantasy content.

### Gaps
- No study was found that varies picture-change rate in narrated essay or explainer video for adults and measures retention or learning. This is the central missing piece.
- I could not verify the "ten cuts in two minutes" turn-over figure against Lang's original papers; the 2000 abstract contains no numeric edit rates.
- I could not confirm the often-repeated scene-change rates for the Lillard and Peterson stimuli (the report I opened gives no numbers).
- No public YouTube or creator dataset relating cut rate or visual-change rate to audience retention was found; the MrBeast document speaks only to structure at the minute scale.
- The 2010 Psychological Science paper was seen only through press coverage, and sources describe its sample inconsistently (films to 2005 vs. to 2010).
- Mayer's principles were taken from university summary pages, not from Mayer's own texts; the count of principles differs between summaries (12 vs. 15).
