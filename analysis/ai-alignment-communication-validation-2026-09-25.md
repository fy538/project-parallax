# Making alignment understandable: a small validation study

25 September 2026. Proposed research and audience test for the AI/philosophy direction. This is a communication study, not a new episode commitment or a replacement for source verification.

## The question to test

Can someone with no alignment background watch a short Parallax argument and then explain **what happened, what it shows, and what it does not show**? A strong video should also make them curious enough to continue. Enjoyment and retention alone cannot tell us whether they understood the mechanism or mistook a fictional scene for an observed AI incident.

Keep three decisions separate: **comprehension** (did the idea land?), **appetite** (would they choose another video about a related question?), and **production cost** (can this format fit the creator's actual time budget?). A few interviews can expose misunderstandings; they cannot establish channel demand. That requires published videos and repeat-viewer behavior over time.

The existing [curiosity review](../project/psychology/01-information-gap-theory.md) addresses how to make a question feel approachable. The [forest visual comparison](image-remotion-synergy-2026-09-23.md#implemented-comparison--23-september) compares a generated-image opening with a code-only opening, but records no cold-viewer comprehension data. This study fills that gap.

## What the evidence suggests, and its limits

| Finding | Practical implication | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Mayer and Moreno's [multimedia-learning review](https://carpentries.github.io/instructor-training/files/papers/mayer-reduce-cognitive-load-2003.pdf) argues that limited visual and verbal processing capacity can be overwhelmed by unnecessary material. | Reveal one relationship at a time; align spoken terms with the relevant visible object; remove labels or motion that do not help the current inference. | The research is about instructional multimedia, not a tested formula for YouTube retention. |
| The [Parallax curiosity review](../project/psychology/01-information-gap-theory.md) finds stronger support for a bridgeable question than for maximal mystery. | Open with a familiar expectation and one contradiction the viewer can reason about. | Its exact pacing recommendations are inferences from adjacent educational research. |
| In a [2024 controlled study](https://research.google/pubs/believing-anthropomorphism-examining-the-role-of-anthropomorphic-cues-on-user-trust-in-large-language-models/), speech-plus-text and some first-person AI cues increased anthropomorphism and perceived answer accuracy. | Treat a machine voice or inner monologue as a deliberate editorial choice. Test whether viewers incorrectly attribute human motives or consciousness to the illustrated system. | This studied interaction with a pseudo-LLM, not Parallax videos. |
| A [2024 user study of AI explanations](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.1084/) found that explanations increased perceived competence without making participants better at identifying one system's actual limitations. | Ask what viewers *think the machine can and cannot do*, not just whether the explanation felt clear. | The study used a visual question-answering task; it does not prove all explanations mislead. |
| [YouTube's retention guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en) says a rewatch spike can indicate interest **or confusion**, and a dip can indicate skipping or abandonment. | Interpret retention alongside comprehension questions and comments. | Platform analytics become available only after publication and need enough views to be useful. |

## Three research tasks, in order

1. **Audience baseline.** Ask 5–8 people unfamiliar with AI alignment what they think “AI is aligned” means and what they expect a trained model can notice, intend, or control. Collect their words before explaining anything. This identifies which intuitions an opening can use and which terms need translation. Do not treat this small convenience sample as representative of YouTube.
2. **Communication audit.** Review roughly 9 existing videos across technical explainers, reported AI cases, and philosophical essays. Record the opening question, first concrete example, when jargon appears, what each visual change teaches, how sources or speculation are marked, and what a viewer could wrongly infer. Use this as a repertoire study, not as evidence that any style causes higher views. Keep the audit time-boxed; do not build a comprehensive competitor database.
3. **Cold-viewer pilot.** Use an existing 60–90 second *What We Count* rough cut before polishing a full episode. Show one version first to each viewer. Immediately ask: (a) What was the point? (b) Which events were fictional, and which were research findings? (c) What does this example **not** prove about current AI? (d) What question would you want answered next? Only then show a visual alternate or explain the intent. Note exact moments of confusion and measure human revision time.

The pilot should preserve one research-backed claim, one fictional illustration, and one visible distinction between **score** and **outcome**. Any exact numbers shown in the illustration must be clearly authored demonstration values, not implied empirical measurements.

## Decision rule

Revise the script or visual argument if multiple viewers confidently infer the same false claim, confuse fiction with observed evidence, or cannot state the score/outcome distinction in ordinary words. A cut that looks richer but causes those errors has not passed. If viewers understand the idea but do not care what happens next, revise the opening question or human implication. If they understand and want more, make one full episode within the existing production ceiling before expanding the template library.

After publication, compare these interview findings with video-level intro retention, later dips/spikes, and comments. Treat those as diagnostic signals, not proof that one format is universally best. The practical result of this study should be a short **communication contract** for Parallax: how we introduce a concept, label invented scenes, reveal a mechanism, and state an analogy's limit.
