# AI imagery and viewer trust: evidence, YouTube rules, law, and what to disclose

> October 10, 2026. Web research by a sub-agent (text sources only; pages fetched directly where possible, otherwise search summaries, marked as such), plus a byte-level scan of this episode's own asset files for embedded provenance metadata. Tests D3 in `project/DECISIONS.md` and the AI sections of `project/RESEARCH_LOG.md` (§3, §5, §20) and `analysis/ai-video-landscape-2026-10-10.md` (§4); does not repeat them. Nothing in those files has been changed. Section 5 is orientation, not legal advice.

## Bottom line

D3's number does not survive a source check, but its direction mostly does. The "~50%" figure is not in the report D3 cites; it comes from a vendor study of written web articles and measures what happens when readers *suspect* AI, whether or not AI was used. No study measures a trust penalty for clearly stylised AI illustration inside a human-written, human-narrated essay. The penalty that is well documented attaches to three things: the belief that no human was involved, realistic images standing in for the real, and being found out instead of saying so. YouTube requires no label for this episode as described. The larger risks are now on a different axis: YouTube's automated low-quality-AI detection, and an emerging creator norm (since August 2026) of stating what AI did. Recommendation: answer No in YouTube's AI use field, put a short plain disclosure in the description, and keep AI video stylised, illustrative and a minority of the runtime.

## 1. Where the "~50%" came from, and whether it holds

1. **The cited source does not contain it.** D3 and RESEARCH_LOG §3 point to Checkr's consumer report ([Checkr, Dec 9, 2025](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/the-great-untrust-consumer-report-2025); 3,000 US adults via Pollfish). Its AI findings are about worry and confusion (88% find it harder to tell what is real; 48% worry they have missed AI fakery). It reports no drop in trust.
2. **The number is Raptive's.** A study commissioned by Raptive, an ad-monetisation company (3,000 US adults; reported July 15, 2025), found content rated 48% less trustworthy, 57% less authentic and 60% lower on emotional connection when participants thought it was AI-made ([Raptive, updated Aug 22, 2025](https://raptive.com/blog/the-ai-stink-is-real-and-its-costing-brands/)). Three limits: the stimuli were written articles, not images or video; the effect followed belief, not fact (human-written pieces were marked down too); the sponsor sells human-made publisher inventory.
3. **"26%, down from 60%"** is real but soft: Billion Dollar Boy, an influencer agency, surveying preference for AI-made creator content in 2023 and 2025 ([eMarketer, Oct 2025](https://www.emarketer.com/content/consumers-rejecting-ai-generated-creator-content)). The two waves may not ask the same question.
4. **"70% lower retention" (RESEARCH_LOG §3) and "35% drop-off in 45 seconds" (D1)** trace only to commercial blogs citing a Retention Rabbit report I could not find. Treat both as unsourced.
5. **Date error in RESEARCH_LOG §5:** YouTube's disclosure tool launched March 18, 2024, not May 2025 ([YouTube blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/)).

## 2. What the 2024–2026 evidence says about the penalty

1. **It attaches to the label and to assumed automation.** Headlines labelled AI-generated were judged less accurate and less shareable whether true or false, human- or machine-written; the penalty was about a third of a "false" tag, and it came from readers assuming no human was involved ([Altay & Gilardi, PNAS Nexus, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11443540); 4,976 participants). A news outlet lost about half a point of trust on an 11-point scale when an article carried an AI label, and listing the sources largely cancelled the loss ([Toff & Simon, IJPP](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:65830edf-2b12-41f6-98e3-5855de38dfdd); [Nieman Lab, Dec 2023](https://niemanlab.org/2023/12/most-readers-want-publishers-to-label-ai-generated-articles-but-trust-outlets-less-when-they-do)).
2. **Disclosure costs something; discovery costs more.** Across 13 experiments (more than 5,000 participants), people who disclosed AI use were trusted less than those who did not, and those whose AI use was exposed by someone else were trusted least ([Schilke & Reimann, OBHDP, 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html); [summary, May 29, 2025](https://news.arizona.edu/employee-news/being-honest-about-using-ai-work-makes-people-trust-you-less-research-finds)).
3. **For AI images in science communication, a label did not move credibility overall.** In the closest study to our case (742 US adults; Instagram posts about CRISPR illustrated with AI images), an AI disclaimer had no significant overall effect. Viewers familiar with AI rated disclosed posts as more credible; unfamiliar viewers rated them lower ([Eom et al., Science Communication, Oct 19, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10755470251380116)). One study, abstract and author summary only.
4. **Labels on misleading AI images work as intended, with side effects.** They lower belief in the claim the image supports ([Wittenberg et al., PNAS Nexus, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12166545/); 7,579 participants), and they make unlabelled images look more authentic by contrast ([Pawelczyk et al., ICWSM 2025](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/42721)).
5. **Identical art is rated lower when labelled AI,** on creativity, worth and liking ([Bellaiche et al., 2023](https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41235-023-00499-6); [Horton et al., Scientific Reports, 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10624838/); [Millet et al., 2023](https://openresearch.amsterdam/en/page/110038/defending-humankind-anthropocentric-bias-in-the-appreciation-of-ai-art)). These measure aesthetic value, not whether the accompanying argument is believed. Perceived human effort is a mechanism in some of this work ([Heigl, talk abstract, May 2025](https://www.eflab.de/events/detail/jour-fixe-all-just-in-your-head-unraveling-the-side-effects-of-generative-ai-disclosure-in-creative-tasks)); evidence for effort specifically is thin.
6. **Photoreal versus stylised: indirect evidence only.** Across six countries, 26% were comfortable with news outlets creating a realistic image when no photograph exists, against 55% for grammar editing and 19% for an artificial presenter; 62% were comfortable with fully human news, 43% with mostly human plus some AI, about one in eight with fully AI ([Reuters Institute, Oct 7, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2025-how-people-think-about-ais-role-journalism-and-society); YouGov, about 2,000 per country). An RMIT-led project found audiences mostly comfortable with AI-made infographic icons and uncomfortable with AI presenters, and journalists more comfortable with non-photoreal illustration than with anything replacing the camera ([RMIT, Feb 2025](https://www.rmit.edu.au/news/all-news/2025/feb/ai-journalism-report)). No experiment crosses style with label in educational video.
7. **Human involvement is the variable that matters.** In instructional video, learning outcomes are about equal for AI and human presenters while liking and engagement favour the human ([IRRODL, 2024](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/7815); [JMIR Formative Research, 2026](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e90037)). On TikTok-style video, an AI label raised curiosity and also raised perceived risk and lowered trust (720 participants aged 18–24; [Behavioral Sciences, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071179)).
8. **Vendor surveys point the same way and should be weighted lightly:** 36% say AI video lowers trust in a brand ([Animoto](https://animoto.com/blog/news/what-people-think-about-ai-video); 460 US respondents); about half of Canadians say they would engage less with a post they know is AI-made ([YouGov and Meltwater, 2026](https://yougov.com/en-us/reports/54566-how-people-really-feel-about-ai-content-report)).

**Reading.** The evidence for our exact case (stylised AI stills, human script and voice, essay format, YouTube) is thin: there is no direct study and no watch-time data. What exists says the penalty is about perceived absence of a human and about realism, is modest when the label comes with context and sources, and is worst when viewers find out for themselves. An audience that follows AI is, on one study, the audience most likely to read disclosure as honesty. That same audience includes people who object to generative imagery on principle; disclosure does not answer that objection.

## 3. Creator cases

1. **Hank Green, July 29 to August 7, 2026.** Viewers suspected an AI-written script in a Complexly episode. He denied the line in question was AI but said he had used ChatGPT for research and had come to rely on it too much; he paused his own channel. On August 7 he published rules: no part of a script written, edited or outlined by a language model; the thesis always human; no AI-generated images or music; model output never a source; primary sources linked. Reception of the rules was largely positive ([Kotaku, Jul 31](https://kotaku.com/famous-science-youtuber-admits-he-has-unhealthy-relationship-with-ai-after-facing-backlash-over-recent-video-2000720908); [TechCrunch, Aug 1](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/01/youtuber-hank-green-says-his-ai-usage-is-not-healthy/); [Tubefilter, Aug 11](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/08/11/are-creator-ai-disclosures-going-to-be-a-thing/)). **Correction to `ai-video-landscape` §4:** this was about the script, not an AI diagram; none of the coverage mentions an AI image.
2. **Kurzgesagt.** Its October 7, 2025 video committed to human-made videos while using AI as a minor tool (secondary summary: [duncan.dev](https://duncan.dev/link/kurzgesagt-on-ai-slop)). On July 31, 2026 it reported that YouTube's automated detection had treated a hand-animated video as low-quality AI content: the worst-performing upload since 2013 while click-through and watch time were above average. YouTube staff confirmed an error and intervened; the channel pulled the video to re-upload it ([Dexerto, Aug 7](https://www.dexerto.com/youtube/youtubes-ai-slop-detector-incorrectly-targets-kurzgesagt-as-other-creators-fear-same-fate-3395930/); [Kotaku, Aug 8](https://kotaku.com/youtube-mistakenly-penalizes-popular-science-channel-kurzgesagt-for-ai-generated-slop-2000722702)). The mechanism was recommendation suppression, not the disclosure label. No YouTube statement on the cause.
3. **After Green.** Colin and Samir wrote team AI policies and published the discussion; Local News International uses no generative AI and human-made graphics. A CreatorIQ survey of 5,000+ creators found about 5% use AI to generate visuals, against more than half for brainstorming, writing or editing ([TheWrap, Aug 25, 2026](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/tech/content-creators-ai-usage-backlash/)).
4. **Faceless channels as collateral.** Human creators who do not appear on camera report demonetisation during the 2026 enforcement wave; the reporting has no YouTube confirmation ([TNW, Jun 15, 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-faceless-creators-collateral-damage)). The same piece repeats the January 2026 termination of 16 channels (35 million subscribers, 4.7 billion views), which matches RESEARCH_LOG.
5. **What I did not find.** No documented essay or explainer channel that uses clearly stylised AI illustration with human narration and was either punished or rewarded for it. D3's observation that no successful educational channel uses AI illustration as its primary visual still has no counter-example. The absence cuts both ways.

## 4. YouTube's rules as of October 2026

1. **What must be labelled.** Realistic content that is AI-made or meaningfully altered: a real person shown saying or doing something they did not, altered footage of a real event or place, a realistic scene that did not happen, and AI music that is the main focus. Non-realistic AI content is exempt, as is AI used for outlines, scripts, thumbnails, titles, infographics, captions, upscaling and cloning your own voice ([YouTube Help, current](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). The Studio field is now called AI use, under Attributes. This confirms RESEARCH_LOG §20.
2. **New on May 27, 2026** ([YouTube blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/)):
   - The label for photoreal AI content moved from the description to directly below the player (an overlay on Shorts).
   - A second tier exists: disclosure for unrealistic, animated or lightly altered content shows in the expanded description.
   - YouTube now applies a label itself when a creator has not disclosed and its systems detect "significant photorealistic AI use".
   - A wrongly applied label can be changed in Studio, except for content made with YouTube's own tools and content whose C2PA metadata says it is fully generative; those are permanent.
   - A label alone does not change recommendation or monetisation.
3. **Monetisation.** The inauthentic content policy (renamed July 15, 2025) excludes image slideshows and templated videos with little narrative, commentary or educational value, and AI content built on generic templates that looks mass-produced. A separate section allows AI used to visualise a creator's own narrative or to make a unique background, and disallows unrelated AI clips stitched together; another bars AI personas posing as experts ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392); the last two sections carry no date). A human-written, human-narrated essay with one art direction is on the allowed side of every example given.
4. **A separate, less visible system.** The CEO's January 21, 2026 letter committed to reducing low-quality AI content using the spam and clickbait systems ([Variety](https://au.variety.com/2026/digital/global/youtube-channels-using-ai-tools-reduce-slop-neal-mohan-letter-32190/)). From about March 17, 2026 some viewers were shown a prompt asking how much a video felt like low-quality AI, on a five-point scale; YouTube has not said how answers are used ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/movies/youtube-is-now-asking-viewers-if-videos-feel-like-ai-slop/); [PCWorld](https://pcworld.com/article/3096317/youtube-wants-you-to-help-spot-ai-slop-videos.html); wording rests on user screenshots). The Kurzgesagt case shows this system can suppress reach on hand-made flat animation. It keys on how a video looks and is perceived, not on what the creator declares.
5. **Provenance metadata: what our own files carry** (byte scan for C2PA/JUMBF markers on Oct 10; not a full C2PA validator):

   | File | C2PA manifest |
   |---|---|
   | 12 of 12 stills in `assets/stills/` (ChatGPT) | Yes. Signed by OpenAI's media service, source type "trained algorithmic media", plus a recorded watermarking action |
   | 6 of 6 raw clips in `assets/clips-i2v/` (H3 Max via fal) | Yes. Claim generator fal.ai, same source type |
   | Edited keyframes (2 of 2), 4K upscales (2 sampled), Ken Burns clips (2 sampled) | None |
   | Trimmed clip actually used (`public/.../clips/aigen-03-unwatched.mp4`) | None |
   | Rendered previews from Remotion (3 checked, including the full episode) | None |

   So the manifests exist at source and do not survive our normal upscale, edit and render steps. The file we would upload carries none, and the permanent-label rule has nothing to read.
6. **The pixel watermark is a different matter.** OpenAI announced on May 19, 2026 that its images carry Google's SynthID watermark as well as C2PA ([PetaPixel, May 20](https://petapixel.com/2026/05/20/openai-gets-serious-about-detecting-fake-images/)); our manifests record a watermarking step. SynthID is built to survive resizing and compression. Whether it survives a 4x upscale, a moving crop and three encodes is untested, and YouTube has not said whether its detection reads it. If detection did fire on stylised stills, stated policy puts the result in the expanded description, and the creator can change it.
7. **Other generators** (secondary sources): Google's image and video models and Adobe Firefly embed credentials; Black Forest Labs' hosted API signs outputs; Midjourney embeds nothing; Kling and Recraft are disputed or undocumented ([C2PA Viewer survey](https://c2paviewer.com/articles/ai-tools-c2pa-support)).
8. **The camera-capture label** is unrelated: it needs C2PA 2.1 capture hardware and unedited footage ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15446725)).

## 5. Law (orientation only)

**EU AI Act, Article 50. Settled:**
- The transparency duties have applied since August 2, 2026 ([Commission FAQ, updated Jul 24, 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)). The 2026 omnibus amendment delayed high-risk rules, not these, apart from giving generative tools already on the market until December 2, 2026 to add machine-readable marking ([K&L Gates, Jul 31](https://www.klgates.com/EU-Digital-Omnibus-on-AI-Enters-Into-Force-7-31-2026)).
- Marking outputs is the tool provider's duty (OpenAI, fal, MiniMax), not ours ([Article 50(2)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)).
- The user's duty covers two things: deep fakes, and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest. The text duty falls away where a human reviewed the text and someone holds editorial responsibility. For evidently artistic or creative works the deep-fake duty shrinks to saying AI content is present, in a way that does not spoil the work.
- A deep fake must resemble existing people, objects, places or events and falsely appear authentic ([Article 3(60)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/3/)).
- Non-EU users are in scope where the output is used in the EU ([Article 2(1)(c)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/2/)). Personal, non-professional use is excluded; an individual earning regularly from the activity counts as professional (FAQ).
- Content made before August 2, 2026 need not be labelled retroactively. Fines for companies run to 15 million euros or 3% of turnover; national authorities enforce.
- The Code of Practice (June 10, 2026) is voluntary; the Commission's guidelines (July 20, 2026) are non-binding ([Commission notice](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content); [K&L Gates, Jul 28](https://www.cyberlawwatch.com/2026/07/28/eu-ai-act-final-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations-under-article-50/)).

**Unclear:**
- Whether the final guidelines say in terms that stylised or cartoon imagery is outside the deep-fake definition. Commentary on the draft put fantastical content outside and left stylised depictions of real events open ([Covington, May 2026](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/05/10-takeaways-european-commission-draft-guidelines-on-ai-transparency-under-the-eu-ai-act/)). I could not read the final text.
- How the used-in-the-EU test applies to a US creator on a global platform. One law-firm reading is broad.
- Where an unmonetised, pre-launch channel sits. Monetisation moves it toward professional.

**Applied to this episode:** flat, featureless figures illustrating thought experiments do not resemble existing people or events and would not be taken as authentic, so on the statutory definition they are not deep fakes. A human-reviewed script with a named author fits the text exemption. A description disclosure would exceed what the creative-work rule asks in any case.

**Elsewhere** (secondary sources, not checked against primary texts):
- **California:** SB 942 as amended became operative August 2, 2026 and binds large generative-AI providers; from January 1, 2027 large platforms must detect and show provenance data ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/californias-ongoing-ai-regulation-key-deadlines-arriving-2026-and-beyond)). No duty on creators. Expect platform labelling to tighten in 2027.
- **India:** amended IT rules in force February 20, 2026 require large platforms to collect an uploader declaration for synthetic audio-visual content ([HLC](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/india-introduces-mandatory-labelling-for-ai-and-3hour-takedown-for-illegal-content)).
- **South Korea:** the AI Basic Act (January 22, 2026) puts labelling on AI operators ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)).
- **China:** the September 1, 2025 measures bind providers and platforms and require users on Chinese platforms to declare ([Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/chinas-ai-labeling-measures-and-mandatory-national-standards-take-effect-september-1)); relevant only to the Bilibili track (D2).
- **US federal:** I found no labelling duty on creators; not searched exhaustively.

## What this changes for us

**1. D3's premise: keep the decision, replace the rationale.**
- *Does not stand:* "trust drop (~50%)" as a finding about AI visuals, and "evidence overwhelmingly shows". The number is mis-cited, comes from text articles, and measures suspicion.
- *Stands for realistic AI imagery:* lowest audience comfort, mandatory and now automatic and prominent YouTube labelling, and the only category EU law reaches. Keep the ban.
- *Stands for AI as the primary visual:* still no successful example, and since 2026 a platform classifier that suppresses videos that look machine-made. Motion graphics stay primary.
- *Does not apply, on current evidence, to what this episode does:* a minority of clearly non-realistic, single-style illustration under a human argument and voice. Nothing measured says this costs credibility; the one close study says a label is neutral overall and positive for AI-literate viewers.
- *New since D3:* the cost has shifted from "viewers distrust AI pictures" to "viewers and the platform punish what looks effortless or undeclared". D4 (AI-assisted but not AI-labelled) is the decision now most out of date, along with the line in `PROJECT_VISION.md` about skirting the edge on disclosure.

**2. What YouTube requires of this episode as described.**
- AI use field: **No.** The stills and the one clip are non-realistic; nothing depicts a real person, alters real footage or shows a realistic event that did not occur; narration is human. That is the accurate answer to the question the form asks.
- Nothing forces a label: the renders so far carry no C2PA manifest. Re-check the final export after NLE assembly, and do not rely on this staying true. If YouTube ever applies a label, policy puts it in the expanded description, where our own disclosure will already be.
- Monetisation policy: on the allowed side, as long as the episode remains an argued essay and not a narrated slideshow.
- Keep the receipts already being produced (prompt files, JSON sidecars, script versions, project files). A new channel has no partner manager to call if the low-quality-AI classifier misfires.

**3. Recommended disclosure: yes, in the description, in our own words.**

Reasons: a bare label invites the assumption that everything was automated, while context and sources cancel most of the penalty; discovery is worse than declaration, and discovery is likely (the image model's look is recognisable, and the source files carry provenance marks); published AI rules have been spreading among education creators since August 2026; and the episode asks viewers whether they can trust an AI they cannot check.

Proposed text, placed above the source list:

> **How this was made.** The argument and the narration are mine. I used AI tools for research and drafting, checked every claim against the sources below, and take responsibility for the script. The illustrations are AI-generated in a deliberately non-realistic style that I directed and edited; one is animated with an AI video model. The photographs, documents and footage are real, and reconstructions are labelled on screen. Charts and diagrams are built in code.

Two conditions. The second sentence must be true exactly as written; after the Hank Green episode the script is the sensitive part, not the pictures, so edit it to match how this script was really produced. And the fourth sentence is a promise: no AI image may stand in for evidence anywhere in the episode. Optional and cheap: one line on the end card ("Illustrations: AI-generated. Voice and argument: human.") so the statement is in the video and not only under it.

**4. Rule of thumb for AI video in this style.**
- **Illustrate, never evidence.** AI imagery belongs to thought experiments and analogies (the shopkeeper, the river). Real events (Volkswagen, the EPA notice, published experiments) keep real photographs, documents, footage and labelled reconstructions. This one rule keeps the episode outside YouTube's required label and the EU deep-fake definition. It argues against the proposal to replace the Beat 6 stock car footage with AI plates: that beat is the real-world case, and its pictures are what the disclosure promises are real.
- **If a clip drifts toward realism, cut it.** The tests in `ai-video-landscape` §10 found that whatever moves gets redrawn smoother and more sculpted. Realism drift is now a policy trigger as well as a style failure: photoreal is what YouTube detects and labels prominently.
- **Do not raise the share.** AI imagery is about a third of the runtime. Animating stills that are already in the cut changes nothing on this count; adding AI shots to replace graphics or real footage does. Coded graphics and real sources stay the majority. The threshold is a judgment; no source gives a number.
- **Motion has to carry the point.** The pinned first-and-last-frame shot (the street emptying) earns its place because the change is the argument. Ambient motion added for liveliness is the kind of low-effort look the perception research and the platform classifier both punish.
- **No real people, no faces with features, no AI voice or AI music.** Each would move the episode into a category with a rule attached.
- **Say so every time.** The description block travels with every episode that uses AI imagery, adjusted to what was actually done.
