## 1. Music under narration: the experiments

- **The founding study is narrower than its reputation.** [Moreno & Mayer 2000](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Moreno_Mayer00.pdf): two experiments, 75 students each, novices only (anyone with prior knowledge was replaced), a narrated animation shown once at the system's pace (180 s on lightning, 45 s on brakes), on headphones. The music was a 20-second instrumental loop the authors themselves describe as bland; they state the narration stayed clearly audible and was not masked. Music still lowered recall and transfer in both experiments (Exp. 1 transfer: 1.54 solutions with music, 2.84 without). No level in dB is reported and nothing longer than three minutes was tested.
- **Sound effects split by kind in the same paper.** Seven natural sounds, each played once and matched one-to-one to an event (wind, thunder), did not hurt (Exp. 1 transfer 3.06 with, 2.74 without). Two mechanical sounds repeated throughout a 45-second animation did (Exp. 2 transfer 3.06 with, 5.55 without). The authors' reading: repeated, arbitrary sounds are the costly kind, and the more relevant and integrated a sound is, the less it costs.
- **Pooled evidence is mixed and mostly not about narration.** Adults, all tasks: null overall with a small memory cost, [Kämpfe et al. 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735610376261) *(abstract)*. Learners, 47 studies to 2021: a small positive average (d = 0.31), with a slight edge for classical pieces, [de la Mora Velasco et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03057356231153070) *(abstract)*. Reading, 36 studies: g = −0.19 for music overall, about −0.35 with lyrics, near zero for instrumental, [Vasilev et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139986/). None isolates an instrumental bed under a spoken argument. The one pooling that tries puts the memory cost where music competes with narration (g = −0.46, five effects) and near zero elsewhere, but it is single-author, its peer-review status is unclear, and the author declares he sells background music for video: [Amato, Aug 2026](https://culturaesaude.med.br/en/background-music-increases-viewer-engagement-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/). A pointer, not a finding.
- **Who pays, and when.** Low working-memory learners understood less with music; high-capacity learners did not, [Lehmann & Seufert 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5671572/) (n = 81, fast instrumental pop). In real 14–19 minute teaching videos a slow (64 bpm) ambient instrumental bed hurt the easier video, did nothing for the harder one, made both feel less demanding, and did not raise liking or motivation, [Meyerhoff et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9438891/) (n = 175). Across Mayer's whole corpus, removing seductive detail remains among the largest effects, [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927).
- **Turning it down is not taking it out.** Background sound disrupted verbal short-term memory equally at 60 and 75 dB(A); what mattered was how much the sound changes from moment to moment, [Ellermeier & Hellbrück 1998](https://pred.uni-regensburg.de/id/eprint/49466/) *(abstract)*. That is serial recall, not comprehension, so an analogy only; it predicts a held pad costs less than a moving piano line at the same level. As a masker of speech, piano was worst low and fast, least bad high and slow, [Ekström & Borg 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21768731) *(abstract)*.
- **Engagement and perceived quality: no measured benefit so far.** Six widely viewed data videos with and without their music, preregistered: no consistent effect on engagement, recall or persuasion; viewers split between finding it distracting and finding it helpful, [Djavaherpour et al., CHI 2026](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/37604/). A companion preprint: stock music made one video more persuasive, did not raise engagement, and viewers reported stronger emotions without music, [Gomasta et al., Jan 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17893). I found no study that measures watch time with and without a bed. What does move perceived credibility is the quality of the voice recording itself, [Newman & Schwarz 2018](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/67350c38-062b-4837-84c9-81419829d59d) *(abstract)*.

## 2. Small sounds on graphic reveals

- **No study tests them.** I found no experiment on UI-style sounds (clicks, ticks, settles) on graphic reveals in a narrated video, for learning, engagement or perceived quality. The nearest evidence follows.
- **A synchronised sound pulls the eye to whatever changes at that instant.** A tone carrying no location makes the visual change it coincides with pop out, automatically, even when that item is irrelevant, [Van der Burg et al. 2008](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/pip-and-pop-non-spatial-auditory-signals-improve-spatial-visual-s/) *(excerpt)*. Later work reports the binding works for one event at a time ([excerpt](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393717)). So one hit on one reveal is a pointer; a run of hits is not.
- **Meaning decides cost.** § 1's split is the closest experimental analogue: event-matched natural sounds were harmless, repeated arbitrary ones harmful. A click that means nothing is nearer the second kind. In broadcast research, story-relevant effects helped normal-hearing listeners follow speech in noise, helped only about half of hard-of-hearing listeners, and cost the speech the equivalent of 4.5 dB, [Ward et al. 2017](https://hub.salford.ac.uk/accessibleaudio/2017/05/02/audio-engineering-society-uk-snap-crackle-and-pop-understanding-the-relationship-between-dialogue-intelligibility-and-sfx-for-accessible-broadcast-audio) *(excerpt; preliminary conference work, figures drawn from two related papers by the same group)*.

## 3. What practitioners say they do

First-hand accounts exist mainly for radio. None of them gives a level in dB or LU.

- **This American Life (2015).** [Menjivar](https://transom.org/2015/using-music-jonathan-menjivar-for-this-american-life/), a producer who also served as the show's music supervisor: cues usually run a minute to a minute and a half; go about as long without music as with it, and several unscored minutes count as a win; music enters part-way through rising action at a chosen spot rather than creeping up; it comes out just before the idea you want heard; a *post* (music alone) is typically 4–7 s; simple, neutral pieces; busy drums get dropped.
- **NPR Throughline (2020).** [Arablouei](https://journalismcourses.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Interview_2_Sound_design_101_from_Ramtin_Arablouei_of_the_podcast_Throughline.pdf): every sound needs a reason you could state, otherwise leave it out; "restraint is as important as laying it on"; effects are blended so that no single one is noticed (pointing at individual sounds is, to him, old radio theatre); music comes in slightly before the turn it marks; Planet Money mostly runs without music and holds attention by rhythm alone.
- **Vox (2020).** [Fong](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/): library music, fitted by trial and error (she reports stopping to change a track about every 20 seconds while editing); the starts and ends of tracks act as cues to the viewer; she drops tracks whose instruments sit at the pitch of the voice. Nothing on effects.
- **Film mixing (2005).** [Murch](https://transom.org/2005/walter-murch/): speech is *encoded* sound and music *embodied*, so the two coexist more easily than speech with speech; effects sit between; about two and a half layers of one kind, about five overall; to add something, take something else away, and music is what he drops when words must be heard.
- **Motion-design sound (podcast transcript, undated).** [Sono Sanctus on School of Motion](https://www.schoolofmotion.com/blog/sono-sanctus-podcast-podcast): explainers can be fine on music and voice with no sound design; once music starts, effects must take up less space; whooshes are dull but functional and are meant not to draw attention.
- **Kurzgesagt.** Its [process page](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/) (undated) says only that music and effects come last and matter. **Documentary mixers:** the one current interview found ([Andrew Wilson, Mix, June 2026](https://www.mixonline.com/post-and-broadcast/mixer-talks-tools-for-tv-docs)) is about meters; subtler dynamics than film, no narration-to-music figure.
- **No first-hand account found** for 3Blue1Brown, Nerdwriter, LEMMiNO, Johnny Harris, Wendover or Every Frame a Painting on their own mixes. **No source of any kind gives effect density on motion graphics.** The bed figures that circulate (roughly 18 to 25 dB under the voice) come from vendor blogs with no stated basis.

## 4. Standards and numbers

- **Speech over music, tested.** [Torcoli et al. 2019](https://cris.fau.de/publications/234640600) *(abstract)* found no documented ducking practice, so measured preference (22 listeners): at least 10 LU between commentary and music, 15 LU over ambience; non-experts wanted about 4 LU more than experts; individual preference varied widely.
- **Accessibility.** [WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.7](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/low-or-no-background-audio) (Level AAA): background at least 20 dB below speech (occasional sounds of one or two seconds are exempt), or no background, or a way to switch it off. Its scope is audio-only content, so for us it is a benchmark, not a requirement.
- **Where music stops being heard.** On a TV, background music was *barely audible* between about 23 and 36 dB below the foreground and inaudible beyond; headphone listeners reacted fastest, [Batlle-Roca et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9819249/).
- **EBU and BBC.** [EBU R 128 v5, Nov 2023](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf): −23.0 LUFS programme loudness; true peak no higher than −1 dBTP in production, with lower ceilings possible for data-reduced distribution; measured over the whole mix, not the speech. Streaming is deferred to [R 128 s2](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r128s2) (v3, Nov 2023), which notes streaming services run louder than broadcast. The BBC promised recommendations on speech-to-background levels in [Jan 2014](https://tech.ebu.ch/news/bbcs-cohen-welcomes-major-paradigm-shift-27jan14); the only figures I could find are second-hand: music down 4 dB after the 2011 complaints about a Brian Cox series ([Broadcast](https://www.broadcastnow.co.uk/audio-experts-call-for-caution/5025307.article), excerpt), 3 dB plus avoid lyrics and heavy percussion ([Salford dataset note](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/3636737/effect-of-background-music-arrangement-and-tempo-on-foreground-speech-intelligibility-listening-experiment-data-and-statistical-analyses-spreadsheets), excerpt), and a DPP minimum of 4 LU between dialogue and background with speech loudness range within 6 LU in factual programmes ([Production Expert 2018](https://www.production-expert.com/home-page/2018/8/9/loudness-and-dialog-intelligibility-in-tv-mixes-are-tv-mixes-becoming-to-cinematic), excerpt).
- **Streaming specs.** Netflix: dialogue-gated −27 LKFS ± 2 LU, −2 dBTP ([spec](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001794307-Netflix-Sound-Mix-Specifications-Best-Practices-v1-5), excerpt; the page would not open). AES TD1008 (2021), now AES77-2023, for audio-only streaming: speech −18 LUFS, music −16 LUFS ([Telos summary](https://docs.telosalliance.com/docs/understanding-loudness-for-streaming-audio); [Radio World, July 2023](https://www.radioworld.com/?p=121334)). Neither governs YouTube.
- **YouTube as it works now.** YouTube publishes no loudness target. Its only official text is on [Stable volume](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14106294?hl=en) (live page, © 2026): on by default, continuously evening out loud and quiet, off for music; it reached TVs in June 2024 ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/youtube-stable-volume-android-tv-3454090), excerpt). The −14 LUFS figure, and the behaviour that louder uploads are turned down while quieter ones are never turned up, come from reading *Stats for nerds*: [Production Advice 2017](https://productionadvice.co.uk/stats-for-nerds/) and [Youlean 2020](https://youlean.co/?p=30813). **Both are older than 2024.** A [July 2025 creator blog](https://blog.remasterify.com/audio-normalization-reasons-your-uploads-sound-quiet/) (excerpt) repeats them. I found no measurement of what Stable volume does to a voice-over-bed mix.
- **Sync tolerance.** [ITU-R BT.1359-1](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-1-199811-I!!PDF-E.pdf) (1998): sound arriving early is detectable from about 45 ms, sound arriving late only from about 125 ms. Tested on a newsreader's lips, so an analogy for effects.

## 5. Sound at seams

- **No experiment on J or L cuts.** I found none on how far audio may lead or trail a cut. Lead times in circulation, including our 0.7 s default, are editors' rules of thumb.
- **Continuity blurs a seam; it does not hide a real change of scene.** The finding we already hold ([Smith & Martin-Portugues Santacreu 2017](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21152/)) states no mechanism in its abstract. Adjacent results: across 24 films, viewers agreed less on where events begin and end while music was playing (d = 0.22, correlational), [Cutting 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6675763); and cuts at scene boundaries were detected equally well with or without the soundtrack, [Meitz, Meyerhoff & Huff 2020](https://bibliographie.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/126265) *(abstract)*.
- **Sound that crosses a seam is a link, and a visible one.** [Bordwell 2008](https://davidbordwell.net/essays/hook.php): a line or sound at the end of one scene answered at the start of the next ties the scenes together and makes the narration more overt, not less.
- **Practice agrees on leading with sound, not on amounts.** Menjivar varies entrances (mid-sentence, in the clear, a few seconds early); Arablouei starts music before the thing it underlines; Fong treats a track change as the cue.
- **An effect on a transition draws the eye to the transition.** That follows from § 2's pop-out result (inference: no study tests a whoosh on a cut). The motion-design account says the same from the other side: something should be there, as unremarkable as possible. I found no source arguing that an effect makes a cut less visible.

## 7. What this changes for us

The current plan scores about 88% of the runtime (bed from 1:04 to 11:12, less 11 s of silence) and places 64 non-speech events, one every 10.6 s (23 transition sounds, 41 texture hits).

| Layer | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| L1 music bed | **Keep, thin.** Cues, not a bed: toward half the runtime scored. | A continuous instrumental bed under narrated explanation has a real if debated cost to recall, falls hardest on weaker listeners and easier passages, and has no measured benefit for engagement. The people who score narrated argument for a living run cues of a minute or so, leave about as much unscored, and take music out before the line that matters. |
| L2 transition sounds | **Thin hard.** 23 to about 4, plus beat boundaries marked by the music itself. | A new sound at a seam marks the seam and pulls the eye to it. That is right at a chapter break and wrong inside a beat, which matches the seam table we already adopted (hard cut, no whoosh; chapter break with a music change or a beat of silence). |
| L3 texture hits | **Cut for this episode.** 41 to 0–3. | No evidence of benefit exists; the nearest evidence says repeated, arbitrary sounds are the costly kind; each one captures attention automatically under a spoken argument; and our own checklist requires them to be inaudible on laptop speakers, so the best case is that they do nothing. |

**L1 in practice.** Take music out first where a diagram and the narration are both carrying new reasoning: the Beat 4 table and timeline, and above all Beat 7 from 8:04 to 9:41, where the plan currently *adds* a drone layer and a build under the hardest explanation in the episode. Keep it under the story passages over stills (shopkeeper, rescue, ring, car), at the 6:08 return, and through the resolution. The silent cold open and the 10-second silence before the definition are already what the practitioners describe. If Beats 4 and 7 go mostly unscored, `bed-analytical` may not need sourcing: three beds, not four. When choosing tracks, prefer stems, sustained textures under speech with piano saved for the gaps, nothing low and busy, nothing sitting at the narrator's pitch (the last three are inference from § 1 and Fong).

**L2 in practice.** Keep the rise and lock on the 40× figure (the one designed event), the landing of the title phrase out of silence at 6:08, and the end stinger. Let a music entry, change or exit carry the seven beat boundaries. Cut the four quote bells, the three within-beat colour-wash swells, the two within-beat whooshes, the second stat reveal and the rise-and-resolve at 9:36–9:41. Files to source drop from 13 to about 4.

**L3 in practice.** Two candidates are worth trying by ear because the sound means something: a message arriving on the chat card at 0:09, and paper on the EPA notice at 6:49. If kept, make them audible.

**Numbers to mix to**

- **Master:** −14 LUFS integrated for the full mix; true peak at or below −1 dBTP, −2 dBTP for margin through the transcode. Quieter than −14 is probably left quiet by YouTube, louder is turned down.
- **Narration:** the anchor. Keep its loudness range within about 6 LU (second-hand DPP figure).
- **Bed under speech:** 16–20 LU below the narration, measured stem against stem over the same passage; start at 18. Never closer than 10 LU (Torcoli's floor); 14 LU is that floor adjusted for non-expert listeners; 20 is the WCAG benchmark. Past about 23 it enters the barely-audible band on a TV: at that point take it out rather than keep lowering it.
- **Bed in the clear:** no source gives a level. Start at +3 dB over the under-speech level, not +6, since Stable volume probably lifts quiet passages anyway (inference).
- **Transition sounds:** at most two seconds, in a gap between phrases, never over a word that carries the argument. Level by ear; no standard gives a number.
- **Sync for any effect:** on the frame or up to three frames late at 30 fps; never more than one frame early.
- **Gain is not loudness.** The cue sheet's `volume` values (0.11–0.13 is −19 to −18 dB of gain; hits at 0.06–0.08 are −24 to −22 dB) multiply files of unknown loudness. They do not say where the bed sits against the voice. Measure the stems.

**Only the ear can settle**

- Whether a track's register sits on top of Tiger's voice.
- Whether 18 LU down reads as music or as murk on a phone speaker; check phone, laptop, TV and headphones.
- Where each cue enters and leaves relative to the sentence, and how long the posts and silences run.
- Whether the chapter seams feel marked enough with a music change and no whoosh, and whether the 0.7 s narration lead is right once the narration exists.
- Whether 64 seconds without music at the top reads as deliberate.
- What Stable volume does to the upload: publish unlisted, compare with it on and off, read Stats for nerds.

**Lines in `AUDIO_DESIGN.md` these findings contradict (not edited)**

- Avoid silence longer than 3 seconds, and the checklist's no gaps over 3 seconds: the opposite of documented radio practice.
- Every beat boundary gets at least a subtle transition sound, and the quotas of 15–25 transition sounds and 30–50 texture hits: counts with no basis I could find; the working test is a stated reason per sound.

- Two different texture levels in one document (15–20 dB below narration in the rules, −24 to −30 dB in the hierarchy).
- All music transitions as 1–3 second fades: Menjivar advises against fading music up slowly at entrances.
- The iris rows in the transition-to-sound tables are stale now that iris is retired.
