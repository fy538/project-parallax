# Verification Architecture for AI-Assisted Longform Video Essays

## Executive judgment

The current frontier stack is already useful for your pipeline, but not yet safe to treat as a citation-authority layer. The best public evidence I found says the dominant failure is **not** just “the model made something up”; it is a family of source-linked failures: invented references, corrupted metadata, valid links attached to the wrong claim, dead or never-existent URLs, altered quotations, and wrong numbers or dates presented with confidence. In other words, retrieval helps, but “has citations” is still far from “is verified.” citeturn32view0turn31view1turn25view4turn17news25turn16view0

For a solo geopolitical essayist, the practical implication is simple: use LLMs as **brief generators, search expanders, and contradiction finders**, not as final arbiters of what a source says. If the script contains a direct quote, a date, a percentage, a ranking, or a “scholars say” clause, that item still needs a source-level check by you or by a tightly constrained audit pass that has the source text in view. The literature supports adding exactly that kind of intermediate verification layer between research output and script draft. citeturn32view0turn24view0turn14view10turn14view12turn14view16

The literature also now gives a more useful taxonomy than the old undifferentiated “hallucination” label. A 2026 failure-mode paper on fabricated references in published NeurIPS papers breaks reference failures into **total fabrication**, **partial attribute corruption**, **identifier hijacking**, **semantic hallucination**, and **placeholder hallucination**. Pair that with the newer deep-research work and you get a practical two-level taxonomy: **reference-level errors** and **claim-level support errors**. The first class invents or corrupts the citation object; the second uses a real source to support a sentence it does not actually support. citeturn30view2turn30view5turn31view1turn24view0

One important gap remains. The public 2024–2026 literature is now fairly good on **reference validity**, **URL validity**, **source support**, and **short factual QA**, but it is still relatively thin on direct, head-to-head benchmarking of **exact book-quote fidelity**. The best publicly available proxies are the BBC quote-distortion findings and the source-support benchmarks, which strongly suggest that exact-quote trust should be treated as a separate verification problem, not assumed solved by citation grounding. citeturn17news25turn32view0turn24view0

A related nuance: benchmarks like **SimpleQA** and **SimpleQA Verified** are useful for measuring raw fact recall, and they do include a lot of the answer types you care about—**dates** and **numbers** account for **22%** and **19%** of SimpleQA Verified, respectively—but they do **not** test citation grounding. They tell you something about a model’s memory and calibration, not whether its attached source actually backs the statement. citeturn38view0turn39view0turn39view1

## A five-step audit protocol for your research-audit stage

Your current “deep-research → research-audit → angle-memo” architecture is already well shaped. What it needs is a narrower definition of the audit. I would make the audit stage answer five explicit questions, in this order.

### Citation existence lens

For every citation the model gives you, verify that the source object exists **as cited**. That means title, author, year, venue, DOI/ISBN/URL all match the same real object. This catches fabricated references, metadata corruption, and identifier hijacking—the three most common reference-level failures in the recent literature. If any citation fails this gate, the memo does **not** advance unchanged. citeturn20search0turn27view0turn30view5

### Source-support lens

For every paragraph in the research memo, verify that the cited source supports the **precise proposition**, not just the general topic. This is the single most important lesson from SourceCheckup and the newer source-attribution benchmarks: a working link to a relevant page still leaves a lot of room for unsupported sentence-level claims. If you use a second LLM here, give it the raw claim *and the source text*, and make the human the final adjudicator. citeturn32view0turn24view0turn25view4

### Quote-date-number lens

Automatically extract every **direct quote**, **date**, **percentage**, **magnitude claim**, **superlative** (“largest,” “first,” “only”), and **timed causal sequence** from the memo and line-check each against the source. This is the fastest way to catch the most reputation-damaging failures: invented or altered quotations, wrong experiment dates, and statistical claims that are off by a digit, a year, or a denominator. The BBC studies are especially relevant here: quote distortions, number errors, and date errors remain common even in citation-bearing outputs. citeturn17news25turn36news29

### Disagreement lens

Force every nontrivial interpretive claim into one of three buckets: **consensus**, **active dispute**, or **unclear / mixed evidence**. This is how you stop models from flattening scholarly disagreement into a false singular view. If the claim is about historian interpretation, strategic causation, policy effects, or a contested empirical estimate, the audit must find at least one counter-source or explicit note of uncertainty before the angle memo gets written. This is the most direct defense against “smoothed-over disagreement presented as consensus.” citeturn14view12turn14view16turn13search3

### Temporal freshness lens

Any claim that depends on live institutions, recent events, current officeholders, evolving casualty counts, fresh economic indicators, or newly released archives gets a time-stamp check before it enters script. This is not just a general freshness issue; the agent studies show that deeper search often increases citation volume while also creating stale or hallucinated URLs, and the news studies show that outdated information is still a recurring error class. Your audit should therefore mark every time-sensitive claim with **verified as of [date]** in the memo itself. citeturn31view1turn36news29

In practice, I would turn those into five pass/fail fields in your research-audit template:

| Audit field | Minimum pass condition |
| --- | --- |
| Citation exists | Every cited work resolves as a real object with matching metadata |
| Source supports claim | Every paragraph’s lead claim is supported by an inspected source |
| Quote/date/number exactness | All direct quotes, dates, and numerical claims are line-checked |
| Disagreement handled | Contested claims are marked as contested, not narrated as consensus |
| Freshness checked | Time-sensitive claims are marked with verified-as-of date |

If you want the **minimum viable** version, verify **every quote, date, number, and named paper/book**, plus the lead claim of every paragraph. If you want the **standard essay** version, add disagreement and freshness tagging. If you want the **flagship / controversial geopolitics** version, keep a private evidence appendix with source excerpts or page references for every nontrivial on-screen factual assertion. The incremental time cost is real, but it is much smaller than the reputational cost of one invented Schelling quote or one wrongly dated experiment. citeturn20search0turn30view5turn17news25turn14view12

## Open questions and limits

Three parts of your brief remain under-measured in the public literature I reviewed. First, there is still no strong public benchmark specifically for **exact book-quote fidelity** across frontier models; most work measures source support and citation validity rather than quote-perfect passage retrieval. Second, public disclosures from creators are much better on **workflow boundaries** than on their literal **verification checklists**, so the creator case studies above are more revealing about discipline patterns than about line-by-line SOPs. The highest-confidence conclusion, though, is already clear enough to act on: **the winning move is not to abandon AI research tooling, but to narrow the job description of the model and harden the audit stage around source existence, source support, quote/date/number exactness, disagreement, and freshness.** That is the smallest protocol likely to catch the failure modes most likely to blow up an intellectually rigorous essay channel. citeturn32view0turn31view1turn24view0turn14view12turn14view16
