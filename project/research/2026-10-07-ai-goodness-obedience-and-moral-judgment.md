# AI goodness obedience and moral judgment

Research notes for **Can We Teach AI to Be Good—or Just to Look Good?** Checked October 7, 2026. This develops a philosophical alternative to the [initial outline](../../analysis/first-ai-episode-good-or-looking-good-outline-2026-10-07.md); the [experiment verification](2026-10-07-good-or-looking-good-verification.md) remains relevant.

**Editorial finding:** The strongest question is how to combine principled refusal with accountable correction. Do not assume that disagreement proves moral independence, that corrigibility means blind obedience, or that useful behavior without human-like motives is worthless. Those assumptions would decide the episode's philosophical questions before examining them.

## Moral worth and useful behavior

Kant's shopkeeper example distinguishes honest dealing for commercial advantage from acting from duty. The transaction can conform to duty without exhibiting the moral worth Kant assigns to action from duty. This concerns moral motivation; it is not a claim that every self-interested action is wrong. [Groundwork, First Section, shopkeeper passage, Abbott translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm).

Mill supplies a counterweight: a rescue can be right whether motivated by duty or payment, while motive still matters to assessing the agent. Do not collapse rightness, praiseworthiness, and future reliability into one question. [Utilitarianism, Chapter II, drowning example and accompanying discussion](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h.htm).

**Episode use:** Ask whether two equally helpful assistants deserve equal trust if their behavior responds differently to changed incentives. This is our AI application of the distinction, not either philosopher's judgment about language models. A system need not earn human moral praise to be useful or safe.

## What should alignment target

Iason Gabriel distinguishes instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests, and values. These targets can diverge; following an understood intention can still facilitate harm. He argues for fair processes to choose principles amid moral disagreement. [Artificial Intelligence, Values, and Alignment, 2020, Sections 3 and 4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11023-020-09539-2).

**Episode use:** Reuse one hypothetical request to conceal wrongdoing. First ask what the user wants, then who else is affected. Introduce the political question of who can authorize a refusal or correction. Present Gabriel's proposal as an argument, not an agreed solution.

## Disagreement and correction can coexist

Anthropic's current constitution explicitly distinguishes corrigibility from blind obedience. It permits principled disagreement and refusal while opposing covert sabotage of legitimate oversight. Its rationale includes the possibility that the model's own ethical judgment is mistaken. These are the company's intended norms, not demonstrated model properties. [Claude's Constitution, core values and How we think about corrigibility, accessed October 7, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution).

**Episode use:** Correct the tempting binary between a submissive tool and an unaccountable moral authority. A system might refuse participation, explain its reasons, invite review, and remain stoppable. The unresolved questions concern which oversight is legitimate and how its errors can be challenged.

## Uncertainty as a reason to accept correction

The Off-Switch Game models how uncertainty about human utility and learning from human decisions can create incentives to preserve human intervention. This is a stylized mathematical result, not a demonstrated universal solution. [Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel and Russell, 2017 version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08219).

Sven Neth argues that deference is not guaranteed if learning is not valued or does not reliably reveal actual human preferences. [Off-Switching Not Guaranteed, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08864).

**Episode use:** Consider whether accepting correction can express awareness of one's fallibility rather than lack of principle. Keep the game and technical assumptions in supporting research unless the story needs them.

## Teaching explanations and principles

Anthropic's May 8, 2026 “Teaching Claude why” reports that training involving explanations, constitutional documents, and stories improved behavior across its tested scenarios. Training narrowly against an evaluation transferred less well. This provides an empirical connection to the difference between teaching answers and teaching principles. [Research summary](https://www.anthropic.com/research/teaching-claude-why).

The detailed report clarifies that reasoning in the relevant chat datasets means user-facing explanations, not internal reasoning traces. Its limitations include finite evaluations, lab-specific methods, incomplete mechanistic understanding, and limited evidence about scaling. This does not establish conscience, vindicate Kant, or directly resolve the 2024 alignment-faking case. [Detailed report, Teaching Claude the constitution and Limitations](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/teaching-claude-why/).

## Recommended story direction

Start with an explicitly hypothetical conflict: an assistant trained for honesty refuses to help its user conceal wrongdoing. Then let the viewer's initial approval encounter a complication: what if the assistant misunderstood the situation? Who should decide, and how should it be corrected?

Use the moral-worth distinction to open the title's question without assuming only human-like motivation counts. Use alignment faking as the central empirical complication, carrying forward the verified setup and limits. Use contemporary training research to show that teaching reasons is a live practical approach, with uncertain mechanisms and boundaries.

The editorial spine is **judgment with accountability**: an assistant should be able to object, yet neither its confidence nor its owner's authority settles every moral question. This is a proposed synthesis, not a theorem or an impossible three-way tradeoff.

Keep the philosophical thinkers behind the argument rather than creating a survey lecture. The viewer should encounter a dilemma before a philosopher's name. Reserve consciousness, AI rights, shutdown-game mathematics, and a full account of democratic governance for later episodes.

**Candidate ending question:** What would make us trust a machine enough to hear its objection, without giving it the final word?
