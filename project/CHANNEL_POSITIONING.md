## Visual Identity statement (for press / About / FAQ)

When asked "what's that art style?" — the answer should pull the question into design-history territory, not political-iconography territory.

### Standard answer

> Parallax draws on the 20th-century constructivist editorial-design family — the shared graphic discipline carried by Bauhaus design school, American mid-century editorial modernism (Saul Bass title sequences, Push Pin Studios, Charley Harper, *Fortune* magazine industrial-modernism), British industrial modernism (E. McKnight Kauffer), and Japanese Showa-era graphic design (Yusaku Kamekura), alongside Soviet Constructivism (Rodchenko, Lissitzky), German political photomontage (John Heartfield), and 20th-century industrial woodcut (Frans Masereel).
>
> Each episode picks the cultural inflection appropriate to its content. Soviet visual rhetoric for Soviet-bloc content, deliberately and transparently — when we render a 1972 Soviet rocket factory in Russian Constructivist style, the visual rhetoric is doing analytical work, not endorsement. American mid-century restraint (Saul Bass, Push Pin) for contemporary American tech and Cold War content. Chinese-state visual language with traditional Chinese vermillion (distinct from Soviet crimson) for Chinese contemporary scenes. The visual layer participates in the analytical argument the narration makes.
>
> The aesthetic is built around three editorial principles: every visual surface earns its presence (no decoration), the illustration vocabulary stays consistent (channel identity through graphic discipline), and the cultural inflection per episode is deliberate rather than aesthetic-by-default.

## Guidance for early episode framing

The visual language teaches itself across episodes through repetition. EP01 doesn't need to explain itself; viewers will pattern-match by EP05. But EP01 *should* avoid the political-aesthetic readings that come from premature deployment of culturally loaded imagery without context.

**EP01 recommended visual approach** (whatever the topic):

1. **Lead with American Modernist or neutral palette emphasis** unless the topic is explicitly Soviet-bloc. Push Pin / Saul Bass / *Fortune* discipline reads as "smart American editorial design" — broadly positive default associations. Saves Soviet/Chinese/Japanese propaganda emphasis for episodes whose content explicitly engages those visual rhetorics.

2. **In the cold open or first 2 minutes, deploy at least one moment of self-aware visual sophistication** that signals "this channel makes deliberate aesthetic choices." A Saul Bass-style title sequence, a deliberate compositional homage, a typography moment that displays craft. Tells the viewer "the design is the design" — not accidental, not generic, not ideologically loaded.

3. **Avoid aggressive propaganda tropes** in EP01: low-angle hero figures, fist-raising, banner-holding crowd scenes, monumentalist scale on individuals, saturated revolutionary-red dominance. Save for episodes whose content explicitly engages these tropes.

4. **The brand mark ∴ should appear early and often.** Title card, lower-third metadata, footer. Cumulative recognition starts immediately.

5. **Voiceover should at some point analytically frame the visual language.** Not heavy-handed ("today we're going to talk about constructivist design") but a moment where the imagery being shown gets named analytically. "The Soviets built this rocket factory under five-year-plan logic — the same propaganda visual rhetoric we're showing you was the visual rhetoric the workers themselves saw." This is the Adam Curtis move at smaller scale: when the channel deploys politically loaded visual language, the narration positions it analytically rather than letting it stand as channel aesthetic.

## Episode-level positioning rules

For episodes that deploy Soviet, Chinese-propaganda, or Japanese Showa visual emphasis (i.e., culturally loaded imagery):

1. **Name the visual rhetoric in narration explicitly** — "this is the visual language Soviet workers themselves saw" / "Cultural Revolution propaganda traditions" / "Showa-era state imagery." Removes ambiguity about whether the channel endorses or analyzes.

2. **Pair with American or Bauhaus framing in the same beat** — juxtaposition makes the comparison the argument. Adam Curtis's signature move. The episode's structural argument is ABOUT the recurrence of state-power visual rhetoric across regimes; the visual layer carries that argument by showing the rhetoric.

3. **Include the editorial guardrail moment** — somewhere in the episode, name what would change your mind about the structural parallel being drawn. Per NAR-19. This explicit falsifiability anchors the analytical posture against any reading of ideological alignment.

4. **End with personal stakes, not propaganda intensity.** The closing beat should pull back to the viewer's actual life ("your phone has 3,000 chips") — keeps the channel's voice distinct from the propaganda traditions it deploys analytically.

## Maintenance

When the channel adds new content categories or ships episodes that meaningfully shift the visual identity (e.g., starts using a new typography tradition, introduces a recurring motif character, establishes a new format like Q&A episodes):

1. Update the channel description to reflect the new dimension.
2. Update the Visual Identity statement to name the new lineage references.
3. Update the social media bio if the change is significant enough to be worth surfacing.
4. Note the positioning shift in PROJECT_VISION.md or DECISIONS.md.

This doc is the public-facing identity layer. PROJECT_VISION.md is the internal-facing identity layer. The two should be coherent (no contradiction between what we tell ourselves and what we tell viewers) but they serve different audiences.
