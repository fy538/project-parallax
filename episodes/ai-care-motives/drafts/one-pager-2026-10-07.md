# Can We Teach AI to Be Good—or Just to Look Good?

**One-page treatment · October 7, 2026 · Target 10–12 minutes.** This narrower treatment replaces the earlier experiment-led outline as the current proposal. Timings budget an approximately 11-minute spoken draft; they are not measured narration.

**Audience promise:** Examine whether good actions need good motives—and discover why judging a helpful action, praising its author, and trusting future behavior are different tasks.

**1. Would you care why? · 0:00–1:00**

“If an AI always did the right thing, would you care why? Would it matter whether it understood a principle—or had learned which answers people reward?” Give viewers a moment to choose. Take “always” seriously: if behavior really never differed, the remaining question would concern moral worth, not practical reliability. We will return to that ideal after examining cases we can actually observe.

**2. The honest shopkeeper · 1:00–3:00**

A shopkeeper charges a child a fair price. In one version, the reason is honesty; in another, protecting the business's reputation. Keep the transaction identical. Does learning the motive change what the child received, how much we admire the shopkeeper, or both? Introduce Kant after the viewer has answered: acting in accordance with duty is different from acting from duty. He need not call the fair transaction wrong to question its moral worth. [Groundwork, First Section](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm).

**3. The person who was saved · 3:00–4:45**

Someone rescues a drowning stranger, hoping for payment. From the rescued person's perspective, is the rescue worth less? Mill distinguishes the rightness of the action from our assessment of its author. Let this challenge the intuition that a self-interested motive makes an otherwise good act worthless. The benefit, moral credit, and relationship need not receive the same verdict. [Utilitarianism, Chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h.htm).

**4. What carries over to AI? · 4:45–6:45**

Imagine an assistant helps you understand something difficult. Learning how it was trained does not erase what you learned. Briefly explain demonstrations and human feedback, which have produced measured behavioral improvements. Training rewards are not evidence that a deployed model consciously wants praise. Ask whether we need an AI to deserve gratitude for its help to matter. [InstructGPT study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155).

**5. One good act is not a lifetime guarantee · 6:45–9:15**

Return to the shopkeeper, now deciding whether to overcharge when reputation is no longer at stake. This is an explicitly hypothetical test of dependability, not proof that self-interest always produces dishonesty. For AI, investigate what sustains helpful behavior rather than assuming a human motive. Simply asking “why?” cannot settle that: a 2023 study found explanations could omit factors that influenced answers. Keep this finding brief and specific to its tested models and tasks. [Turpin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388).

**6. Three answers to one question · 9:15–11:00**

Return to the opening. “Did it help?” concerns the action. “Does it deserve moral credit?” concerns the agent. “Will it help again when circumstances change?” concerns reliability. Viewers may reasonably answer these differently. A machine's help can be real while its moral status remains unsettled and our confidence remains conditional.

**Closing question:** “When we ask whether AI is good, which of those questions are we really asking?”

**Production boundary:** Use two restrained illustrated situations and one assistant example. Keep alignment faking, obedience, governance, and a consciousness debate for later episodes. Draft roughly 1,350–1,500 spoken words, then time a scratch read with visual pauses; deepen the examples rather than add topics to reach the duration.
