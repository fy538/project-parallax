# EP01 — THE SILICON TRAP
## Narration Script v3 (English)
### Target length: ~18 minutes (~2,700 words at analytical narration pace)

**[BEAT 1 — THE PARADOX: 0:00–3:00]**

*[VISUAL: Aerial shot of TSMC Arizona construction site. Zoom into cleanroom footage.]*

In December 2025, TSMC's first Arizona fab hit a 92% chip yield — four percentage points higher than the equivalent line in Taiwan.

A fab — a fabrication plant — is where chips are physically made. Not designed, not packaged. Made. Buildings that cost tens of billions of dollars, where the air is a thousand times cleaner than a hospital operating room, where a single speck of dust can ruin a wafer worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. There are only a handful of companies on earth that can build one. And now there was one in the Arizona desert, and it was working.

*[VISUAL: Cleanroom macro footage — wafer handling, lithography machines.]*

One hundred and sixty-five billion dollars in total investment. The most expensive foreign direct investment project in American history. By every engineering metric, it was a success.

By every strategic metric, it was a rounding error. That fab covers about seven percent of US chip demand. The chips cost fifty percent more than the ones made in Taiwan. And to get it running, TSMC flew in thousands of Taiwanese engineers — many of them living in temporary housing blocks in the desert, working the same twelve-hour shifts they'd worked in Hsinchu, because there weren't enough trained American technicians to operate the machines.

*[VISUAL: Pull back to wide shot. Graphic overlay: "7% of US chip demand."]*

The flagship achievement of American semiconductor reshoring is both a technical triumph and a strategic drop in the ocean. America can make chips. The question is whether anyone — any country — can rewire the most complex supply chain humans have ever built without breaking it in the process.

---

**[BEAT 2 — THE LOGIC OF DENIAL: 3:00–7:00]**

*[VISUAL: Timeline transition — present day dissolves into 1941 footage.]*

In the summer of 1941, the United States froze Japan's assets and cut off its oil supply. Japan imported roughly eighty percent of its oil from America. The logic was clean: constrain Japanese military expansion in Southeast Asia without firing a shot.

Japan's navy had about eighteen months of fuel reserves. That created a terrifying calculus: use the fleet now, or watch it become a monument. One hundred and thirty-four days later — Pearl Harbor. The resource denial designed to prevent war triggered the exact catastrophe it was meant to avoid.

*[VISUAL: Split-screen — 1941 oil embargo timeline on left, 2022-2026 chip control timeline on right.]*

That was 1941. In 2022, the United States tried it again. Not with oil — with the machines that make the machines that run the modern world.

The first Huawei restrictions came under Trump. Then Biden's team made the intellectual leap — Jake Sullivan's argument was that America shouldn't just stay a couple of generations ahead. It should maintain as large a lead as possible. That was a fundamental shift in posture. The "small yard, high fence" — except the yard kept expanding and the fence kept getting taller.

Then Trump came back and did something nobody predicted. In August 2025, he let Nvidia and AMD sell AI chips to China — in exchange for fifteen percent of the revenue going directly to the US government. He'd originally asked for twenty percent. Jensen Huang talked him down to fifteen.

*[VISUAL: Brief graphic — export controls timeline across three administrations, ending with the revenue-share deal.]*

Export controls — a national security instrument — had become a tax. Possibly an unconstitutional one. In three years the policy went from "deny China access to protect American advantage" to "let China have access, but take a cut." Meanwhile, the CHIPS Act — fifty-two billion dollars authorized to rebuild American manufacturing — had disbursed only six billion. Trump called the whole program "horrible."

So — does technology denial ever actually work? Once. For forty-five years, seventeen Western nations ran a coordinated regime called COCOM that restricted computing and advanced manufacturing to the Soviet Bloc. The USSR never closed the gap. Five to ten years behind, for the entire Cold War.

But the Soviets were economically isolated, technologically stagnant, and systemically dysfunctional in ways that made denial easier. China is none of those things. China is deeply integrated into the global economy, runs the world's most aggressive talent recruitment programs, and has already demonstrated the ability to produce chips at nodes that were supposed to be permanently out of reach.

So one precedent says denial triggers catastrophe. Another says it can work for decades. And the country being denied this time is fundamentally different from either case.

**[BEAT 3 — THE OTHER SIDE OF THE WALL: 7:00–12:30]**

*[VISUAL: Transition — Chinese text overlays, Shenzhen skyline.]*

To understand how China experiences all of this, you need to know a phrase: 卡脖子 — kǎ bózi. It translates roughly as "stranglehold technology." Technologies where a foreign power has you by the throat.

In Chinese public discourse, this isn't abstract policy language. It's visceral. There's a story that became a kind of national parable. Until 2017, China could build high-speed rail, launch astronauts into orbit, assemble the world's fastest supercomputer — but it could not manufacture the tiny ball at the tip of a ballpoint pen. The steel was too specialized. It had to be imported from Japan and Switzerland.

*[VISUAL: The contrast — bullet train, space launch, supercomputer... ballpoint pen.]*

When state media reported this, it became a symbol of everything humiliating about technological dependence. A country that could reach space couldn't make a pen tip. Chips are that story at civilizational scale.

So when the US imposed export controls, it didn't just threaten China's tech industry. It activated a deep reflex — what the Chinese call 举国体制, the whole-nation system. The same mobilization mindset that built the atomic bomb program, the space program, the rail network. Concentrate resources. Tolerate waste. Accept short-term inefficiency for long-term sovereignty.

The question is whether that approach works for semiconductors. And the honest answer is: sort of.

*[VISUAL: SMIC facility. Process diagram showing lithography steps.]*

Consider what SMIC's engineers are doing every day. To make a seven-nanometer chip without EUV lithography — the advanced machines the West denied them — they run each wafer through thirty-four separate lithography passes. A Western fab using an EUV machine does the same job in nine. Every extra pass is another chance for a defect, another source of delay, another cost multiplier. It's like writing a novel by punching out one letter at a time on a typewriter when everyone else has a word processor.

It's more expensive. It's slower. And it works. Yields went from below forty percent to sixty or seventy percent by mid-2025.

But there's a ceiling — and the evidence of that ceiling is written on the chip itself. When Huawei released the Kirin X90 and marketed it as a five-nanometer chip, independent teardowns told a different story. It was seven-nanometer silicon with a new label. The gap between what China claims and what China can build — that gap is the real metric.

*[VISUAL: Teardown comparison graphic — Kirin X90 marketing vs. TechInsights analysis.]*

You can see that same gap — ambition running ahead of capability, then capability catching up in ways nobody expected — compressed into a single story.

In early 2025, a Chinese AI lab called DeepSeek released a model called R1 that was competitive with the leading American systems — built on inferior hardware. In China, this was electric. Vindication. Proof that Chinese ingenuity could route around any restriction America imposed. The mood on Chinese tech forums was something close to euphoria.

Then DeepSeek tried to train their next model, R2, on domestic chips — Huawei's Ascend processors, the ones that were supposed to replace Nvidia. They couldn't complete a single successful training run. Not one. They had to fall back on the very Nvidia hardware the US was trying to restrict.

*[Beat. Let this land.]*

Both of those things are true at the same time. China can innovate brilliantly around constraints. And the constraints are real. The question — the one that determines whether export controls are a historic success or a trillion-dollar mistake — is which of those truths has the longer half-life.

**[BEAT 4 — THE TRAP: 12:30–15:30]**

*[VISUAL: Animated chess board on left, go board on right.]*

There's a metaphor that keeps pulling me back. The US plays chess — targeting specific companies, capturing specific pieces. Nvidia, blacklisted. ASML, restricted. Entity List, expanded. Precise. Aggressive. Focused on chokepoints.

China plays go — wéiqí. And if you haven't played go, the difference matters. You don't win by capturing the king. You win by surrounding territory. Stone by stone, across the entire board. You accept losses in one corner to gain advantage somewhere else. The game rewards patience over tactical brilliance.

The problem is that both players are trapped.

*[VISUAL: Animated supply chain map — a single chip touching six countries.]*

A single advanced chip touches photoresists from Japan, lithography from the Netherlands, design software from California, packaging from Taiwan, rare earths from China. No country can replicate this alone. One estimate puts the cost of full self-sufficiency at a trillion dollars. The extreme globalization that made chips cheap, ubiquitous, and transformative is exactly what makes them strategically vulnerable. And every attempt to reduce that vulnerability — reshoring, controls, stockpiling — undermines the efficiency that made chips matter in the first place.

That's the trap. Not a trap for one side. A trap for everyone.

*[VISUAL: Brief world map — ASML (Netherlands), photoresist (Japan), packaging (Taiwan), rare earths (China).]*

And the countries caught inside the trap are the ones who built it. ASML — the only company on earth that makes EUV lithography machines — watched China drop from thirty-six percent of their revenue to twenty. South Korea has forty billion dollars sunk in Chinese fabs. Japan controls seventy percent of global photoresist. Every one of them is being asked to pick a side in a conflict where picking either side costs them enormously.

Morris Chang understands this better than anyone alive. He founded TSMC. He built the globalized semiconductor supply chain — the very system that's now being torn apart. And when he looked at all of this, what he said was: "Globalization is almost dead. Free trade is almost dead."

If Miller is right — Chris Miller, who wrote *Chip War* in 2022 — then what comes next isn't victory for either side. It's bifurcation. A splitting of the technological world into two partially overlapping, partially incompatible ecosystems. Both less efficient than the integrated system they replaced. Three years later, that looks like the most prescient call anyone made.

---

**[BEAT 5 — YOUR CHIPS: 15:30–17:30]**

*[VISUAL: Montage — car dashboard, phone screen, hospital monitor, data center rack. Each one briefly flashes a chip count.]*

There's a question underneath all of this that nobody can answer, and it changes everything depending on which way it goes. How quickly will transformative AI arrive? If it comes fast — two, three years — then controls may have bought enough time for the US to keep a decisive lead. If it takes a decade, China may build self-sufficiency during that window, and the controls will have accomplished nothing except costing American companies tens of billions of dollars and handing China the motivation to never depend on the West again.

That might sound like someone else's problem — two superpowers and their chess match.

It's not. Every modern car has between a thousand and three thousand chips. Your phone. Your hospital's MRI machine. The data center that serves your email. In 2021, the COVID chip shortage shut down auto factories, delayed medical equipment, and wiped hundreds of billions of dollars from the global economy. That was a supply chain hiccup — a few months of disrupted logistics.

*[VISUAL: COVID shortage impact montage — empty car lots, hospital equipment delays, price spikes.]*

What we're watching now isn't a hiccup. It's a rewiring. Two parallel technological ecosystems emerging — one built on American architecture, one on Chinese alternatives. If they become incompatible, your next phone might not run the same apps as your colleague's in Shanghai. Your company's AI tools might not work across borders. The standards that let a device designed in Seoul, manufactured in Taipei, assembled in Shenzhen, and sold in San Francisco all work together — those standards are what's actually at stake.

The semiconductor supply chain is the most complex thing humans have ever built. And right now, the two most powerful countries on earth are pulling it apart from both ends — each convinced they're making themselves safer, each making the whole system more fragile.

You're already inside this story. You just might not have known it yet.

*[VISUAL: Fade to black. Title card: THE SILICON TRAP / 硅陷阱]*
