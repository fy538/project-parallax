# EP01 — THE SILICON TRAP: Revision Log

This log tracks not just what changed between versions, but *why* — what we learned about storytelling, audience, and our own editorial instincts. The goal is to build a compounding knowledge base about what makes scripts work.

---

## v1 (April 5, 2026)

**What it was:** First complete draft. ~2,500 words, 17 minutes. Five-beat structure: Hook → Historical Pattern → Current Chapter → Bayesian Update → Closing. Lean and fast-moving, heavily data-driven.

**What worked:**
- The opening paradox (TSMC Arizona as triumph *and* drop in the ocean) landed immediately
- Chess vs. go metaphor gave the whole piece a structural spine
- Beat 4's "what we thought vs. what happened" format was a clean way to do Bayesian updating without naming it

**What was missing:**
- No explanation of what a fab actually is — assumed too much viewer knowledge
- China section was thin. Treated China as a reactive player rather than showing its internal logic, motivations, and emotional drivers
- Western-centric framing overall

---

## v1 → v2 (April 5, 2026)

**What changed:**
- Added fab explanation (~80 words) woven into the hook rather than as a parenthetical aside
- Expanded China coverage by ~400 words: added 卡脖子 emotional framing, ballpoint pen parable, 举国体制 mobilization concept, Big Fund corruption details, Made in China 2025 reality gap, DeepSeek R1/R2 whiplash
- Expanded go metaphor explanation for Western audience unfamiliar with wéiqí
- Compressed Beat 4 from ~350 to ~250 words — each belief update tightened to two sentences
- Minor pacing trims in Beat 5

**Why:**
- The China expansion was the most important change. Without 卡脖子 and the ballpoint pen story, Western audiences can't understand *why* China's response is so intense. The story reads as purely strategic, which misses half the motivation. The emotional register matters — this isn't just industrial policy, it's national humiliation channeled into technological sovereignty.
- The fab explanation was about generosity, not dumbing down. If a viewer doesn't know what a fab is, the entire opening is meaningless numbers. Inviting them in early means the paradox actually lands.
- Beat 4 compression was a pacing call — it was restating conclusions the evidence already supported.

**Lesson:** When covering a bilateral conflict, underdeveloping one side doesn't create balance — it creates a lecture about one side with footnotes about the other. True balance means showing each side's internal logic on its own terms.

## v2 → v3 (pending)

**Planned changes based on critique session (April 25, 2026):**

Key diagnosis: the script is organized by *logic* (setup → history → present → analysis → conclusion) when it should be organized by *tension*. Every beat should tighten a question, not deliver information. Specific issues identified:

1. **Beat 2 feels like a textbook.** COCOM is analytically important but dramatically inert. The "Parallel A / Parallel B / comparison" structure is essay layout, not storytelling. Need to find the narrative pulse — possibly through a specific human moment or by interleaving the parallels with present-day rather than presenting them sequentially.

2. **Too many signpost phrases.** "Here's the problem." "Here's the critical difference." "Think about what that means." These are professor moves that signal "I'm about to teach you." Need to trust the material and the audience more.

3. **Beat 3 is overloaded.** ~12 topics in 6 minutes means the best moments (ballpoint pen, DeepSeek whiplash, Kirin fake-out) can't breathe. Need to cut or move material so the strongest beats have silence around them.

4. **Almost no human characters.** One human moment (Jensen/Trump negotiation) in 18 minutes. Need to find 2-3 more: TSMC engineers in Arizona? SMIC team doing 34 lithography passes? A specific person embodying the 卡脖子 generation?

5. **The ending retreats into abstraction.** "Nobody knows how this ends" is generic. The brief had stronger, more personal closing material (every car has 3,000 chips, HarmonyOS/Android bifurcation, COVID shortage as preview) that was mostly dropped. The ending should make the viewer feel *personally implicated*, not philosophically contemplative.

6. **Pacing is back-loaded.** The most interesting ideas arrive in the final third. The first 7 minutes are mostly setup. On YouTube, you have 3-4 minutes to earn a viewer's commitment.

**Core lesson (to be validated in v3):** Organizing by "things you need to know" is lecture structure. Organizing by "questions that keep tightening" is story structure.

## v2 → v3 (April 25, 2026)

**What changed — structure:**
- Reorganized the entire script from logic-order to tension-order. v2 was: setup → history → present → analysis → conclusion. v3 is: paradox → denial logic (history interleaved with present) → China's experience → the trap → personal stakes. Every beat now tightens a question rather than delivering a block of information.
- History is no longer siloed. The oil embargo flows directly into the present-day chip control escalation — same logic, same mistake, 80 years apart. COCOM compressed from a full parallel section to a ~4-sentence counterpoint. This cut ~250 words and kept the analytical value without the dramatic dead zone.
- Old Beat 4 ("What We Thought vs. What Happened") dissolved entirely. Its best insights — Nvidia's $5.5B write-down, Huawei's profit surge, "controls constrained scale but not capability" — were redistributed into Beats 2 and 3 where they serve as evidence within the narrative, not as a standalone analytical section. The Bayesian framework is still there; it just happens invisibly now instead of being presented as a labeled exercise.
- New Beat 4 ("The Trap") created from pieces that were scattered across v2: chess vs. go metaphor (moved from Beat 3 opening), supply chain complexity (moved from Beat 5 opening), countries caught in between (moved from Beat 3 ending), Miller's bifurcation prediction (moved from Beat 4 closing). Bringing these together creates the structural argument that *both sides are trapped* — which is the thesis of the episode, and in v2 it was never stated that cleanly.

**What changed — texture:**
- Removed all signpost phrases: "Here's the problem," "Here's the critical difference," "Think about what that means," "Here's what we know so far," "Let's be honest about what the experts got wrong." Counted 6+ in v2. Zero in v3.
- Added three human moments: (1) Taiwanese engineers in temporary desert housing in Arizona — makes "reshoring" tangible; (2) SMIC engineers running 34 lithography passes, with typewriter metaphor — makes the brute-force achievement feelable; (3) Morris Chang declaring "globalization is almost dead" — the man who built the system saying the system is breaking.
- Beat 3 thinned from ~12 topics to 3 stories told with breathing room: SMIC's 34-pass method, the Kirin relabeling, the DeepSeek whiplash. Cut: Big Fund corruption details, Made in China 2025 percentages, ASML/Korea/Japan (moved to Beat 4). The strongest moments now have silence around them.
- Beat 5 completely rewritten. Replaced abstract philosophical ending ("Nobody knows how this ends. And that uncertainty... is the real story.") with concrete personal stakes: your car's 3,000 chips, COVID shortage as preview, app incompatibility across bifurcated ecosystems. Final line is direct address to the viewer.

**What we cut and why:**
- Big Fund corruption (CEO arrested, executives jailed): interesting but not narrative. It's a policy detail that interrupts the emotional arc of Beat 3. Could return in a dedicated "China's semiconductor ecosystem" episode.
- Made in China 2025 percentages (70% target, 23-33% achieved): same issue. Accurate, important for a policy audience, but the Kirin relabeling tells the same story (ambition vs. reality) in a way that's actually dramatic.
- The entire "Parallel A / Parallel B" framing: the labels themselves were part of the problem. Labeling your structure is essay writing. Story structure is felt, not labeled.

**Lessons:**
- **Dissolving a beat is more powerful than compressing it.** v2 tried to fix Beat 4 by making it shorter. v3 dissolved it entirely and distributed its insights. The information all survived — it just works harder now because it appears as evidence inside a story rather than as conclusions in a summary.
- **Human moments don't have to be long.** The Taiwanese engineers in Arizona housing is one sentence. Morris Chang's quote is two. But they anchor abstractions to reality in a way that data can't.
- **Signpost phrases are a symptom, not the disease.** The real problem was that the script needed them — the structure was so information-dense that without signposts, the viewer would get lost. Fixing the structure made the signposts unnecessary.
- **"Your chips" > "the real story."** Ending on what the viewer personally has at stake is more powerful than ending on an epistemological observation about uncertainty. The uncertainty is still there — it just lives inside the concrete stakes rather than being stated as a thesis.
