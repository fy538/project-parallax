## Cross-Persona Analysis

**Best served:** Sofia (Framework Thinker) and Marcus (Algorithm Discovery). Sofia gets the transferable mental model she craves — "The Wrong Game" is a diagnostic framework she can immediately apply across domains. Marcus gets the surprising revelation (failed experiment, conquered the world) and the visual storytelling that hooks casual viewers. These two personas together represent the channel's ideal growth audience: smart generalists who want *thinking tools*, not *news*.

**Underserved:** Wei (Chinese Diaspora Viewer). The episode is a critique of Western strategic culture told entirely through Western intellectual history — RAND, Nash, Schelling, Ostrom, Rousseau, Skyrms, Reagan/Gorbachev. The only non-Western content is Ostrom's Japanese commons example (one phrase). China appears solely as a counterpart to the US in the closing beat. For a channel created by a Chinese-American whose differentiator is cross-cultural fluency (NAR-16), this episode doesn't deploy that asset at all. Wei would bounce around minute 7 or watch to the end but not share.

**Tension map:**

| Tension | Personas in conflict | Current script resolution |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Western-only intellectual history vs. cross-cultural depth | Wei needs non-Western strategic thought; episode is 100% Western | Resolved entirely in Wei's disfavor |
| Technical precision vs. accessibility | Priya/Sofia want formal precision (Skyrms proof, Folk Theorem); Marcus needs it simple | Resolved well — Skyrms is simplified to one sentence without losing rigor |
| Speed of thesis delivery vs. evidence building | Marcus wants the thesis fast; Priya wants it earned | Resolved well — thesis is felt in Beat 1, crystallized in Beat 3, proven in Beat 4 |
| Concreteness vs. abstraction | Marcus/James need tangible examples; Sofia works in abstractions | Resolved well — Ostrom's specific cases ground the abstract argument |

**The trade-off this script is making:** It optimizes for *framework transferability* (Sofia) and *surprise-driven casual engagement* (Marcus) at the cost of *cross-cultural perspective* (Wei). This is a defensible trade-off for a launch episode that needs to establish the channel's analytical method — but it underuses Tiger's strongest differentiator (the Translator identity direction). The channel cannot afford to underserve Wei consistently across multiple episodes.

## Learning for Next Episode

**Hypothesis 1: A single non-Western strategic concept would dramatically increase Wei's resonance without losing other personas.** The script already gestures at the idea that "which game is canonical is a political choice" — one sentence connecting this to Chinese strategic philosophy (e.g., 势/shi as inherently relational vs. PD's transactional framing) would serve Wei, fascinate Sofia (a new framework!), and intrigue Marcus (a Chinese concept he's never heard of) without alienating anyone. Test in next episode: introduce one Chinese-language term with emotional context, placed mid-episode, and check whether all personas respond.

**Hypothesis 2: The "illustrate-then-name" pattern for named concepts works across all personas.** The v2 revision moved "The Wrong Game" name to after the mechanism. This likely strengthens resonance for every persona — Marcus experiences the trap before getting a label; Sofia crystallizes the framework at the moment of maximum insight; Priya doesn't feel lectured to. This should become a channel-wide pattern for introducing named concepts.

**Hypothesis 3: The equilibrium-dot visual motif is the episode's strongest cross-persona asset.** Every persona's engagement spikes at the same moment: when the second dot appears in the stag hunt (Beat 4) and when all the dots gain companions (Beat 5). Simple geometric motifs that evolve across an episode may be a channel signature that works universally. Test in next episode: design another simple motif (a line that splits? a shape that multiplies?) and track whether the same cross-persona engagement spike occurs.
