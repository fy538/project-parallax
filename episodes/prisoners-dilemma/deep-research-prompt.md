I'm producing Parallax's launch episode: **"The Prisoner's Dilemma Is Wrong About Almost Everything"**

**Format:** Philosopher's Lens — the framework IS the primary subject; contemporary geopolitics
provides the case study that makes it urgent and searchable. This is not "game theory explains
geopolitics." It's "here's the most influential wrong idea in modern strategy — and a precise
account of what it actually gets right."

**Topic:** The Prisoner's Dilemma is the intellectual foundation of modern international relations
theory, nuclear deterrence doctrine, and trade conflict analysis. The problem: the classic framing
assumes a one-shot game, but almost no real geopolitical situation is one-shot. Once iteration,
reputation, and the "shadow of the future" enter, the optimal strategy flips completely — and the
PD's predictions invert. The most important insights come not from the classical dilemma but from
its precisely identified failure modes.

**Preliminary thesis (pressure-test this aggressively):**
The Prisoner's Dilemma correctly diagnoses why cooperation is structurally difficult. It fails to
predict when cooperation emerges anyway — because it strips out exactly the features that make real
geopolitical situations tractable: repeated interaction, reputation, communication, and the long
shadow of the future. The PD is simultaneously the most useful and most misapplied framework in
modern strategic thinking. Its value is not the equilibrium it predicts — it's the conditions it
identifies under which that equilibrium breaks down.

**Historical parallels and cases to investigate:**

1. **Axelrod's tournament (1980–1984)** — What did the computational evidence actually show about
   iterated PD? What is commonly misunderstood about why tit-for-tat won? What are tit-for-tat's
   failure modes (noise, miscalculation, vindictive opponents)? What did Axelrod's second tournament
   add that the first missed?

2. **Cuban Missile Crisis** — Did Kennedy and Khrushchev reason explicitly about iteration and
   shadow of the future? Was Khrushchev's back-channel communication via TASS a signal that the
   game was iterated? What would have happened if either side had treated it as a one-shot game
   (some advisors clearly did — who, and what did they recommend)?

3. **US-China trade war (2018–present)** — Is this better modeled as a one-shot PD (mutual
   defection equilibrium), an iterated PD (tit-for-tat dynamics), a coordination game (multiple
   equilibria with different focal points), or a game of incomplete information? The answer has
   different predictions for whether de-escalation is achievable.

4. **Medieval merchant guild networks** — Milgrom, North, and Weingast's work on Maghribi and
   Genoese traders. How did reputation systems and private-order enforcement solve the iterated PD
   before formal institutions existed? This may be the most powerful historical parallel — a
   civilization discovering Axelrod's solution empirically, 700 years early.

5. **Nuclear deterrence as iterated PD** — Why did MAD work, and why does it continue to work in
   a multipolar world with more players? The "stability-instability paradox" — nuclear weapons
   make all-out war less likely but limited conflicts more likely — is a second-order consequence
   of the iterated PD logic.

6. **Climate negotiations (Paris Agreement)** — Is climate a tragedy of the commons (PD structure)
   or a coordination game with multiple equilibria? The difference matters enormously for what
   interventions work. Side payments and monitoring mechanisms are the solution paths — which maps
   onto iterated PD theory how?

7. **NATO burden-sharing** — Is this actually a PD, or a different game structure? The free-rider
   problem looks like PD but may be a coordination game or assurance game. Getting the structure
   right changes the policy prescription completely.

**Philosophical frameworks to investigate:**

1. **Classical game theory** — Nash equilibrium, dominant strategies, the tragedy of the commons.
   Schelling's focal points. Harsanyi's incomplete information games. Where exactly does the PD
   model break down when applied to real strategic interactions?

2. **Evolutionary game theory** — Axelrod and Hamilton's evolution of cooperation. Nowak's five
   mechanisms for cooperation (kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network
   reciprocity, group selection). Which mechanisms actually operate at the nation-state level?

3. **Confucian reciprocity (禮/lǐ)** — As a cultural technology for solving the coordination
   problem that game theory discovers mathematically. Confucian ethics is built around reciprocal
   obligation, face, and the long time horizon of relationship. Does this constitute an independent
   solution to the iterated PD? Is there scholarly work directly connecting Confucian philosophy
   and game theory (beyond surface analogies)?

4. **Legalist (法家) strategic thinking** — Han Feizi's self-interest analysis. How does Legalist
   thought model the cooperation problem differently from Western game theory? Does it anticipate
   the one-shot / iterated distinction?

5. **Bayesian epistemology in incomplete information games** — How does updating on an opponent's
   behavior across repeated interactions change the equilibrium? This is the theoretical foundation
   for why iterated PD produces different outcomes than one-shot PD.

6. **Moral philosophy vs. game theory** — Kant's categorical imperative as a decision rule in PD
   contexts. Does "act only according to maxims you could universalize" produce the cooperative
   outcome? Where does deontological ethics meet mechanism design?

**Specific research questions:**

1. What exactly is the Prisoner's Dilemma getting wrong? Be precise about the failure modes.
   Is it primarily: the one-shot assumption? The rational actor assumption? The symmetric payoffs
   assumption? The no-communication assumption? The complete information assumption? Each failure
   mode has a different fix and different implications for geopolitics.

2. What does PD correctly model — where is it genuinely the right framework? Cases where treating
   the situation as a one-shot game is actually accurate. (Some arms races? Crisis bargaining where
   parties will never meet again? Specific negotiation scenarios?)

3. How did PD enter international relations theory? Schelling's *The Strategy of Conflict*, Jervis's
   *Cooperation Under the Anarchy* — what were the intellectual claims made for PD? Which have held
   up empirically and which have failed? Is there a revisionist IR literature that has pushed back?

4. What is the "shadow of the future" precisely, and when is it long enough to change the
   equilibrium? Is there empirical work on how long the shadow needs to be? Does it vary by
   regime type, by issue area, by power asymmetry?

5. Has Axelrod's tit-for-tat result replicated? Later work by Nowak and May (1992) found that
   spatial structure matters enormously and tit-for-tat doesn't always win. What is the current
   state of the evolutionary game theory evidence on cooperation?

6. Chinese intellectual tradition and the cooperation problem: is there pre-modern Chinese
   scholarship or institutional practice that explicitly grapples with the iterated PD structure

7. What does prediction market pricing tell us about the current US-China strategic relationship?
