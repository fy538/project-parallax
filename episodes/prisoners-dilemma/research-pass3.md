# PART A — DEEP VERIFICATION OF THE TOP THREE CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTIONS

## CONNECTION 1: The Flood-Dresher Experiment (1950) as the PD's Own First Refutation

### Primary Sources

The original primary document is Merrill M. Flood, *Some Experimental Games*, RAND Research Memorandum RM-789-1 (Santa Monica: RAND Corporation, June 1952; revised reissue). RAND has digitized the memo at rand.org/pubs/research_memoranda/RM789-1.html (the scanned PDF lacks machine-readable text, but the document exists in the RAND archive). The published-journal version is Merrill M. Flood, "Some Experimental Games," *Management Science* 5, no. 1 (October 1958): 5–26, which contains the canonical reproduction of the 100-round Alchian–Williams session and the Nash commentary.

The most reliable secondary account is Alvin E. Roth, "On the Early History of Experimental Economics," *Journal of the History of Economic Thought* 15, no. 2 (Fall 1993): 184–209, especially §II on the Dresher–Flood experiment. Roth quotes the Nash commentary directly from Flood (1958, p. 16):

> "Dr. Nash makes the following comment (private communication) on this experiment: 'The flaw in this experiment as a test of equilibrium point theory is that the experiment really amounts to having the players play one large multimove game. There is too much interaction…' "

Roth (1993, p. 17 of online manuscript) summarizes: "Dresher and Flood interpreted this as evidence against the general hypothesis that players tend to choose Nash equilibrium strategies, and in favor of the hypothesis that a cooperative 'split the difference' principle would be more powerful in organizing the data from games of this kind."

A second Nash remark, more often cited as illustrating his irritation, is paraphrased by S. M. Amadae, *Prisoners of Reason: Game Theory and Neoliberal Political Economy* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2016), citing Poundstone and Nasar: Nash's note about Alchian and Williams included the line "I would have thought them more rational." William Poundstone, *Prisoner's Dilemma: John von Neumann, Game Theory, and the Puzzle of the Bomb* (New York: Doubleday, 1992), 106–116, narrates the same episode. Sylvia Nasar, *A Beautiful Mind* (New York: Simon & Schuster, 1998), Chapter 13, treats Nash's RAND period but does not add detail beyond Poundstone on this specific exchange.

For broader context: Philip Mirowski, *Machine Dreams: Economics Becomes a Cyborg Science* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002), pp. 327–354, places the experiment within RAND's institutional culture and argues that Nash's equilibrium displaced von Neumann's cooperative-game preferences not on empirical grounds but because it served Cold War strategic gaming. S. M. Amadae, *Rationalizing Capitalist Democracy* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2003), and *Prisoners of Reason* (2016), Chapter 2, argue the same point with sharper attention to ideology.

### Mechanism Verification

The structural mechanism is: a model is published, an empirical test is run, the test contradicts the model, and the founder treats the contradicting data as a problem with the experiment rather than the model. Map of actors and incentives:

| | Abstract model (PD) | Founding experiment (1950) |
|---|---|---|
| Actors | 2 rational utility-maximizers, no communication, no future | 2 sophisticated subjects (Alchian, Williams), repeated interaction, observable history |
| Predicted outcome | Mutual defection (DD) every round | Mostly mutual cooperation (CC) with end-game defection |
| Observed outcome | n/a | CC = 60 rounds; DD = 14 rounds; mixed = 26 rounds |
| Founder's response | n/a | Nash: experiment "amounts to having the players play one large multimove game" |

The causal channel that makes the parallel non-trivial is the **interpretive move**: Nash's response was technically defensible (he was articulating, informally, what would later be formalized as the Folk Theorem by Aumann 1959) — but the institutional consequence was that the deviation was filed as "noise about repeated games" rather than as "evidence that cooperation needs explanation, not assumption." For 75 years this template recurred: the model gets defended by ring-fencing the conditions under which it would apply (one-shot, anonymous, finite, common-knowledge-of-rationality), even as those conditions almost never obtain in the cases the model is invoked to explain.

### Break-Point Analysis

Three structural break-points where the analogy is weaker than it first appears:

1. **Nash's objection was substantively correct, not merely defensive.** The Folk Theorem (Aumann, "Acceptable Points in General Cooperative n-Person Games," in *Contributions to the Theory of Games IV*, 1959) demonstrates rigorously that an iterated PD with sufficient probability of continuation supports cooperative equilibria. Flood and Dresher knew their game wasn't a "pure" one-shot PD. So this is not a clean case of theory being shielded from refutation; it is closer to a case of theory being clarified about its scope. The episode should not present Nash as merely stonewalling.

2. **Flood and Dresher themselves did not treat the model as falsified.** Roth (1993) reports they read the data as showing a "split-the-difference" principle was operating *alongside* equilibrium reasoning. Both founders explicitly hoped someone would resolve the puzzle (Poundstone 1992, p. 116 paraphrased in Roth and the *Conversable Economist* summary). The narrative "founder defends model, founder's response becomes template" is too strong if it implies all parties were closing ranks; the picture is more that the field absorbed the result selectively.

3. **The PD is a useful pedagogical object precisely because the one-shot version isolates the conflict.** That isolation is the point of the abstraction. Treating the one-shot prediction as "wrong" because real subjects in repeated play cooperate misreads what the model is doing. The script's stronger claim — that the PD's *influence* has been ideological rather than analytical — is defensible; the weaker claim that the model was "empirically refuted in 1950" is not, and should be qualified.

### Scholarly Precedent

Yes, extensive. Mirowski (2002, ch. 7) is the canonical historicist treatment. Amadae (2003, 2016) develops the ideological-influence thesis at book length. Paul Erickson et al., *How Reason Almost Lost Its Mind: The Strange Career of Cold War Rationality* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2013), pp. 141–193, treats the same period and concludes that Cold War game theory was less a neutral analytical framework than a "style of reasoning" with institutional sponsors. The Parallax framing — that the founding experiment instantiated an interpretive habit — is squarely within this scholarly tradition; it does not need to claim novelty here. What the episode can add is condensation and dramatization for a public audience, without overclaiming originality on the historical thesis.

### Toxin-Line Check

The legitimate analytical move is: *the experiment-then-defense pattern is structurally common in early formalist science, and the PD is an unusually consequential case of it*. The overclaim move would be: *Nash personally suppressed evidence* or *RAND knew the model was wrong and pushed it for ideological reasons*. The latter is not supported by the documentary record and would push into conspiracy framing.

Recommended phrasings:
- "When shown that Alchian and Williams cooperated, Nash's reply — that the experiment 'amounts to having the players play one large multimove game' — was technically defensible. It was also, in retrospect, the template for how the field would handle every later cooperation finding: ring-fence the model's domain rather than revise it."
- "Flood and Dresher themselves expected the puzzle to get resolved. The discipline absorbed it as a clarification, not a refutation."
- Avoid: "Nash hid the data" / "RAND buried the experiment" / "the founders knew the model was empirically broken."

## CONNECTION 2: Ostrom's Commons Governance vs. Hardin's Tragedy as Contest of Metaphors

### Primary Sources

The two anchor texts:

- Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons," *Science* 162, no. 3859 (13 December 1968): 1243–1248. Hardin (p. 1244) explicitly frames common-pool resources as multi-player PDs in which individual rationality dictates overuse.
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1990). The eight design principles are laid out in Chapter 3, pp. 88–102. Ostrom (p. 7) quotes Hardin and explicitly adopts the PD framing as the formalization she will challenge.

Case-study primary sources:
- Spanish *huertas*: Ostrom (1990), Ch. 3, pp. 69–82 (Valencia, Murcia, Orihuela, Alicante), drawing on Thomas Glick, *Irrigation and Society in Medieval Valencia* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard, 1970).
- Swiss alpine commons (Törbel): Ostrom (1990), pp. 61–65, drawing on Robert McC. Netting, *Balancing on an Alp* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1981).
- Japanese *iriaichi*: Ostrom (1990), pp. 65–69, drawing on Margaret McKean's fieldwork.
- Maine lobster gangs: Ostrom (1990), pp. 173–178; James M. Acheson, *The Lobster Gangs of Maine* (Hanover, NH: University Press of New England, 1988).

The Cox-Arnold-Villamayor-Tomás review is real and stronger than a "literature review." Michael Cox, Gwen Arnold, and Sergio Villamayor-Tomás, "A Review of Design Principles for Community-based Natural Resource Management," *Ecology and Society* 15, no. 4 (2010): art. 38. They evaluate **91 studies**, code presence/absence of each principle and case success, and conclude (Abstract): "the principles are well supported empirically." They also propose a refinement, splitting some principles into sub-components.

The 2009 Nobel: Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, *Scientific Background on the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel 2009: Economic Governance*, 12 October 2009 (nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/advanced-economicsciences2009.pdf). The background document explicitly credits Ostrom's empirical case-study program with overturning the Hardin formalization.

Critical literature:
- Mark Pennington, "Elinor Ostrom and the Robust Political Economy of Common-Pool Resources," *Journal of Institutional Economics* 9, no. 4 (2013): 449–468 — argues principles work locally but scaling is structurally limited.
- Eduardo Araral, "Ostrom, Hardin and the Commons: A Critical Appreciation and a Revisionist View," *Environmental Science & Policy* 36 (2014): 11–23 — argues Hardin's pessimism is justified for large-scale, regional, and global commons; Ostrom's empirical sample is biased toward small, locally governed cases.
- Walter Block and Peter T. Lewin (sometimes cited as "Block & Jankovic"), "Tragedy of the Partnership: A Critique of Elinor Ostrom" — argues Ostrom did not actually refute Hardin's formal claim but proposed a third option (collective property) that is not the same as "open-access commons."

### Mechanism Verification

Mapping:

| | Hardin's PD-framed commons | Ostrom's empirical commons |
|---|---|---|
| Players | n anonymous rational maximizers | Identified, mutually known users |
| Communication | None (assumed) | Continuous, face-to-face, often inherited |
| Time horizon | One-shot or memoryless | Multi-generational, intergenerational sanctions |
| Boundaries | Open access (assumed) | Defined membership, exclusion enforced |
| Monitoring | None (assumed) | Mutual + appointed monitors, often the users themselves |
| Sanctions | None (assumed) | Graduated, locally calibrated |
| Predicted outcome | Inevitable depletion | Long-run sustainable use (centuries in many cases) |

The non-trivial causal channel: **the PD model gets its predictive force by abstracting away exactly the institutional features that empirically determine the outcome**. This is not a small modeling choice — it is the modeling choice that determines the answer. So the "contest of metaphors" framing is precise: Hardin's predictions follow from the model; Ostrom's findings follow from the institutional features the model excludes. Whether either is "right" depends on whether the modeled abstractions match the case being analyzed.

### Break-Point Analysis

1. **Scale.** Pennington (2013) and Araral (2014) both make the empirically grounded point that Ostrom's design principles are validated mainly in small-to-medium-scale commons (irrigation systems, alpine pastures, fisheries with hundreds-to-thousands of users). For genuinely global commons — atmospheric carbon, ocean fisheries beyond exclusive economic zones, deep-sea mining — boundary-definition, monitoring, and graduated-sanctions principles are far harder to operationalize. Hardin's pessimism is more defensible at planetary scale than Ostrom's optimism. The episode should not let Ostrom carry the climate case unaided.

2. **Selection bias.** Ostrom's 800+ cases were selected partly because they survived; failed commons that collapsed before being studied are under-represented. Successful case studies show what *can* work; they are weaker evidence about what *will* work. Cox et al. (2010) partly addresses this with 91 cases coded for both success and failure, but the underlying corpus inherits Ostrom's selection.

3. **Distinct ontological claim.** Block and Jankovic argue Ostrom's commons are not Hardin's commons: Hardin's "commons" is open-access (no exclusion), while Ostrom's are common property with defined membership. If true, Ostrom is not refuting Hardin — she is documenting something Hardin would call a different category. The episode should acknowledge this terminological move rather than treat the refutation as clean.

### Scholarly Precedent

The framing of Ostrom-vs-Hardin as a refutation of *rational-choice institutionalism* is canonical: see Ostrom's own Nobel lecture, "Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems," *American Economic Review* 100, no. 3 (June 2010): 641–672. The narrower framing — Ostrom as specifically a refutation of *PD-based reasoning* — is less developed in the literature but is implicit in many treatments. Amadae (2016) treats both as part of the same critique. Erickson et al. (2013, ch. 5) gestures at this. The Parallax episode's framing is therefore an emphasis or condensation of an existing literature, not a novel synthesis. Calling it a "contest of metaphors" is a rhetorical contribution, not an analytical one.

### Toxin-Line Check

Legitimate: *Hardin's framing predicted what the PD predicts; Ostrom's empirical program documented something different; the difference is informative about when PD-style reasoning earns its keep*. Overclaim: *Hardin was wrong* (he wasn't, in the cases his model fits — see overgrazing on truly open-access pasture or post-collapse fisheries) or *Ostrom proved markets and states are unnecessary* (she didn't; she argued for polycentric governance, including state and market roles).

Recommended phrasings:
- "Where Hardin's model said the commons must collapse, Ostrom found communities that had governed theirs successfully for centuries — and the success depended on exactly the institutional features the model abstracts away."
- "The Ostromian critique works best at the scale of villages and watersheds. At the scale of the atmosphere, Hardin's pessimism gets harder to dismiss. The interesting question is which scale your problem actually has."
- Avoid: "Ostrom proved the tragedy of the commons is a myth" / "Hardin was refuted." Both overclaim.

## CONNECTION 3: Skyrms's Stag Hunt as Suppressed Canonical Game

### Primary Sources

- Brian Skyrms, "The Stag Hunt," Presidential Address of the Pacific Division of the American Philosophical Association, March 2001; published in *Proceedings and Addresses of the American Philosophical Association* 75, no. 2 (November 2001): 31–41. Available open-access at sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/papers/StagHunt.pdf.
- Brian Skyrms, *The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2004), 149 pp.
- The Rousseau passage is Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *Discours sur l'origine et les fondements de l'inégalité parmi les hommes* (1755), Part II. Skyrms quotes it in the 2001 Presidential Address (p. 1) and again in the 2004 book (p. 1): "If it was a matter of hunting a deer, everyone well realized that he must remain faithful to his post; but if a hare happened to pass within reach of one of them, we cannot doubt that he would have gone off in pursuit of it without scruple…"

Skyrms's central claim, in his own words from the 2001 address (p. 4): "Compared to the Prisoner's Dilemma, the Stag Hunt has received relatively little discussion in contemporary social philosophy — although there are some notable exceptions. But I think that the Stag Hunt should be a focal point for social contract theory."

He also formally demonstrates (pp. 5–6) that an iterated PD with Trigger strategies is mathematically transformable into a Stag Hunt: "The Shadow of the Future has not solved the problem of cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma; it has transformed it into the problem of cooperation in the Stag Hunt." This is decisive: in many cases where PD framing is used, what is actually being modeled — once repetition is allowed — is a stag hunt.

Related work:
- Ken Binmore, *Natural Justice* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2005), Ch. 3, follows Skyrms in treating the stag hunt as the canonical social-contract game.
- Robert Aumann, "Acceptable Points in General Cooperative n-Person Games," in *Contributions to the Theory of Games IV*, ed. R. D. Luce and A. W. Tucker (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1959): 287–324 — the original Folk Theorem result that makes the PD-to-stag-hunt transformation rigorous.
- Cristina Bicchieri, *The Grammar of Society: The Nature and Dynamics of Social Norms* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006) — applies a stag-hunt-like analysis to norm formation.

On historical contingency: Mirowski (2002) and Erickson et al. (2013) document that game theorists at RAND in the 1950s were familiar with coordination games (Schelling's later work on focal points is downstream of this) but the institutional incentives — solving Cold War deterrence problems, scoring publications in *Management Science* and *Journal of Conflict Resolution* — favored the more dramatic PD framing.

### Mechanism Verification

The structural difference is decisive and well-formalized:

| | Prisoner's Dilemma | Stag Hunt |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff ranking | T > R > P > S (Temptation > Reward > Punishment > Sucker) | R > T ≥ P > S (Reward dominates Temptation) |
| Pure-strategy Nash equilibria | One: (Defect, Defect) | Two: (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare) |
| Cooperation status | Strictly dominated; "irrational" | Equilibrium; rational if you trust |
| Diagnostic | Defection-extraction problem | Coordination/assurance problem |
| Required intervention | Change payoffs (e.g., punishment) | Change beliefs about partners' choice |

The non-trivial causal channel: **which game you choose as your default metaphor determines what counts as the problem**. If cooperation is a PD, the question is "how do we suppress defection?" — answer space: enforcement, punishment, hierarchy, hegemony. If cooperation is a stag hunt, the question is "how do we coordinate on the better equilibrium?" — answer space: communication, signaling, trust-building, focal points, conventions. Skyrms's argument is that for most real-world cooperation problems involving repeated interaction with identifiable partners, the stag hunt is the better fit, and choosing the PD as default mis-describes the problem.

### Break-Point Analysis

1. **Some problems really are PDs, not stag hunts.** One-shot anonymous interactions with no future and no reputation effects — e.g., poaching by transient operators in unmanaged international waters — are genuinely closer to PD than stag hunt. Treating every cooperation problem as a coordination problem under-rates cases where the temptation payoff really does dominate. The episode should not present "PD bad, stag hunt good" as universal.

2. **The stag hunt has its own pessimism.** Skyrms's 2001 address acknowledges (pp. 8–10) that with replicator dynamics, populations often get stuck at the hare-hunting equilibrium and cannot easily escape. He writes: "From the standpoint of rational choice, for the Hare Hunters to decide to be Stag Hunters, each must change her beliefs about what the other will do. But rational choice based game theory as usually conceived has nothing to say about how or why such a change of mind might take place." A stag-hunt frame is not automatically optimistic; it transforms the problem rather than solving it.

3. **The PD's choice as canonical may not have been "suppression."** It became canonical partly because its predictions are sharper (single equilibrium, dominant strategy, no equilibrium-selection problem) and partly because Tucker's prison-sentences narrative framing was unusually memorable. Calling the choice "suppression" implies an active mechanism; the more defensible claim is that institutional incentives selected for the more striking model. The script should avoid framing this as ideological suppression.

### Scholarly Precedent

Strong. Skyrms (2001, 2004) is the locus classicus. Binmore (2005), Bicchieri (2006), and Peter Vanderschraaf, *Learning and Coordination: Inductive Deliberation, Equilibrium, and Convention* (London: Routledge, 2001) all argue versions of the position. In international relations, Robert Jervis's stress on the security dilemma as a coordination problem (rather than a defection problem) implicitly does the same work — see Jervis, "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma," *World Politics* 30, no. 2 (1978): 167–214, especially his distinction between deep and shallow security dilemmas. The Parallax framing of "PD-vs-stag-hunt as a contingent intellectual choice with massive downstream implications" is consistent with this literature; the rhetorical framing of "suppressed canonical game" is the episode's contribution and should be presented as a rhetorical sharpening, not a novel discovery.

### Toxin-Line Check

Legitimate: *the stag hunt is, for many of the cases the PD is invoked to explain, the better-fitting model; choosing the PD as default has consequences*. Overclaim: *the stag hunt was deliberately suppressed by Cold War institutions* (Mirowski's stronger version) or *PD-framing is itself an ideology* (Amadae's stronger version, which is defensible at book length but tendentious in a 30-second framing).

Recommended phrasings:
- "There was always another option. In 1755, Rousseau described a different game — the stag hunt — where cooperation isn't dominated; it's just one of two equilibria you have to coordinate on. The choice to make the prisoner's dilemma canonical was not inevitable, and the choice has consequences."
- "Skyrms shows that an iterated prisoner's dilemma is mathematically a stag hunt. So when game theorists invoke the PD to model long-run cooperation, they are often, formally, modeling the stag hunt — they just keep the dramatic name."
- Avoid: "The stag hunt was suppressed" / "Cold War game theorists hid Rousseau" — these are not supported by the documentary record and risk crossing the toxin line.

# PART B — VERIFICATION OF FIVE LOAD-BEARING FACTUAL CLAIMS

## (1) Grofman and Pool 2,000-articles claim — **VERIFIED, with citation correction**

The original citation is **Bernard Grofman and Jonathan Pool, "Bayesian Models for Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma Games," *General Systems* 20 (1975): 185–194**, *not* the 1977 *Journal of Mathematical Sociology* paper. Multiple sources (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on "Prisoner's Dilemma" by Steven Kuhn; Wikipedia "Prisoner's dilemma" article; multiple academic review articles) trace the "over 2,000 scholarly articles by 1975" estimate to this *General Systems* paper. The original phrasing is approximately: "The literature on the iterated prisoner's dilemma is enormous; we estimate the number of scholarly articles devoted to it at over two thousand."

The estimate was almost certainly an order-of-magnitude judgment, not a systematic bibliometric count. A 2018 bibliometric study (Bergmann et al., arxiv.org/pdf/1911.06128) using modern databases found 2,420 published articles on the Prisoner's Dilemma between 1951 and 2018 — which means Grofman and Pool's 1975 estimate of "over 2,000" was either wrong then or has remained roughly flat for fifty years (the latter being implausible). Read it as a rhetorical estimate, not a bibliometric measurement.

**Recommended phrasing:** "By 1975, Grofman and Pool estimated the literature on the iterated prisoner's dilemma at over two thousand articles — a figure best read as 'enormous' rather than as a rigorous count."

## (2) FCC spectrum-auction figures — **VERIFIED**

Three sub-claims:

- "By 2014 the FCC alone had raised over $120 billion via spectrum auctions": **Verified** by the official Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences popular background to the 2020 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (Milgrom and Wilson): "The FCC's auctions alone, using this format, have brought more than 120 billion dollars over twenty years (1994–2014)." (nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2020/popular-information/).

- "Globally, spectrum auctions using descendants of SMRA have generated $200+ billion": **Verified** by the same Nobel popular background: "globally, this mechanism has generated more than 200 billion dollars from spectrum sales." Britannica's biographical entry on Milgrom corroborates the $200 billion figure for spectrum sales worldwide by 2014. GSA 2024 industry reports indicate global spectrum auction totals reached an additional ~$140 billion in 2021 alone, so cumulative global revenue now substantially exceeds the $200 billion 2014 figure.

- "The 2016 broadcast incentive auction generated $19.8 billion in forward auction revenue": **Verified** by the FCC's own announcement (fcc.gov/about-fcc/fcc-initiatives/incentive-auctions): "The auction yielded $19.8 billion in revenue, including $10.05 billion for winning broadcast bidders and more than $7 billion to be deposited to the U.S. Treasury for deficit reduction." The FCC public-notice document "Incentive Auction by the Numbers" (DOC-344398A1.pdf) confirms forward auction gross revenues of $19.8 billion (second largest in FCC auction history). Note for precision: the *forward auction* generated $19.8 billion gross; *net revenues to Treasury after broadcaster payments and bidding credits* were closer to $7.3 billion.

All three sub-claims are usable as written.

## (3) Kennedy 1963 "25-30 nuclear states by 2000" — **PARTIALLY VERIFIED, with significant correction**

The primary source is **John F. Kennedy, News Conference 52, State Department Auditorium, Washington, D.C., March 21, 1963, 6:00 PM EST**, archived at jfklibrary.org/archives/other-resources/john-f-kennedy-press-conferences/news-conference-52. The full quote, in response to a question, is:

> "With all of the history of war, and the human race's history, unfortunately has been a good deal more war than peace, with nuclear weapons distributed all through the world, and available, and the strong reluctance of any people to accept defeat, I see the possibility in the **1970's** of the President of the United States having to face a world in which **15 or 20 or 25 nations** may have these weapons. I regard that as the greatest possible danger and hazard."

Kennedy also said earlier in the same press conference (paraphrased in subsequent press accounts and quoted in the Arms Control Association timeline): "[P]ersonally I am haunted by the feeling that by 1970, unless we are successful, there may be 10 nuclear powers instead of 4, and by 1975, 15 or 20."

**The date endpoint in the brief is wrong.** Kennedy referenced **the 1970s, not the year 2000**. He was speaking about a horizon of seven to twelve years from 1963, not thirty-seven years. He was also citing — though this was not public at the time — a February 1963 Department of Defense memorandum (referenced in the Arms Control Association timeline) assessing that eight specific countries (Canada, China, India, Israel, Italy, Japan, Sweden, West Germany) could have weapons capability within ten years. So the figure was Kennedy's own statement of his anxieties, partly informed by classified intelligence-community estimates.

**Recommended phrasing:** "In a March 1963 press conference, John F. Kennedy warned that he could 'see the possibility in the 1970s of the President of the United States having to face a world in which 15 or 20 or 25 nations may have these weapons.' Sixty years later, the count is nine."

## (4) Aklin & Mildenberger 2020 — **VERIFIED, with caveat about challenge literature**

The paper is real, the venue is correct, and the central claim is as represented:

- Michaël Aklin and Matto Mildenberger, "Prisoners of the Wrong Dilemma: Why Distributive Conflict, Not Collective Action, Characterizes the Politics of Climate Change," *Global Environmental Politics* 20, no. 4 (November 2020): 4–27. DOI: 10.1162/glep_a_00578. Confirmed via direct.mit.edu/glep/article/20/4/4/95068 (MIT Press), Project MUSE, SSRN, and Semantic Scholar.

The abstract states: "We review empirical support for this model and find that the evidence for its claims is weak relative to the theory's pervasive influence. We find, first, that the strongest collective action claims appear empirically unsubstantiated in many important climate politics cases. Second, collective action claims — whether in their strongest or in more nuanced versions — appear observationally equivalent to alternative theories focused on distributive conflict within countries."

The paper has been substantively challenged and engaged:
- Matthew Kennard and Keith Schnakenberg, "Comment: Global Climate Policy and Collective Action," *Global Environmental Politics* 23, no. 1 (2023): 133–144 — argues that standard collective-action models do not in fact imply the two empirical predictions (policy reciprocity; inaction in absence of treaties) that Aklin and Mildenberger test.
- Aklin and Mildenberger, "Reply: The Persistent Absence of Empirical Evidence for Free-Riding in Global Climate Politics," *Global Environmental Politics* 23, no. 1 (2023): 145–152 — they concede some points, refine their original claim, and maintain that free-riding is not the binding constraint on global climate politics.

The paper has also been featured in major outlets (*New York Times* Ezra Klein podcast, *New York Magazine*, *Bloomberg*, *Ars Technica*) and has been picked up in the IPCC AR6 WG3 chapter on international cooperation, which now uses "four different analytical frameworks" rather than the single global-commons frame (see ndc-aspects.eu commentary "No Longer Prisoners of the Wrong Dilemma?").

**Usable as written. Recommended that the script note the existence of the Kennard-Schnakenberg comment and the authors' refined response, to demonstrate the episode is engaging the live scholarly debate, not a settled position.**

## (5) Nash-Flood-Dresher exchange — **PARTIALLY VERIFIED, REPHRASING REQUIRED**

The characterization "Nash protested the experimental design when shown the cooperation data" is **substantively accurate but rhetorically too strong**. The primary documentation:

- Flood (1958, p. 16) reproduces Nash's commentary as a private communication: "The flaw in this experiment as a test of equilibrium point theory is that the experiment really amounts to having the players play one large multimove game. There is too much interaction…" Quoted directly in Roth (1993).
- The "I would have thought them more rational" line is reported by Amadae (2016) citing Nasar (1998) and Poundstone (1992); the original source is the same Flood notes from 1950 that became the 1952 RAND memo.

Two important nuances:

1. **Nash was technically correct.** The point he was making — that an iterated game is structurally different from a one-shot game — is the foundational insight that Aumann would formalize as the Folk Theorem in 1959. Nash was not stonewalling; he was making a real game-theoretic point that the field would later canonize.

2. **"Protested" overstates the affect.** The Nash note is brief, technical, and signed off with what reads more as exasperation at the sophisticated subjects' behavior ("I would have thought them more rational") than as defensive insistence on the model. The interpretive overlay — that this established a defensive template — is Amadae's and Mirowski's, and is defensible *as historiography*, but should not be presented as Nash's stated intent.

**Suggested more accurate phrasing:**

> "When shown the data, Nash sent the experimenters a brief note. The experiment, he wrote, was 'really one large multimove game' — a fair point that the field would later formalize. He also remarked that he 'would have thought them more rational.' Whatever Nash intended, the response set a template: when subjects don't behave as the model predicts, look first at the experiment, not at the model. That template would recur for seventy-five years."

This phrasing is defensible because (a) it quotes Nash accurately, (b) it acknowledges his point was technically valid, (c) it locates the "template" claim where it belongs — in the field's subsequent absorption of the result, not in Nash's intent.

# PART C — SECTION 9: SPECULATIVE IMPLICATIONS

*Forecasts, not predictions. The probability ranges below are heuristic estimates calibrated against base rates of major-conference outcomes, expressed as ranges to reflect genuine uncertainty.*

## (1) Three Named Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — The Schelling Lock (probability range: 35–50%)

US–China decision-makers continue using prisoner's-dilemma framing for strategic competition: AI-chip controls, Taiwan posture, semiconductor export licensing, and trade policy are all conducted as if the game is one in which any unilateral restraint is exploited. Track 1.5 dialogues continue but produce no shared strategic vocabulary. By 2028, the US–China relationship has the structural features Skyrms warned about: stuck at the risk-dominant hare-hunting equilibrium not because anyone prefers it but because no actor can credibly initiate the move to the payoff-dominant stag-hunting equilibrium. The "lock" is what the episode's analytical framework predicts: when self-confirming strategic framing dominates, the framing creates the strategic reality that justifies the framing.

**Structural rationale:** This is the direct prediction of the "self-confirming strategic framing" thesis. If the PD framing actively constructs the world it claims merely to describe, then continued PD framing should produce continued mutual hardening. The probability is high because (a) the institutional incentives in both Washington and Beijing reward framings that justify the policies already underway, and (b) base rates for dramatic mid-cycle reframings of major-power rivalries are low (the Reagan–Gorbachev shift is the rare exception, not the rule).

### Scenario 2 — The Ostromian Drift (probability range: 15–25%)

AI safety governance gradually drifts away from the "race-to-the-bottom" PD framing toward something resembling polycentric commons governance. Compute thresholds, model-evaluation protocols, and incident-disclosure norms emerge through overlapping institutions: the AI Safety Institutes (UK, US, Singapore, Japan, EU), industry bodies (Frontier Model Forum), the GPAI, and bilateral US–China technical exchanges. None of these produces a global treaty; together they produce an Ostromian patchwork with monitoring (third-party evaluations), graduated sanctions (compute restrictions for non-compliance), and nested decision-making (national + international). This works imperfectly but better than the PD framing predicted.

**Structural rationale:** AI labs, unlike anonymous PD players, are identifiable, repeated-game actors with reputational stakes, regulatory exposure, and substantial overlap in personnel. The empirical conditions for Ostromian governance (defined membership, monitoring feasibility, graduated sanctions) are partly present. The probability range is moderate-low because (a) compute and capability thresholds are still hard to operationalize at the international level, (b) US–China technical mistrust is a hard ceiling on monitoring, and (c) the most-discussed framings in 2024–2026 have been racing-coded rather than commons-coded.

### Scenario 3 — The Aklin Pivot (probability range: 20–35%)

Climate diplomacy quietly pivots away from the "global collective action problem" framing toward the distributive-conflict framing Aklin and Mildenberger argued for. COP31 (2026, Belém) and COP32 (2027, Africa region TBD) communiqués increasingly foreground intra-national distributive politics — fossil-fuel-sector workers, energy-poor regions, climate-finance recipients — rather than free-riding-by-other-countries. By COP33, the IPCC's pluralist analytical frame (already present in AR6 WG3) becomes the operational frame for negotiation. Climate-policy ambition rises in countries where domestic political coalitions support it and stagnates where they do not, with little reference to what other states are doing.

**Structural rationale:** This is what Aklin and Mildenberger's empirical findings predicted would happen: countries are already implementing climate policy on domestic-political grounds rather than reciprocity grounds; the framing will eventually catch up. The probability is moderate because (a) the framing change is partly already underway (the Paris Agreement's NDC structure already abandoned the Kyoto-style reciprocity framing), and (b) institutional momentum and a generation of trained climate-policy professionals work against rapid reframing.

## (2) Falsifiable Predictions (6–24 months)

**Prediction P1 (NPT RevCon outcome, by end of May 2026):**

> If the Eleventh NPT Review Conference (April 27 – May 22, 2026) **fails to produce a consensus final document for the third consecutive cycle** (after 2015 and 2022), the episode's thesis is *strengthened*: the PD framing of nonproliferation as a defection-prone collective-action problem is producing what Aklin and Mildenberger said the climate frame produces — empty rituals of reciprocity that obscure the actual distributive politics of nuclear capability.
>
> If the RevCon **does produce a consensus final document with substantive new commitments**, the thesis is *weakened*: PD-framed multilateral institutions can in fact reproduce themselves under stress.

This is a genuinely risky prediction. The base rate is unfavorable to consensus (3 of the last 5 RevCons failed), and observers including the Vienna Center for Disarmament and Non‑Proliferation, the European Leadership Network, and IDSA have publicly assessed the 2026 RevCon as heading toward failure. But Vietnamese leadership and a less-toxic-than-2022 atmosphere give it a non-trivial probability of partial success.

**Prediction P2 (US–China AI Track 1.5 outcome, by end of 2026):**

> If by December 2026 there is **no joint communiqué from any US–China AI safety dialogue (Track 1 or Track 1.5) using shared technical vocabulary about model evaluations, compute thresholds, or incident reporting**, the thesis is *strengthened*: PD framing has prevented the development of the shared vocabulary that the stag-hunt framing would predict.
>
> If by December 2026 such a joint communiqué exists — even from a Track 1.5 channel like the Carnegie Endowment / Tsinghua Center for International Strategy and Security dialogue, or the equivalent — the thesis is *weakened*: shared technical vocabulary is emerging despite PD-framed political rhetoric.

This is risky in both directions: the Biden–Xi 2024 commitment to bilateral AI dialogues created a real institutional channel, and modest technical-level communiqués are not impossible. But the post-2025 deterioration in US–China relations cuts the other way.

## (3) "What Would Change My Mind" Factors

**Factor 1 — Climate-finance-as-distributive-bargain succeeds at scale.** If at COP31 or COP32, the Loss and Damage Fund and Article 9 finance flows scale toward $300 billion+/year primarily because *domestic political coalitions in major emitters support it* (rather than because of reciprocity or treaty pressure), this is strong evidence for the Aklin–Mildenberger framing and against PD-style climate analysis. Conversely, if climate finance stalls precisely on free-riding-style arguments — "we won't pay until they pay" — the PD framing retains its predictive power and the episode's thesis weakens specifically for the climate case.

**Factor 2 — A major actor publicly reframes a PD-coded conflict as a stag hunt and the reframing changes outcomes.** If, for example, a US administration explicitly adopted a "coordination, not extraction" framing for AI safety dialogues with China, and that framing measurably changed the trajectory of negotiations — produced the kind of joint technical communiqué predicted in P2 above — that would be evidence that strategic framing has the causal force the episode claims. Conversely, if a leader attempted such a reframing and it had no effect on outcomes (the opponents simply continued defecting), that would be evidence that PD-framing is descriptive of actually-existing strategic reality rather than constructive of it. The Reagan–Gorbachev "common security" reframing is the historical proof-of-concept; the next test is live.
