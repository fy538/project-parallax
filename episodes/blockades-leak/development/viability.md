## The 5 Questions

### 1. Structural Resonance
**Pattern:** Technology-denial regimes structurally fail because they create a tragedy of the commons: participants have individual incentives to circumvent (higher profits, national security advantage, competitive positioning) while the costs of enforcement are diffused across all members. When controls restrict access to X, rational actors develop workarounds (smuggling, dummy corporations, espionage, indigenous development). These workarounds gradually degrade the control regime's effectiveness, forcing more draconian measures, which accelerates circumvention, until the regime becomes unenforceable and collapses. The problem isn't implementation — it's structural.

This pattern reframes the entire debate from "can we make export controls work better" to "why do all such regimes follow the same tragic arc."

### 2. Historical Parallel
**Named case:** COCOM (Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls), 1949-1994.

**Shared mechanism:** COCOM was established to deny the Soviet bloc access to Western computing and dual-use military technologies. For 45 years, Western nations enforced controls on semiconductors, computer equipment, and advanced manufacturing tools. By the 1980s, the Soviet Union systematically circumvented controls through KGB/GRU operations (thousands of "technology collection officers" deployed across Western Europe and North America). Dummy corporations were established. Smuggling networks proliferated. A British parliamentary review labeled the incidents a "horror story of illegal exports." COCOM's effectiveness degraded year by year — participants had individual incentives to cheat (sell to intermediaries, turn a blind eye, compete with each other). Enforcement costs were high; benefits were dispersed. The regime collapsed quietly in 1994 with the Wassenaar Arrangement replacing it (tacitly acknowledging that strict controls on the scale COCOM attempted were unworkable).

**Structural parallel to modern chip controls:** The US/Japan/South Korea consortium restricting semiconductor and advanced computing exports to China follows identical logic. Participants have incentives to cheat (TSMC's Taiwan operations, indirect routes through intermediaries, competitors undercutting restrictions). Enforcement costs are high. The regime is showing signs of stress (China's indigenous advances despite controls, reports of failed enforcement, the White House itself admitting in December 2025 that the strategy "may be failing"). The structural arc is the same: initial confidence → slow degradation → eventual admission of failure.

**Verification:** COCOM is well-documented in academic literature (National Academies, RAND, CSIS, Jamestown Foundation). The tragedy of the commons logic is explicitly identified in recent analysis (e.g., Texas National Security Review "Hard Then, Harder Now: CoCom's Lessons and the Challenge of Crafting Effective Export Controls Against China," September 2025, notes that COCOM's lessons are being ignored and new controls may face the same structural problems).

### 3. Decoder Framing
**Standard frame:** "Are US/Western export controls on China's semiconductor industry working? Are they effective at slowing China's tech development?"

This is the frame of policy effectiveness. It asks an empirical question: do the controls achieve their stated goal? Media, think tanks, and policy analysis all orbit this question.

**Parallax frame:** "Why do all technology-denial regimes fail — and what does history reveal about the structural logic that makes them fail regardless of enforcement intensity?"

This reframes from "policy effectiveness" (an engineering problem: can we enforce better?) to "structural inevitability" (a tragedy of the commons problem: the structure itself is the issue). The viewer doesn't just learn whether the controls are working; they learn *why* they can't work, and why 80 years of trying different enforcement methods haven't changed the outcome.

**Gap assessment:** This is a significant reframe. The standard conversation assumes that tighter controls or better enforcement could solve the problem. The Parallax frame reveals that the problem is *structural*, not technical. This shifts the viewer's understanding of what's actually at stake — not "will the controls work," but "what does it mean that all attempts to build technology denial regimes fail in the same way."

### 5. Angle Gap

**Existing coverage:** YouTube hosts multiple videos on export control effectiveness ("Why US Chip Export Controls Are Failing," "White House Admits Export Strategy Failing," "China Rejects US AI Chips," etc.). Most treat the question as empirical: do they work yes/no, are they effective yes/no. Some note that controls have not achieved their full goal (China continues advancing in semiconductors). A few hint at structural problems (SemiAnalysis: "very short-sighted"; FPRI analysis: "limits of export controls"). One video ("Cold War Lessons for Export Controls Against China") mentions COCOM but does not connect it to commons theory or to the systemic inevitability of failure.

**Parallax angle:** Nobody connects COCOM's documented 45-year collapse to tragedy of the commons to modern semiconductor controls as a unified historical + philosophical pattern. No existing coverage makes the argument "this failure was *inevitable* because of how commons tragedies work, and we already learned this lesson in 1994." This is the angle gap.

The reframe is both historical (COCOM as a detailed precedent) and philosophical (commons theory as the explanatory structure). Existing coverage lacks one or the other; Parallax supplies both.
