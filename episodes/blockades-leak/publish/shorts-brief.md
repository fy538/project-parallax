### Short 1: The Blockade Paradox (45 Seconds)

**Series:** Framework in 45 Seconds  
**Template:** KineticShort — definition variant  
**Duration:** 45-50s  
**Source beat:** Beat 2 (The Framework), foundational explanation

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"When you make something scarcer, you make it more profitable to steal. This is why every attempt to restrict technology ends in leaks."

**Script (narration text, ~110 words):**

Imagine a shared pasture. Each herder can add one more goat. You get the profit. The cost of overgrazing? Shared across everyone. So everyone adds a goat. The pasture dies. That's the tragedy of the commons. Now apply it to technology denial. When countries restrict access to advanced chips through export controls, each nation faces a choice: cooperate and fall behind, or defect and gain advantage. Breaking ranks is profitable if enough of your shipments get through — and when restrictions tighten, that profit margin climbs. Tighter controls, higher prices for restricted goods, stronger incentive to smuggle. The enforcement mechanism creates the defection it's trying to prevent. This structural pattern isn't a policy failure — it's baked into the system.

  - Frame 4 (10s): Curves graphic — X-axis "control tightness," Y-axis "smuggling profit," curve rising sharply
  - Frame 5 (10s): Branding (amber accent, ∴ mark)
- Text callouts: "TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS," "THE BLOCKADE PARADOX," channel logo
- Accent color: amber (#E5A544)

**Concept registry:**

### Short 2: Venice's 250-Year Monopoly That Lasted Until Print (60 Seconds)

**Series:** History Rhymes  
**Template:** SplitShort (or KineticShort — quote variant)  
**Duration:** 55-60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 5 (Cross-Domain Parallels), Murano glass

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"Venice banned glassmakers from leaving on penalty of death. The monopoly lasted 250 years. Then one priest published a book."

**Script (narration text, ~145 words):**

For two and a half centuries, Venice owned global glassmaking. The secret: cristallo glass formulas — essentially the advanced chips of the Renaissance. The enforcement was brutal. Venetian law: don't let glassmakers leave without permission. Reveal the secrets? Death penalty. The physical island of Murano made surveillance easy — every artisan under watch, every boat monitored. And it worked. The monopoly held.

Then in 1612, a Franciscan priest named Antonio Neri published "L'Arte Vetraria" — "The Art of Glass" — revealing all the secrets in print.

That was the endpoint. Once knowledge spreads, no amount of enforcement recreates the monopoly. The structural parallel to modern chips is strong: restricted knowledge, incentive for defection, individual rational actors breaking ranks. Where it breaks: knowledge can't be unlearned, but chips are physical goods that can be intercepted. Same defection logic. Different enforcement stakes.

### Short 3: COCOM vs Modern Controls — The Speed Problem (60 Seconds)

**Series:** Both Sides Are Wrong  
**Template:** TimelineComparison or DataChartShort (dual timelines)  
**Duration:** 58-60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 3 (COCOM) + Beat 4 (Modern Controls)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"The Cold War export controls lasted 45 years. Modern chip controls are leaking faster. But not because we're doing it wrong — because the world is faster."

**Script (narration text, ~130 words):**

Cold War. COCOM — 17 Western nations coordinating export controls on technology to the Soviet Union. Result: the USSR stayed 5-10 years behind in computing. Controls worked, sort of. Catch: a scandal takes two years to surface (Toshiba-Kongsberg, 1983-1985). Punishment takes another two years to formalize. The enforcement was glacially slow because the technology was moving slowly.

Fast forward to 2022. Modern chip controls hit. October 2022: restrictions announced. By May 2024 — eighteen months later — at least $390 million in banned Nvidia chips had been smuggled into Asia. Not slower than COCOM. Faster. A lot faster.

Here's what both sides get wrong: hawks think better enforcement will hold the line. Doves think this is inevitable collapse. It's neither. The system isn't broken — the speed of the modern economy is just faster than the enforcement can track. The structure remains the same. The clock runs different.

**Visual spec:**
- TimelineComparison dual-track layout:
  - **TOP TRACK (COCOM):** Years 1949-1994
    - 1949: "17 nations coordinate"
    - 1983-1985: "Toshiba scandal: 2-year discovery lag"
    - 1987: "2 more years to formal recognition"
    - 1994: "Controls end"
  - **BOTTOM TRACK (Modern):** Years 2022-2026
    - Oct 2022: "Advanced chip restrictions announced"
    - May 2024: "$390M+ smuggled (18 months)"
    - Jan 2026: "Multiple networks prosecuted"
  - **VISUAL MOTIF:** A clock or speedometer showing speed differential (top slow, bottom fast)
- Color scheme: amber for timeline markers, rust for smuggling/violation indicators
- Final overlay: ∴ mark with text "Same structure. Different speed."

**Concept registry:**
Callback: `technology-denial`, `cocom`. Introduces: `enforcement-lag-vs-technology-speed` (a structural distinction explaining acceleration). Relevant to registry but not a standalone concept yet — notes it as a thematic pattern.

**Standalone test:** Viewer without knowledge of COCOM understands that export controls have existed and worked before, sees the speed differential as the novel problem, and recognizes that "enforcement is weak" misses the point (it's not about competence, it's about clock speed). ✓

### Short 4: The Defection Cascade — Why Controls Create Smuggling Networks (45 Seconds)

**Series:** What Happens Next?  
**Template:** KineticShort (statistic variant) or SplitShort  
**Duration:** 45-50s  
**Source beat:** Beat 4 (Modern Controls & The Defection Cascade)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"When one company smuggles banned chips and gets caught, the rest of the smuggling networks see the real enforcement risk. What happens next?"

**Script (narration text, ~105 words):**

Imagine you're a chip broker in Hong Kong. Restricted Nvidia GPUs cost $4,000 each on the open market. On the black market, with 30% confiscation risk but 70% expected profit margin, they're worth $8,000. The math: if seven of your ten shipments get through, you're profitable. Enter the network. One ring gets prosecuted (Luxuriate Your Life network, 2024). Three hundred ninety million in chips seized. The rest of the networks update their risk models. If enforcement caught one network with that resources commitment, what's the actual detection probability? And here's the perverse effect: as enforcement tightens, prices climb. Higher prices = higher profit margins. The incentive to defect accelerates. Each nation pulling tighter triggers the next actor to enter the market. That's the Defection Cascade. It's structural, not optional.

**Visual spec:**
- KineticShort progression:
  - Frame 1 (8s): Simple character icon (broker) with chip icon, price labels ($4K legal, $8K black market)
  - Frame 2 (12s): Network diagram — one node highlighted (caught), rest updating
  - Frame 3 (10s): Rising curve — "Enforcement Tightness" (X) vs "Defection Incentive" (Y)
  - Frame 4 (12s): Cascade animation — one actor → next actor → network → exponential
  - Frame 5 (8s): Branding
- Text overlays: "$4K vs $8K," "70% Expected Profit," "THE DEFECTION CASCADE"
- Accent colors: amber for price, rust for enforcement action

**Concept registry:**
Introduces: `the-defection-cascade` (named concept, core to EP02 thesis), `individual-rational-choice` (game theory). Both marked for registry addition.

**Standalone test:** First-time viewer without game theory background understands the profit incentive, the scaling problem (one network caught → others update risk models → more networks enter), and the perverse inversion (tighter controls = higher profits = more smuggling). The "cascade" concept is tangible (visible in prosecution waves). ✓
