### Short 1: Oil Embargo & Chip Controls: 80 Years of the Same Mistake
**Series:** History Rhymes  
**Template:** TimelineComparison (dual-timeline variant) or SplitShort  
**Duration:** 60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 2 — "The Logic of Denial" (3:00–4:15)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"In 1941, the US cut off Japan's oil. 134 days later: Pearl Harbor. Today, the US cut off China's chips. What happens in 134 days?"

**Script (narration text, ~135 words):**
In summer 1941, America froze Japan's assets and cut off 80 percent of their oil supply. The logic was clean: constrain military expansion without firing a shot. Japan's navy had 18 months of fuel reserves—not enough. The calculus was terrifying: use the fleet now, or watch it rust. One hundred thirty-four days later: Pearl Harbor. The resource denial meant to prevent war triggered the catastrophe it was designed to stop.

That was 1941. In 2022, America tried the exact same playbook. Not oil—the machines that make the machines that run the modern world. Export controls. Chip denial. Same logic. Same hope. Same risk.

Here's the question nobody wants to ask: what's different this time?

**Visual spec:**
- Split-screen timeline layout: left side dates and events from 1941 (asset freeze → oil embargo escalation → Pearl Harbor), right side 2022–2026 (Huawei ban → chip controls → ?)
- Dual markers converging at "134 days" for 1941, then "?" for 2026
- Amber accent for dates, rust for escalation/conflict moments
- Final frame: both timelines converging with a question mark overlay

**Concept registry:**
Introduces: `oil-embargo-1941` (cold-intro), `technology-denial` (structural pattern across both timelines)  
Callbacks: Any future episode in Arc 1 comparing historical constraints

**Standalone test:** Works perfectly standalone—the viewer learns the 1941 precedent and immediately grasps the stakes of the current moment without needing the full episode.

### Short 2: 卡脖子: Why Space-Faring China Can't Make a Pen
**Series:** Framework in 45 Seconds  
**Template:** KineticShort (quote variant with image reveals)  
**Duration:** 60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 3 — "The Other Side of the Wall" (7:30–8:15)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"China built a space program. They couldn't build a pen."

**Script (narration text, ~130 words):**
Until 2017, China could launch astronauts into orbit. They assembled the world's fastest supercomputer. They built 40,000 kilometers of bullet trains. They could not manufacture the steel ball at the tip of a ballpoint pen. Not couldn't. Had to import it from Japan.

When state media reported this, it became a symbol—a parable about being strangled by technology you can't control. In Chinese, they have a word for this: 卡脖子. Literally: "choked at the throat."

That's the parable. This is the reality: semiconductors are that story at civilizational scale. One country, one company, one machine—and your entire industry stops. Chips are what keeps you in the modern world. And if you can't make them, you're hostage to whoever can.

### Short 3: 34 vs 9: China's Typewriter Solution for Making Chips
**Series:** The Market Says  
**Template:** DataChartShort (side-by-side comparison variant)  
**Duration:** 60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 3 — "The Other Side of the Wall" (9:00–10:30)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"China makes 7-nanometer chips without the world's most advanced machines. Here's how: do everything 34 times instead of 9."

**Script (narration text, ~140 words):**
The West denied China EUV lithography—the advanced machines that make cutting-edge chips. So SMIC engineers found a workaround: run every wafer through 34 separate lithography passes instead of 9. Same result, different path. It's like writing a novel by punching out one letter at a time on a typewriter when everyone else has a word processor.

Every extra pass is another chance for a defect. Another cost multiplier. Another month of delay. More expensive. Slower. And it works.

Yields went from below 40 percent to 60, 70 percent by mid-2025. That's real progress—proof that constraints breed ingenuity. But there's a ceiling. When Huawei released the Kirin X90, claiming 5-nanometer capability, independent teardowns told a different story: 7-nanometer silicon with a new label.

The gap between what China claims and what China can build—that gap is the metric that matters.

**Visual spec:**
- 0-3s: KineticTypography: "34 LITHOGRAPHY PASSES"
- 3-6s: parallel bars animation showing 34 (rust) vs 9 (amber)
- 6-12s: side-by-side comparison: Western fab in 6s, Chinese fab (multi-pass) in 6s, with timeline durations
- 12-18s: yield improvement line chart (bottom left to 40%–70% range over time)
- 18-24s: Kirin X90 reality-check graphic: "Claimed: 5nm" vs "Actual: 7nm"
- Final frame: hold on "The gap is real" text

Introduces: `smic-multi-patterning` (cold-intro, "34 vs 9" — already registered)

### Short 4: DeepSeek Proved Everyone Wrong (And Also Proved Everyone Right)
**Series:** Both Sides Are Wrong  
**Template:** SplitShort or KineticShort (paradox variant)  
**Duration:** 60s  
**Source beat:** Beat 3 — "The Other Side of the Wall" (10:30–12:00)

**Hook (first 3 seconds):**
"DeepSeek's AI just proved that export controls don't work. Then it proved that they absolutely do."

**Script (narration text, ~145 words):**
In early 2025, a Chinese AI lab called DeepSeek released a model called R1 that competed with leading American systems—built on inferior hardware. In China, this was electric. Vindication. Proof that Chinese ingenuity could route around any restriction America imposed.

Then DeepSeek tried to train R2, their next model, on Huawei's domestic chips—the ones supposed to replace Nvidia. Zero successful training runs. Not one. They had to crawl back to the very Nvidia hardware the US was trying to restrict.

Both of those things are true at the same time. China can innovate brilliantly around constraints. And the constraints are real. The question is which truth has the longer half-life. Can China keep improvising forever? Or does constraint eventually hit a hard ceiling? Nobody knows. That uncertainty is the entire game.
